### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** 383: Psalm 137  
**Hosts:** Brent Billings, with guests Reed Dent and Elle Grover-Fricks  
**Focus:** Psalm 137 (lament, exile, memory, and imprecation)

In this episode Brent, Reed, and Elle work through Psalm 137, one of the rawest and most uncomfortable psalms in the Bible. It opens with exiles weeping "by the rivers of Babylon" and ends with a blessing on whoever dashes Babylon's infants against the rocks. They read it in Elle's own translation, which brings out Hebrew wordplay that English versions often lose. They describe the psalm as a "psalm of disorientation." It makes room for communal grief, honors the role of memory after loss, lets victims refuse to perform their pain for the people who hurt them, and gives words to hatred in prayer. They draw on Eugene Peterson, Walter Brueggemann, Miroslav Volf, and stories of trauma and forgiveness. Their conclusion is that praying our hate honestly is not a holy end point, but it may be a necessary first step toward real forgiveness.

### Key Takeaways

- Psalm 137 is a **psalm of disorientation**. Unlike Psalm 13, it never turns to "but I have trusted." It lets grief and rage stand without a tidy resolution.
- **Grief is communal.** The psalm says "*we* sat and *we* wept," which pushes back on cultures that keep grief private and hold it together in public.
- In Jewish practice the psalm is traditionally recited before the grace after meals on weekdays. That keeps lament in the community's regular rhythm instead of hiding it away.
- **Memory has many jobs after loss.** It can comfort, keep a person's identity intact, and remind us that the present horror is not the only reality.
- **Refusing to sing can be an act of dignity.** The captors' demand to "sing us a song of Zion" resembles abusers who want their victims to soothe or validate them. The exiles say no.
- **The psalmist names the people who did the harm, not God.** The psalm aims its outrage at the human perpetrators. That is one of several honest ways the Bible frames suffering.
- **Hate needs to be prayed, not suppressed** (Eugene Peterson). Feelings we deny can turn into the very evil that caused them.
- **Real forgiveness may require honest accusation first** (Miroslav Volf). Forgiving too easily can be a way of not taking the wrong seriously.
- **Radical forgiveness is Spirit-empowered and "insanely glorious,"** as in the stories from Rwanda. Expecting it as a simple duty cheapens it.
- **Ways forward include gratitude** for who we have become (not gratitude toward the perpetrator) and **curiosity** about the perpetrator, which can slowly turn anger into compassion.
- Justice belongs to God. The goal is to become people who, like Jesus, can say "Father, forgive them."

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. Psalm 137 as a Psalm of Disorientation

- Walter Brueggemann sorts the psalms into **orientation, disorientation, and reorientation**. Psalm 137 belongs firmly in disorientation: a community whose world has been overturned by calamity (the Babylonian exile).
- Reed contrasts it with **Psalm 13** ("How long, O LORD?"), which still ends in trust. Psalm 137 stays in the anguish.
- Elle notes that for the community, simply being able to voice the anguish is enough. The psalm doesn't have to resolve.
- Reed calls it a kind of **"anti-psalm."** Many psalms celebrate singing for joy after victory. Here the people are "*there*," not "*here*," and they are silent instead of singing.
- It also reverses the **Song of Moses** (Exodus 15, "I will sing to the LORD"). Israel is no longer singing over defeated enemies. It has been defeated and cannot sing.

#### 2. Translation Insights from Elle's Rendering

Elle's translation brings out several layers in the Hebrew:

- **"Over the sparkling rivers poured together"** is usually "by the rivers of Babylon." The Hebrew *Bavel* can be:
  - A place name (Babylon)
  - An image of **waters flowing together**, which fits Babylon's elaborate irrigation canals. Social class was even tied to how close you lived to the water.
  - A link to **Babel** and **confusion** (Genesis 11). Trauma brings a "sparkling confusion" that keeps catching your eye: *How did this happen? Why? This wasn't supposed to be my story.*
- **"Upon the braided willow branches… as hanged men our lyres swung"** is a haunting image. Brent and Reed singled it out. The silenced instruments hang like executed bodies.
- **Yerushalayim** is Elle's Hebrew-style rendering of Jerusalem.
- **"My tongue will cling to the roof of my mouth"** uses the root *davaq* (cling), the same verb as Genesis 2:24, where a man "clings" to his wife. It is a sticky, intimate word, "like getting honey off your fingers." It connects with love, sacredness, and covenant faithfulness.
- **"Sons of red dirt"** renders *bnei Edom*. It can mean "Edomites" (as in Robert Alter's translation), but the root *adam/adamah* relates to humanity, red earth, and the dirt Adam was made from. "Sons of red dirt" covers our humanity, where we came from, and where we are going.
- **"Daughter of mixed waters who is being bound by demons"** plays on a Hebrew root (*sh-d-d*) whose meanings include destruction, demons, and binding. A footnote gives an alternative, **"whose breasts are withering,"** which would parallel the reference to infants that follows.
- **"Your unborn"** translates a less common word for small children (*olel*). Elle chose "unborn" because of passages like **Job 3:16**, where it seems to mean babies who were never born. Either way, it refers to tiny, helpless humans.
- Hebrew poetry's punctuation and versification were added much later by the Masoretes, so Elle's indentation and spacing are deliberate interpretive choices.

#### 3. The Role of Memory in Grief

Elle describes several biblical models of remembering and forgetting:

| Model | Example | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **Monument memory** | Stones set up after crossing the Jordan (Joshua 4) | Changes the landscape so future generations retell the story |
| **Moving on** | David mourning his child, then resuming his duties (2 Samuel 12) | Grieving and then deliberately carrying on |
| **Two kinds of forgetting** | Joseph's story (Genesis 41:51) | Deliberate forgetting (breaking covenant) vs. the healing kind, like losing keys on the counter, where the past is woven into your story but no longer sits at the center of your identity |

- **Ebenezer** ("stone of help": *even* = stone, *ezer* = help) suggests that remembering helps, even when it is sad.
- Remembering can **comfort**. Elle's image of a pink-and-gold sunrise still lighting a hill back home shows that the present devastation is not the only reality.
- **Talking about loss takes care and should be earned.** Loss is not always clean. It may carry guilt, shame, unreconciled relationships, or even relief when the person who died was unsafe.
- Some losses are peaceful partings. Others, like the exile, are **tragic tearings apart** made worse by taunting.

#### 4. Refusal as Dignity: "How Will We Sing?"

- Eugene Peterson sees the exiles' silence as **dignifying**. It is the one form of agency they have left.
- Elle reads it through the experience of abuse. Perpetrators often want victims to reassure them ("I did a good job, right, honey?") or to share their inner world ("Tell me about your culture"). The victim can rightly refuse: *You don't get to experience my inner world with me.*
- Reed sees two kinds of captors in the text:
  1. Those who **ask** for the words of a song (curious, maybe trying to ease their own conscience).
  2. Those who **gloat** and demand a song for their entertainment (openly antagonistic).
- The self-curse ("may my right hand forget… may my tongue cling") shows that the silence comes from **love, not forgetfulness**. The song is sacred, and the people who destroyed Zion do not get to enjoy it.
- Reed adds a link between the **right hand** and **playing the lyre**. If I forget Jerusalem, may I lose my ability to play and sing.

#### 5. Naming the Perpetrator

- The psalm centers on **Zion and Jerusalem**, not God, as what was lost, and it blames **people**, not God.
- Elle notes that the Hebrew Bible has room for at least three responses to suffering:
  1. **Questioning God** ("This doesn't fit your character").
  2. **Blaming the human perpetrator** (Psalm 137).
  3. **Self-examination** ("How did we get here?", which is often the prophets' role).
- Reed notes that hatred can at least **identify where the evil is**. That is a necessary first step before any talk of mercy or compassion.

#### 6. The Imprecation: Praying Our Hate

- The closing "beatitudes" ("Happy is the one who…") echo Psalm 1 but turn it horribly upside down.
- Brent finds it disturbing. *How could God take pleasure in this?* Elle points out that the text does not necessarily mean God is the one carrying it out.
- Elle's view is that people with privilege, or without serious adverse life experiences, may not understand this language. For victims of severe and recent injustice, it doesn't feel like justice unless there is real accountability. She compares it to the Exodus plagues, where repeated refusals had real consequences.
- The wish to make perpetrators "feel what I feel" is **natural**. It is not holy, and it is not a place to stay, but it is part of the process.
- Asking victims to "just let it go" is often **for our own comfort**.

**Key voices:**

- **Eugene Peterson** (*Answering God*, chapter "Enemies"):
  - "Our hate needs to be prayed, not suppressed."
  - Hate is "the volcanic eruption of outrage when the holiness of being, ours and another's, has been violated."
  - Hate that isn't admitted "can quickly and easily metamorphose into the evil that provokes it."
  - Prayer "doesn't legitimize hate; it utilizes it."
  - "It is better to pray badly than not to pray at all. A ship that is dead in the water can't be steered."
- **Walter Brueggemann** pictures God as a kind of therapist. We say these things to God so we don't actually act on them. He uses the word **"brutalization"** for what the exiles suffered.
- **Miroslav Volf** (cited through a commentary and a sermon): "Can forgiveness be a mode of coming to terms too easily?… Genuine forgiveness is possible only when there has been a genuine articulation of hatred." The first step in forgiveness is **accusation**, as in Matthew 18 ("go and tell him his fault").

#### 7. Neurology, Prayer, and Redirecting Rage

- Hate releases **adrenaline**, which acts as "glue" that fixes memories in place.
- Rehearsing hate alone strengthens those neural pathways and makes healing (neuroplasticity) harder. Venting on its own is not enough.
- Bringing the anger to **God in prayer** and **processing it in community** turns it into a dialogue the Spirit can work in.
- **Ephesians 6:12** says our struggle is not against flesh and blood. Looping God in helps redirect rage away from other people, ourselves, and our families.
- **Genesis 4:7**: sin is "crouching at the door." Anger is dangerous and should be taken seriously.
- Marty's teaching: forgiveness isn't finished until the **"black cloud"** no longer surrounds the person when you think of them.

### Examples & Applications

1. **Reed's family loss.** Reed's wife's grandmother ("Mima") died on Christmas Eve. The family has been thinking about why remembering matters, and whether saying her name helps or hurts.
2. **The pink-and-gold sunrise.** Elle pictures exiles remembering morning light on a hill back home, the sound of insects waking and oxen calling. It shows how memory comforts and reminds people that another reality still exists.
3. **Abusers seeking validation.** A parent who asks a child they harmed, "I did a good job, right?" puts the victim in the role of therapist. Psalm 137's refusal gives permission to say no.
4. **Ukraine.** Reed describes a video of people driving back into their leveled city, wailing and cursing those who destroyed it. In moments like that, asking whether someone *ought* to hate makes little sense.
5. **Rwanda.** Marty once told the story of genocide survivors who forgave, and even adopted, the people who killed their family members. Elle calls it "insanely glorious," a partnership with the Holy Spirit in resurrection work, not a box ticked out of Christian duty.
6. **Gratitude as a path (2 Corinthians 1).** Paul is grateful that the comfort he received lets him comfort others. Elle stresses that the gratitude is **for who we have become**, never toward the perpetrator. She points to Joseph: "You meant it for evil, but God meant it for good" (Genesis 50:20).
7. **Curiosity about the perpetrator.** Reed says that asking for courage to learn more of the "who and why" behind those who hurt him has humanized them and turned anger into compassion, without excusing or approving what they did.
8. **Community and therapy.** Brent's closing advice is not to process these things alone. Find community, and get therapy if you need it.

### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

- **Brueggemann's orientation/disorientation/reorientation framework** applied to other psalms (e.g., Psalms 13, 22, 88, 109).
- **The imprecatory psalms** as a category, and how Christian traditions have handled, or avoided, them in worship.
- **Eugene Peterson's *Answering God*** and Miroslav Volf's *Exclusion and Embrace* on forgiveness, hatred, and justice.
- **The history of Edom's role in Jerusalem's fall** (Obadiah; Lamentations 4:21–22; Ezekiel 25:12–14).
- **Hebrew wordplay in Psalm 137**: *Bavel*/Babel/confusion, *davaq* (cling), *adam/adamah*, the *sh-d-d* root, and *olel*.
- **Jewish liturgical use of Psalm 137** before the grace after meals, and Psalm 126 on Sabbaths and festivals.
- **Theodicy**: how the Hebrew Bible holds questioning God, blaming people, and self-examination side by side.
- **The neuroscience of trauma, memory, and forgiveness**, and how spiritual practices interact with healing.
- **Restorative justice in post-genocide Rwanda** as a case study in Spirit-empowered forgiveness.
- **Jesus' prayer "Father, forgive them"** (Luke 23:34) alongside Psalm 137. How do both belong in the same Scriptures?

### Comprehension Questions

1. What does it mean to call Psalm 137 a "psalm of disorientation," and how does its ending differ from a psalm like Psalm 13?
2. Elle translates *Bavel* as "sparkling rivers poured together." What three layers of meaning does the word carry, and how does "confusion" connect to the experience of trauma?
3. Why do the exiles refuse to "sing the song of Yahweh" in a foreign land? How does Elle's reading through the experience of abuse explain that refusal?
4. According to Eugene Peterson, why should hate be prayed rather than suppressed, and what does he mean by saying prayer "utilizes" hate rather than "legitimizes" it?
5. What two practices do Elle and Reed suggest for moving from the posture of Psalm 137 toward loving one's enemies, and what cautions do they attach?

### Personalized Summary

Psalm 137 is a gift to anyone who has been through real devastation, because it doesn't ask them to tidy up their pain. It lets a community sit and weep together. It honors the memory of what was lost and refuses to perform that grief for the people who caused it. It also says out loud the rage that brutalization produces. Brent, Reed, and Elle show that this honesty isn't a lapse in faith. It may be where faith has to start: hate that is prayed can be shaped, and hate that is denied can quietly become the evil it hates. The episode is also clear that this isn't the end of the road. Through gratitude for who we are becoming, curiosity about those who hurt us, and the Spirit's surprising power, we can move slowly, in our own time, toward Jesus' expansiveness of spirit: leaving justice to God and even praying, "Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do."
