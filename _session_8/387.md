### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** 387 — Psalm 32  
**Hosts:** Brent Billings with guest co-host Reed Dent  
**Focus:** Psalm 32, read alongside Psalm 51, Psalm 139, and 2 Samuel 11–12 (David, Bathsheba, Uriah, and Nathan)

Reed and Brent continue their study of the Psalms by placing Psalm 32 next to Psalm 51, which they covered the week before with Psalm 139. Reed argues that Psalms 51 and 32 may come out of the same event: David's adultery with Bathsheba, his murder of Uriah, and Nathan the prophet confronting him. Psalm 51 sounds like raw despair written right after the confrontation. Psalm 32 sounds like an older David looking back calmly on the same story. The hosts follow shared words and themes across the two psalms: hiding, bones, silence and confession, and the promise to teach others. Together these show how a person's relationship to their own failures, and to God, can change over time. The episode closes with practical lessons about confession, the cost of hiding, and how God uses brokenness that has been redeemed.

### Key Takeaways

- Psalm 51 and Psalm 32 may form a pair. Psalm 51 is a cry from inside guilt, while Psalm 32 is a reflection written long after.
- The hiding theme moves across three psalms:
  1. Psalm 139: "I was not hidden from you."
  2. Psalm 51: "Hide your face from my sins."
  3. Psalm 32: "You are my hiding place."
- Psalm 51 is full of urgent commands to God (have mercy, wash, cleanse, create, restore). Psalm 32 has none. It opens with a beatitude ("Blessed is the one…") and speaks from experience.
- Keeping silent about sin is destructive. "When I kept silent, my bones wasted away." Hiding takes more energy and does more damage over time than confessing.
- Nathan announces David's forgiveness at once, but David still has to work through his guilt and its consequences. Forgiveness is not a card you carry around. Becoming whole takes a process of repentance.
- In Psalm 51 David promises, "I will teach transgressors your ways." Psalm 32 keeps that promise with a teaching section that reads like Proverbs.
- "Do not be like the horse or the mule" describes David himself. He had to be led "by bit and bridle," through Nathan's parable, before he would admit what he had done.
- Disorientation can come from outside (exile in Psalm 137) or be **self-imposed** (David's own sin).
- God does not erase our failures from our story. God redeems them and uses them for the good of the community.

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. Review: Psalms 139 and 51 as Two Ends of Human Experience

- **Psalm 139** expresses wonder and gratitude at being "knit together in my mother's womb," handmade and fully known by God.
- **Psalm 51** expresses horror at one's own capacity for evil: "Surely I was sinful from birth."
- Reed notes that people often use Psalm 51:5 as a foundation for doctrines of original sin. Read next to Psalm 139, though, it is better understood as a **personal expression of anguish** than as a doctrinal statement.
- Every person has both capacities, and most people feel each one sharply at different moments.

#### 2. The Story Behind Psalm 51 (2 Samuel 11–12)

- **The adultery takes very little space.** Within two verses, David sends for Bathsheba, sleeps with her, and she conceives. Most of the narrative is about the **cover-up**.
- **The murder of Uriah.** David orders the army to pull back from Uriah in battle so that he is killed. Reed compares this scheming to *The Godfather*.
- **Nathan ("gift").** The prophet's name means *gift*, and his rebuke really is a gift from God. His parable of the rich man taking a poor man's only lamb is about **Uriah**, not Bathsheba.
- **David sentences himself.** David declares that the man in the story deserves to die, and Nathan answers, "You are the man!"
- **"You killed him with the sword of the Ammonites."** Brent connects this line to Gregory Boyd's work on how Scripture assigns responsibility. David did not swing the sword, but the guilt is his.
- **The consequence.** Nathan says, "The Lord has taken away your sin… but the son born to you will die." The hosts set aside the theological difficulty of this for now.

#### 3. Psalm 51: Unfiltered and Immediate

- The heading places it "when the prophet Nathan came to him." Brent imagines Nathan still in the room.
- The **many imperatives** (have mercy, wash, cleanse, let me hear, hide, create, restore, deliver, open) show urgency: *do this now*.
- The tone is soaked in guilt, despair, and grief, like the pit in the stomach that comes with sudden recognition of what you have done.

#### 4. Psalm 32: Reflective and Instructional

- **Superscription: "A maskil of David."** The meaning is uncertain:
  - Robert Alter calls it a category of song whose exact nature is unknown, possibly related to giving instruction.
  - The NET Bible translates it as "a well-written song," from a verb meaning *to be prudent or wise*.
  - Reed's conclusion after studying the psalm: it is an **instructive or wisdom song**.
- **Structure:**
  1. **Verses 1–2:** a beatitude spoken from personal experience ("Blessed is the one whose transgressions are forgiven")
  2. **Verses 3–5:** past-tense memory of silence, a heavy hand, then confession and forgiveness
  3. **Verses 6–7:** an exhortation to pray, and God as hiding place
  4. **Verses 8–11:** wisdom-style teaching ("I will instruct you…")
- Brent calls it a **Psalm of New Orientation**, using the orientation, disorientation, and new orientation scheme from the series.

#### 5. The Evolution of "Hiding"

| Psalm | Statement | Relationship to God |
|---|---|---|
| 139 | "My frame was not hidden from you" | Comfort in being fully seen |
| 51 | "Hide your face from my sins" | Terror of being seen, wanting protection *from* God |
| 32 | "You are my hiding place" | Protection found *in* God |

- **The womb image:** a developing child is hidden from the world but fully seen by God. Being seen in total vulnerability is pictured as safety.
- **"Pray to you while you may be found" (v. 6):** Reed links this to hide-and-seek. God is not running a timer that will arbitrarily expire. What makes God hard to find is **our habit of pushing God away**. If "hide your face from me" becomes a way of life, we lose touch with God.

#### 6. Bones: From Wasting Away to Rejoicing

- **Psalm 51:8:** "Let the bones you have crushed rejoice." Crushing is sudden and violent, the experience of someone in the middle of it.
- **Psalm 32:3–4:** "When I kept silent, my bones wasted away through my groaning." Wasting away is slow, described by someone looking back.
- **The movement:** silence and wordless groaning lead to acknowledgment, which leads to rejoicing.
- **God's heavy hand** is not cruelty. Its purpose is to bring us to acknowledge what we have done, because healing cannot start until we do.

#### 7. Forgiveness vs. Restoration

- Brent observes that in 2 Samuel 12, David seems to miss the forgiveness part entirely and falls straight into the despair of Psalm 51. Psalm 32 finally says it: "You forgave the guilt of my sin."
- Reed points out that it would be strange for David to hear "your sin is taken away" and simply reply, "Cool, moving on."
- The goal is not a forgiveness card but becoming **whole, restored, rightly related people**. That takes the real work of repentance, especially when we have badly harmed someone.
- "I'm sorry" followed by "I forgive you" with no further work has been tried in many church settings, and it does not work.

#### 8. "Then I Will Teach Transgressors"

- **Psalm 51:12–13:** "Restore to me the joy of your salvation… then I will teach transgressors your ways."
- **Psalm 32:8–10:** "I will instruct you and teach you in the way you should go." This is David doing what he promised.
- **Horse and mule:** David needed Nathan to lead him to the truth. His advice is to come to God willingly instead.
- **Trust, redefined:** trusting God does not mean never doing wrong. It means giving up damage control, no longer keeping the house of cards standing, and saying plainly, "I did this."
- **A warning against using our sin too quickly:** Using your sin as a teaching prop before your soul has actually faced it robs the lesson of its honesty and power. Brent compares this to quoting "God works all things for good" as a reflex.

### Examples & Applications

- **The weight of keeping quiet:** Brent says he tries not to think too long about a confession he needs to make, because the longer he waits, the bigger and more unpleasant it seems. Reed agrees: an unspoken confession grows like a monster. This is the wasting away of Psalm 32:3.
- **Confession rarely goes as expected:** Brent notes that confession is either much simpler or much more complicated than he imagined, and he always guesses wrong. Either way, staying silent costs more.
- **"The heat of summer" (v. 4):** Brent points out that summer is *a season*. The heavy hand does not last forever. Reed adds the picture of autumn, when the heat breaks and it becomes the best time to be outside.
- **Brief accounts of huge events:** Reed compares the two-verse adultery account to the Gospels' plain "and they crucified him." Scripture often tells its biggest events in the fewest words.
- **Modern parallels:** a person caught in workplace dishonesty, a family secret, or a broken relationship faces the same choice. Keep up an exhausting cover story, or confess and begin restoration.
- **Ministry from redeemed brokenness:** in recovery communities and mentoring, people who have worked through their own failures can help others, but only after real healing, not the day after.

### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

1. **Three Hebrew words for wrongdoing:** Psalms 32 and 51 use three terms, translated "sin," "iniquity," and "transgression." Roughly, they mean missing the mark, being crooked or bent, and violating trust or rebelling. English translations do not always render them consistently. Reed recommends the Bible Project's "Bad Words" video series.
2. **The meaning of *maskil*:** compare Psalm 32 with other psalms that carry this heading to test whether "instructive song" holds up.
3. **Psalm 51 and original sin:** how 51:5 has been used doctrinally, and whether Psalm 139 qualifies that reading.
4. **Divine responsibility and violence:** Gregory Boyd's *Cross Vision* (and *The Crucifixion of the Warrior God*) on how Scripture attributes actions to God and to people, such as "you killed him with the sword of the Ammonites."
5. **The death of David's child (2 Samuel 12):** the hard theological questions the hosts deliberately set aside.
6. **Self-imposed vs. external disorientation:** compare Psalm 32 and 51 with Psalm 137 (exile in Babylon).
7. **Psalm 32 in Romans 4:** Paul quotes Psalm 32:1–2 as an example of righteousness credited apart from works.
8. **Next episode:** Psalm 19, and how it may connect to today's themes of instruction and wisdom.

### Comprehension Questions

1. How do the hosts trace the theme of "hiding" across Psalms 139, 51, and 32, and what does that movement say about David's changing relationship with God?
2. What differences in grammar and tone (imperatives, tense, beatitude, teaching section) suggest that Psalm 51 was written close to the events and Psalm 32 much later?
3. Compare "let the bones you have crushed rejoice" (Psalm 51) with "my bones wasted away" (Psalm 32). What does the change in imagery suggest?
4. Nathan declares David forgiven immediately. Why do the hosts argue that David still had to go through grief, acknowledgment, and repentance before he was truly restored?
5. How does Psalm 32's warning about the horse and mule connect to David's own experience with Nathan, and why does Reed warn against using our failures as teaching material too quickly?

### Summary

This episode shows David's failure as one stage in a longer story, not the end of it. In Psalm 51, David is crushed and pleading, almost unable to take in the forgiveness Nathan has just announced. In Psalm 32, an older David looks back. He remembers how hiding his sin wore him down, how confession brought relief, and how the God he once wanted to hide from became his hiding place. He also keeps the promise he made in Psalm 51 and teaches others: don't be like a mule that has to be dragged to the truth. Come to God willingly.

Several lessons follow for your own life. Confession is hard, but hiding is harder and does more damage. Forgiveness opens the way to restoration, but restoration takes real work. Seasons of heavy guilt are seasons and will pass. Your worst failures do not have to be erased to be redeemed. God does not sweep them under the rug, and you don't need to either. Once they are truly healed, they can become wisdom that helps others find their way back to God.
