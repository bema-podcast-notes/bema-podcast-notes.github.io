### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** 388: Psalm 19 — "Are You Listening?"  
**Hosts:** Brent Billings and Reed Dent  
**Focus:** Psalm 19, the heavens, the sun, and the Torah

Brent leads this episode on Psalm 19, a psalm he thinks about almost every time he flies. Brent and Reed call it a psalm of orientation, the first category in the series' framework of orientation, disorientation and reorientation. They admit what many readers feel: the first half, about the silent glory of the heavens, is easy to love, but the second half, about the law, statutes and precepts, can feel disconnected and repetitive. Drawing on Rabbi David Fohrman's reading, Jewish commentators, C.S. Lewis, and Frederick Buechner, they show how the psalm ties its two halves together. Sun imagery runs all through the Torah section, and Torah language runs through the sky section. The heavens speak without words, and the Torah is made of words yet shines like the sun. The episode closes with two questions: *Are you listening?* and *Will we join in the proclamation?*

### Key Takeaways

- Psalm 19 is a psalm of **orientation**. It feels almost like the garden before the fall: sin is mentioned only briefly and from a distance, unlike Psalm 32, where sin weighs heavily on the psalmist.
- The psalm has **two halves of seven verses each** (using Hebrew verse numbering), plus a closing verse that stands alone: "May these words of my mouth…"
- The first half moves from the **broad heavens** to **one specific light, the sun**. The second half describes **Torah** using sunlight language: pure, trustworthy, radiant, enlightening the eyes, enduring, firm.
- Each half **borrows qualities from the other**. The silent heavens "declare" and "speak" as if they had words, while the wordy Torah "shines" like the sun.
- Torah is not a rigged test meant to prove our helplessness. It is a **path that is life itself**, a gift from the Beloved that the psalmist adores.
- The closing image, **"my rock and my redeemer,"** holds together stillness and movement. In different ways, both the sun and the Torah show God as both.
- The episode's two questions in order: **"Are you listening?"** (receiving creation's silent witness) and **"Will we join in the proclamation?"** (answering with our own words).

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. Psalm 19 as an Orientation Psalm

- Two things orient the psalmist equally: **creation** (vv. 1–6) and **God's instruction** (vv. 7–13).
- The tone is ideal, almost romantic, close to the **Song of Songs**. The psalmist praises the law the way a lover talks about their beloved.
- Sin shows up only as "hidden faults" and "willful sins," a quick look into the darkness before returning to being "blameless" and "innocent."

#### 2. The Silent Speech of the Heavens (vv. 1–4)

- The heavens "pour forth speech" and "reveal knowledge" **day after day, night after night**, without stopping.
- Yet "they have no speech, they use no words, no sound is heard." Fohrman points out that large movements are usually loud, like jet engines, so we might expect the movement of the sun and stars to make a deafening noise. Instead it is completely **silent**.
- This creates a sense of **wonder**, which both hosts identify as the heart of their own experiences: Brent above the clouds, Reed on the train bridge.

#### 3. A Textual Puzzle: "Voice" or "Measuring Line"? (v. 4)

- **Masoretic Text (Hebrew):** *qav*, "their measuring line"
- **Septuagint, Jerome, Syriac:** "their voice." The NET footnote says "measuring line" makes no sense here and prefers "voice."
- The two Hebrew words differ by roughly one letter (a missing *lamed*), so a copying error is plausible either way.
- **Ibn Ezra** and Fohrman keep "line." It fits the image that follows: the sun runs a fixed circuit, a "line," across the sky.
- **Timeline note:** The Septuagint dates to the last few centuries BC. The oldest complete Masoretic manuscripts are from the medieval period, though the Dead Sea Scrolls make the picture more complicated.
- **Takeaway:** Translators sometimes have to make judgment calls, and footnotes don't always explain why.

#### 4. The Sun's Tent, the Bridegroom, and the Champion (vv. 4–6)

- God "pitched a tent for the sun." The sun comes out "like a bridegroom from his chamber" and "like a champion rejoicing to run his course."
- Reed's reading: the sun comes out full of confidence and exuberance, not as cold clockwork but eager to run the path God set for it.
- Brent's open question for listeners: Does God pitch a tent in the heavens for anyone or anything else?

#### 5. Sunlight Language in the Torah Section (vv. 7–11)

| Torah Attribute | Sunlight Connection |
|---|---|
| Perfect / pure, refreshing the soul | Bright light shows whether something is pure. Morning sun warms and revives. |
| Trustworthy, making wise the simple | You can't trust what you haven't seen in the light (the mismatched socks example). |
| Right / straight, giving joy to the heart | The sun's straight, reliable course |
| Radiant, giving light to the eyes | The most direct sun image in the passage |
| Pure / clean, enduring forever | Sunlight cleanses. The sun is our closest picture of permanence. |
| Firm and righteous | The sun's steady, dependable rising and setting |
| More precious than gold, sweeter than honey | Gold and honey share the sun's color |

**Hebrew wordplay Fohrman highlights:**

- **Nizhar** ("warned"): drop the prefix *nun* and you get **zohar**, "radiance/sunlight." So the line could read "by them your servant is *warned*" and also "*illuminated*" (or "warmed").
- **Naqqeni** ("cleanse me"): from *naqah*, a word for purity, which continues the cleansing-light theme.
- **"May they not rule over me"**: Reed links this to Genesis 1, where the sun is made to "rule" the day. If sin should not rule over us, then God's instruction should, the way the sun rules the day.

#### 6. Each Half Borrows From the Other

- The **heavens** have no words, yet they "declare" God's glory as if they could speak.
- The **Torah** is entirely words, yet it "shines," "gives light," and has the color and sweetness of gold and honey.
- The psalmist uses these opposite qualities so that each half brings the other to life. The sun rises and reminds us who we are. The Torah gives us the words that make our purpose clear.
- **Summary:** The sun brings life to the world, and God's words bring life to humanity.

#### 7. Rethinking "the Law"

Reed describes moving away from an inherited view of the law:

- **Old view:** Torah is a test path that ends in heaven. One misstep and you fail. Its real purpose is to show you that you are wretched and hopeless.
- **Better view:** Walking the path of Torah doesn't so much *lead to* life as *is* life, like a hiking loop above Yosemite Valley that ends where it began but leaves you more alive. Stepping off the path isn't breaking an arbitrary rule. It is more like stepping off a cliff.
- **Buechner (*Wishful Thinking*):** There are laws about how things *should be* ("No Trespassing") and laws about how things *are* (gravity). God's law is the second kind, summed up in 1 John 3:14: "Whoever does not love abides in death." If you doubt it, you can test it, just as you could test gravity from a tenth-story window.
- If love fulfills the law, then walking Torah means walking in love. Underneath this is the idea "I am my beloved's and my beloved is mine": the law is a gift from God, and keeping it is a gift we give back.

#### 8. Other Perspectives on the Psalm

- **C.S. Lewis:** "I take this to be the greatest poem in the Psalter and one of the greatest lyrics in the world."
- **Charles Spurgeon:** The psalm compares God's "two great books," nature and Scripture.
- **Rashi:** The Torah lights up a person the way the sun lights their path.
- **Ibn Ezra and Radak:** Both the sun and the Torah testify to their Creator's glory.
- **Metzudat David:** The Torah is more perfect and complete than the powerful sun.
- **Malbim:** The sun shows God's glory in the physical world, and the Torah shows it in the spiritual realm.
- **C.S. Lewis (paraphrased by Reed):** "I believe in Christianity as I believe that the sun has risen: not only because I see it, but because by it I see everything else." The psalmist seems to feel this way about Torah.

#### 9. "My Rock and My Redeemer" (v. 14 / Hebrew v. 15)

- **A rock** is strong because it **doesn't move**. **A redeemer** is strong because it **moves**.
- Reed's example: **Harriet Tubman**, a redeemer who kept moving across borders, yet a firm foundation for the people she led to freedom, like "a rock that's moving."
- **The sun looks like a redeemer.** It moves across the sky, ends the darkness, and brings everything back to life. Yet from our point of view it is the **most stationary** thing we know: its apparent motion comes from Earth moving around it. At a larger scale it does move through the galaxy, carrying us with it. It is both rock and redeemer.

#### 10. Liturgy and Timing: Shacharit

- Psalm 19 is part of the Jewish **Sabbath morning prayers** and is used on festivals, especially **Shavuot**, the celebration of the giving of Torah.
- Imu Shalev and Fohrman explain that **Shacharit** (morning prayer) should be prayed close to sunrise. Creation is "having a party," and you either join the conversation or miss it.
- Fohrman notes that, in terms of halakha (how the law is lived out), some argue it is **more important to pray on time than to pray with a quorum** (*minyan*). Praying alone at sunrise beats praying with others later.

### Examples & Applications

- **Flying at sunrise:** Brent describes climbing out of a dark, rainy day above the clouds into blazing light. It's his regular reminder that "the heavens declare the glory of God." He keeps taking dozens of cloud photos and would even pick a window seat over a first-class aisle seat.
- **The train bridge story:** As a college student in rural Missouri, Reed stood on a train bridge at twilight. Stars were coming out overhead, a sunset-lit thunderhead flashed with lightning on the horizon, and thousands of fireflies glowed in the fields. His campus minister quoted Psalm 19:1–4, then asked, *"Are you listening?"*
- **Louis C.K.'s "miracle of flight" bit:** A reminder that familiarity dulls wonder. We watch clouds out the window like TV and forget we are actually up in the sky.
- **Mismatched socks:** You get dressed in the dark sure you're wearing two black socks, then step into sunlight and find one is navy. Trustworthiness depends on light.
- **Dark fabrics hide stains:** Bright light shows purity or impurity. It's a modern picture of "the law of the Lord is perfect."
- **Morning sunlight and sleep cycles:** Getting early sunlight in your eyes supports alertness and healthy rhythms. It's a physical parallel to Torah "refreshing the soul."
- **Navigating by the sun:** Without GPS, people find direction from the sun's fixed path. Torah offers the same kind of orientation for life.
- **The Merton contrast:** Thomas Merton's prayer ("My Lord God, I have no idea where I am going…") is a prayer of disorientation. Psalm 19 is its opposite: the psalmist has never been more sure where they are going. Both kinds of prayer have their season.
- **Practical habit:** Try being up at sunrise. Spend the first moments listening in silence, then answer with prayer or Scripture as the day starts.

7. **Genesis 1 and "ruling" lights:** follow the language of the sun "ruling the day" into Psalm 19:13's "may they not rule over me."
8. **A modern Psalm 19:** Reed invites scientifically minded listeners to write a psalm comparing Torah or the gospel to the sun as we now understand it, a star moving through the galaxy and carrying us with it.
9. **Shacharit and the Jewish liturgical use of Psalm 19** on Sabbath and Shavuot.
10. **Wonder as a spiritual practice:** how familiarity dulls our awe, and how we might recover it.

### Comprehension Questions

2. What is the textual disagreement in verse 4 between "voice" and "measuring line," and how does Fohrman's preference for "line" connect to the sun imagery that follows?
3. Name at least three attributes of the Torah in verses 7–11 and explain how each connects to the qualities of sunlight.

### Personal Summary

Psalm 19 can feel like two separate poems, one about a glorious sky and one about a list of commandments. This episode shows that they are one song. The heavens speak without words, and the Torah, which is made of words, shines like the sun. Both are gifts from the same Creator, and both orient us in a world that is easy to get lost in. Reed's new understanding of Torah as a path that *is* life, not a test we're bound to fail, explains why the psalmist calls it sweeter than honey. Brent's love of flying and Reed's night on the train bridge are reminders that wonder is always on offer if we pay attention. The episode leaves two questions to carry into each morning: **Are you listening?** And once you've listened, **will you join in the proclamation?** Like the sun coming out of its tent, we're invited to rise and add our own words to what creation is already saying.
