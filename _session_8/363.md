### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** 363: Introduction to Isaiah, Part 2: The Prophetic Imagination  
**Hosts:** Marty Solomon and Brent Billings, with guest Reed Dent  
**Focus:** What a biblical prophet is and does (Exodus 7:1–2; Isaiah 1; Jeremiah 6:14; Jeremiah 29:11; Amos 5:24; Isaiah 25:8), using Walter Brueggemann's *The Prophetic Imagination*

This is the second of two episodes introducing the Isaiah series. Reed Dent leads the conversation. The first part asks what a prophet actually is. The hosts set the popular idea of the prophet as a fortune teller against the biblical picture of a spokesperson who stands with God and confronts a whole community in the present. The second part works through Walter Brueggemann's short book *The Prophetic Imagination*. Brueggemann describes how a "dominant culture" tames God's calling on a people. He contrasts a religion of triumphalism and a politics of oppression with a religion of God's freedom and a politics of justice and compassion. He also shows how the prophet works through two linked moves: criticism that starts in grief, and energizing that starts in hope. The episode ends with Brueggemann's three-part arc of the prophetic message and sets listeners up to read Isaiah itself.

### Key Takeaways

- **A prophet is a spokesperson, not a fortune teller.** Exodus 7:1–2 gives a clear model: Moses will be "like God to Pharaoh," and Aaron will be his prophet. The prophet carries someone else's voice.
- **The prophet speaks out of solidarity with God.** This fits Heschel's idea of *pathos*. The prophet has shared God's experience and brings it to people who have not.
- **Prophecy is mainly about the present and the community.** Predictions about the future, when they appear, are meant to push people to act now. The message goes to a whole people, and kings are addressed as their representatives.
- **Real prophets make people uncomfortable.** Frederick Buechner jokes that "there is no evidence to suggest that anyone ever asked a prophet home for supper more than once."
- **Prophecy enters an existing story.** The prophet reminds a people who have already been rescued and called of who they are, and holds them to it.
- **The dominant culture tames the calling.** Brueggemann calls this culture a *religion of triumphalism* joined to a *politics of oppression*.
- **The prophet imagines an alternative.** Against triumphalism the prophet offers a *religion of God's freedom*. Against oppression the prophet offers a *politics of justice and compassion*.
- **Criticism and energizing must go together.** Criticism without hope turns into cynicism. Hope without grief turns into sentimentality.
- **Grief and hope are linked.** "Real criticism begins in the capacity to grieve," and only people who hope deeply can grieve deeply.
- **"Prophecy without doxology becomes ideology."** Prophetic critique has to be rooted in worship of God and hope in God.
- **The prophetic message has a three-part arc:** indictment, then a call to repentance with a warning of judgment, then God's final act of restoration.

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. Defining the Prophet: Spokesperson and Partner

- **The Exodus model (Exodus 6:28–7:2):** Moses says he has "faltering lips," so God appoints Aaron to speak for him. "See, I have made you like God to Pharaoh, and your brother Aaron will be your prophet." Brent points out that Aaron had it easy: he only had to speak for Moses, while Moses carried the weight of speaking for God.
- **More than a mouthpiece:** Marty connects the text to Heschel. The phrase "you will be as God to Pharaoh" points to solidarity and *pathos*. The prophet does not just pass along a message. The prophet has been *with* God, knows where God stands, and carries that experience to others.
- **Buechner's definition (from *Peculiar Treasures*):**
  1. "Prophet means spokesman, not fortune teller."
  2. Their "unfathomable audacity" was claiming to speak for the Lord and Creator of the universe.
  3. "There is no evidence to suggest that anyone ever asked a prophet home for supper more than once."
- **The weight of "thus says the LORD":** Reed notes that saying these words on God's behalf is a terrible and frightening responsibility.

#### 2. Caricatures of Prophecy

Reed grew up in a charismatic church where visiting "prophets" would "speak into your life." He sees some truth in that practice, but thinks the emphasis was in the wrong place:

| Popular Caricature | Biblical Prophecy |
|---|---|
| Aimed at the individual | Aimed at the community or nation |
| Focused on the future | Focused on the present |
| Says what God will *do for you* | Says what God *requires of you* (justice, righteousness) |
| Comforting and affirming | Confrontational, often offensive |
| Private consultation | Public proclamation (Isaiah 1:2 calls on the heavens and the earth to listen) |

- **False prophets** say "peace, peace, when there is no peace" (Jeremiah 6:14). Comforting prophets get invited back for supper. True prophets do not.
- **Jeremiah 29:11 out of context:** The well-known poster verse ("I know the plans I have for you…") came from a prophet who was thrown into a cistern and left for dead. Its meaning depends on that context.

#### 3. Prophets as "Provocateurs of the Imagination"

- Marty borrows a phrase from Shane Claiborne: prophets are *provocateurs of the imagination*. Reed adds that they are provocateurs *with a purpose*. They do not shock for the sake of shock. They want to get people out of their seats and acting.
- **Modern examples the hosts name:** Martin Luther King Jr., Wendell Berry, Bono, Banksy. They do not put these figures on the level of Isaiah. Each one, in some way, critiques the injustices of their age.
- **Lifestyle as message:** Wendell Berry's life (farming in a small Kentucky town, writing by hand or on a typewriter, reportedly refusing speaking events that require air travel) itself preaches what he writes about.
- **Faith matters:** Reed stresses that biblical prophecy is more than social activism. It is grounded in theology and faith.
- **Unknown prophets:** Many people act prophetically without any celebrity. These are the ordinary people who say what everyone else is afraid to say.

#### 4. Brueggemann's Framework: The Dominant Culture vs. the Prophetic Alternative

**Step 1: A calling already exists.** The prophet speaks to a people who have already been rescued (the Exodus) and commissioned to be "a kingdom of priests" (Exodus 19:6), showing the one true God to the nations and bringing healing to the world. The prophet's job is partly to *remind* them.

**Step 2: The calling has been "domesticated."** The dominant culture tames, co-opts, or overrides the people's distinct calling. This can happen through prosperity, as with the wealthy Israel Amos confronts. It can happen through fear, as when Ahaz turns to Assyria for protection in Isaiah's time.

**Step 3: The prophet names the two pillars of the dominant culture.** Brueggemann's model is Pharaoh's Egypt:

| Dominant Culture (Pharaoh's Egypt) | Prophetic Alternative (Moses / Sinai) |
|---|---|
| **Religion of triumphalism:** "We are here to win, conquer, prosper, and remake the world in our image." This leads to worshipping one's own success, or idols such as economy, military power, or progress. | **Religion of God's freedom:** God (*Adonai*) is completely free and bound by nothing. People are freed to worship this God ("Let my people go, so that they may worship me"). |
| **Politics of oppression:** People who fall behind are dead weight. Those at the bottom matter only as fuel for the system. Example: Pharaoh's demand for more bricks, now made without straw (Exodus 5). | **Politics of justice and compassion:** The vulnerable are lifted up, protected, given relationship, and brought into the life of the community. |

- **Cause and effect:** Brueggemann argues that the religion of triumphalism directly *produces* the oppression of the vulnerable.
- **Imagination:** The prophet does not write a new constitution. The prophet *evokes* a different world that God makes possible, one marked by justice, righteousness, and mercy. One of Brueggemann's favorite words is *alternative*: an alternative community and an alternative consciousness.
- **Marty's summary:** Triumphalism builds a system everyone must bow to. God's freedom brings no system "where we win." Instead, love, justice, compassion, *shalom*, and wholeness fill all of creation.

#### 5. Biblical Justice and Righteousness

- **The American idea of justice** tends to be *punitive* or *retributive*: tit-for-tat, balancing the scales, punishment.
- **The prophetic idea of justice** is *restorative*. The people at the bottom, who are used up so others can flourish, are raised to the same level, protected, and brought back into the community.
- **The "quartet of the vulnerable":** the poor, the widow, the orphan, and the sojourner (immigrant). These are the people the system as designed does not serve.
- **Hebrew roots (*mishpat* and *tzedakah*):** Marty describes justice and righteousness as *right relationship*: a straight line rather than a crooked one. Sin twists and bends relationships. Justice and righteousness straighten them out. Oppressive systems fall, not because of a political ideology, but because God intends everything to be in its right place.
- **The source of prophetic anger:** Reed observes that the prophets are not cynics or pessimists. Their anger comes from a deep conviction that creation was made for *shalom* and is headed toward it. They are angry that the present falls short of that.

#### 6. Criticism and Energizing: Grief and Hope

The prophet evokes the alternative through a two-sided message:

1. **Criticism**, which begins in **grief**
   - Brueggemann (p. 11): "Real criticism begins in the capacity to grieve because grief is the most visceral announcement that things are not right. Only in the empire are we pressed and urged and invited to pretend that things are all right."
   - Criticism means helping people face the *mortality* of what they think is permanent. The "triumphant" empire will end.
   - It also means admitting we are complicit. Even if we do not create oppression, we may benefit from it.
2. **Energizing**, which begins in **hope**
   - It points toward where things can go: "Let justice roll down like waters" (Amos 5:24).

**Why both are needed:**
- **Criticism alone** produces what Reed calls "ironic hipster" cynicism. It takes little intelligence to point out what is broken. Marty notes this is a common weakness in deconstruction conversations, which tend to run on criticism.
- **Energizing alone** produces sentimentality. Reed's brother-in-law struggled in churches that offered only happy songs and no grief. That kind of hope is "impotent" and cannot move people to act.

**Grief and hope are connected:** Numbing yourself to grief also numbs your capacity for hope and joy. You cannot choose where the numbness stops. The result is apathy. Heschel puts it this way: the prophet is troubled not by a world devoid of meaning but by a world *deaf* to meaning. The prophets seem intense or "crazy" because they feel deeply. In fact they are *more* in touch with reality than the half-anesthetized people around them.

#### 7. Doxology: Keeping Prophecy Grounded

- **"Prophecy without doxology becomes ideology."**
- If critique and hope are not rooted in praise of God, devotion to God, and hope in God, they collapse into partisan ideologies on the left or right, each protecting its own "pets."
- Prophecy rooted in doxology can actually transform people and point toward an alternative kingdom that no worldly power can produce.

#### 8. The Three-Part Prophetic Arc

1. **Indictment:** Here is what is wrong.
2. **Call to repentance and warning:** Turn around, or judgment will come. Judgment here is not arbitrary punishment. Unjust systems are *unsustainable* in God's economy and will collapse.
3. **Divine restoration:** In the end, God is the one who saves and makes things right. God will restore the people's fortunes, gather them to the mountain, and "swallow up death forever" (Isaiah 25:8).

There is a tension between parts 2 and 3. The people are truly responsible to act, and yet their final hope rests on God alone.

### Examples & Applications

- **Reading the prophets:** As you read Isaiah, Amos, or Nahum, ask: *Is this criticism or energizing? Where is the grief? Where is the hope?*
- **Spotting domestication:** Ask what "dominant culture" might be co-opting your own community's calling today, whether economic success, national power, progress, or political tribalism. Reed jokes that he is glad this is all "ancient history" with nothing to do with today.
- **Pharaoh's bricks today:** Any system that treats workers or vulnerable people as disposable ("make your own straw, or we'll find someone else") repeats the politics of oppression.
- **Checking your church:** A worship life with only upbeat songs and no lament may be offering sentimentality instead of real hope. Practicing lament can make room for real hope.
- **Recognizing complicity:** Ask where you benefit from systems that harm others, even if you did not create them.
- **Prophets around you:** Think of ordinary people in your life who say the hard things others avoid, and consider how their lives back up their words.
- **Reading verses in context:** Before quoting Jeremiah 29:11 as personal encouragement, remember its original setting: exile, suffering, and a word to a whole community.
- **Light moment (NFL banter):** After the outro, the hosts tease each other about "prophecy" in football predictions, including Marty's correct call that the Chiefs would lose their opener to the Lions. Marty jokes that, as a fan, he has "a great capacity for grief" and "great capacity for hope."

### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

1. **Read *The Prophetic Imagination*:** Brueggemann's book is about 125 pages, dense but short. Compare it with Heschel's much longer *The Prophets*. The hosts call the two books "two sides of a coin."
2. **Frederick Buechner's *Peculiar Treasures*:** Read his short, witty character sketches of biblical prophets.
3. ***Mishpat* and *tzedakah*:** Do a word study tracing justice and righteousness through the Torah and the Prophets.
4. **The quartet of the vulnerable:** Follow the poor, widow, orphan, and sojourner through Deuteronomy, Isaiah, Jeremiah, and Amos.
5. **Lament as spiritual practice:** Explore the lament psalms and Lamentations, and how grief prepares the way for hope.
6. **Prophetic sign-acts:** Study the prophets' radical *actions* (Isaiah walking barefoot, Jeremiah's yoke, Ezekiel lying on his side) as embodied messages.
7. **Ahaz and Assyria:** Prepare for Isaiah 7 by studying the political crisis in which Ahaz trusted an empire instead of God.
8. **True vs. false prophecy:** Look at Deuteronomy 18, Jeremiah 23 and 28, and the "peace, peace" theme.
9. **Modern prophetic voices:** Consider how MLK's "Letter from Birmingham Jail" or Wendell Berry's essays reflect Brueggemann's pattern of criticism and energizing.
10. **Deconstruction and reconstruction:** Reflect on how the pairing of criticism and energizing could shape healthier faith communities.

### Personal Summary

This episode changes what "prophetic" means. The biblical prophets were not mystics reading personal destinies. They were spokespeople who shared God's heart and stood up in public to remind a rescued people of their calling. Their message was uncomfortable, which is why they were rarely invited back for supper. Brueggemann gives a clear way to read them. Empires run on triumphalism and oppression. God's people are called to a different world shaped by God's freedom, justice, and compassion. The prophet critiques through grief and energizes through hope, and both are needed: grief without hope turns into cynicism, and hope without grief turns into sentimentality. Beneath all of it is doxology. Prophecy grounded in worship moves people to act, while prophecy cut off from God becomes another ideology. Going into Isaiah, the guiding questions are: Where is the grief? Where is the hope? And where does this text call me and my community, here and now, to straighten what has been bent?
