### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** BEMA 375: What Perseverance Produces  
**Hosts:** Marty Solomon and Brent Billings  
**Focus:** Isaiah 49:5–6; 50:1–3; 51:1–23; 52:1–4; 52:13–53:12; 54:11–17; 55:1–13

Marty and Brent finish Isaiah's Servant Discourse. They start with the question of who the servant is in Isaiah 49, a passage some rabbis read as Isaiah describing himself. They follow God's reminder that Israel's sin led to exile, and God's call to "remember the rock from which you were cut" (Abraham and Sarah). Then they come to the famous suffering servant passage of Isaiah 52–53. Marty asks listeners to hear it at least once as a word to God's suffering people in exile, before they read Jesus into it. The episode ends with the result of that faithful suffering: a rebuilt city better than the first (Isaiah 54) and the open invitation and joy of Isaiah 55.

### Key Takeaways

- The servant's identity is debated. Through most of the discourse the servant has been Jacob or Israel. In Isaiah 49:5–6 the language becomes more personal, so some Jewish readers think the servant is Isaiah himself. Marty still reads it as the same servant.
- In Isaiah 50, God admits that Israel's sin brought them into exile. He also asks why no one answered when he came to rescue them: "Was my arm too short to deliver you?"
- Isaiah 51 calls the people to remember where they came from, Abraham and Sarah. In Marty's view, exile happened because Israel had "lost the plot" of its own story.
- God is called comforter in Isaiah 51:12. Marty links this to the Holy Spirit as *paraclete* (comforter, helper, advocate) in John 14 and 16.
- Jerusalem has already drunk "the cup of wrath" to the bottom. Now God takes the cup away and hands it to their oppressors.
- **Read the suffering servant (Isaiah 52:13–53:12) as Israel at least once.** Hearing it first as a message to God's suffering people shows Jesus as the perfect example of faithful suffering. He is more than the answer to a prophetic blank.
- Faithful, non-violent suffering is fruitful. Because one generation endured exile well, their children and grandchildren inherit healing and a rebuilt world.
- "No weapon forged against you will prevail" (Isaiah 54:17) is often lifted out of context as a promise of triumph. In Isaiah it comes right after a long passage about a servant who chose to suffer.
- What perseverance produces is redemption. God promises a restored city built of jewels, better than "Jerusalem 1.0."
- Isaiah 55 is a "relief" chapter. It offers a free invitation to the thirsty and the promise that God's word will not return empty.

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. Who Is the Servant? (Isaiah 49:5–6)

- **Jewish readings:** Jewish commentators do not identify the servant as Jesus. They debate whether the servant is:
  - **Israel collectively**, which has clearly been true for much of the discourse ("Jacob my servant").
  - **Isaiah the prophet**, especially where the servant sounds individual and personal.
- **Why Isaiah 49 raises the question:** Verse 5 says the servant will "bring Jacob back to him and gather Israel to himself." If the servant has been Israel, how can the servant also bring Israel back? Verse 6 ("Is it too small a thing for you to be my servant to restore the tribes of Jacob...") sounds like Isaiah's own calling.
- **Grammar:** Brent checks and confirms that the Hebrew is singular. That fits the servant being a single figure throughout, whether that figure stands for a people or a person.
- **Marty's position:** He still sees the same servant, but grants that Isaiah may be living out the role in a distinctive way. He says the question is not settled.

#### 2. Israel's Sin and the Servant's Obedience (Isaiah 50)

- God uses the image of a divorce certificate and a sale to creditors: "Because of your sins you were sold."
- The tone is not only rebuke. God is asking: "Yes, your sin got you here, but when I came looking for you, why was no one there? Did you think I couldn't save you?"
- **The call:** Don't lose hope. Answer when God calls. Suffer well, so that the world can be put back together afterward.

#### 3. Remember Where You Came From (Isaiah 51:1–3)

- "Look to the rock from which you were cut... Look to Abraham your father and to Sarah."
- Marty's diagnosis is that God's people forgot the plot. They forgot what it means to trust the story and to be people of **radical hospitality**, as Abraham was.
- The answer is to go back to Genesis and remember what the whole story is about.
- The promise: God will make Zion's deserts "like Eden" and her wastelands "like the garden of the Lord."

#### 4. The "Islands" (Isaiah 51:5)

- Brent notes that "islands" comes up often in Isaiah.
- **Meaning:** The term stands for the far-off nations, the Gentiles. It works like *goyim* (nations), but it is a different Hebrew word (*iyyim*). The same word still means "islands" in modern Hebrew.
- **Genesis link:** The word first appears in Genesis 10:5 ("the isles of the Gentiles"), the Table of Nations. This shows Isaiah's concern for all the nations.

#### 5. God as Comforter (Isaiah 51:12–16)

- "I, even I, am he who comforts you."
- This continues the previous episode's theme: God as helper, advocate, savior, and *paraclete* ("one who comes alongside").
- **John connection:** In John 14 and 16, Jesus promises the Spirit as comforter, who will turn the disciples' grief into joy.

#### 6. The Cup of Wrath Reversed (Isaiah 51:17–52:4)

- Jerusalem has already "drained to its dregs the goblet that makes people stagger." This matches Isaiah 40:2, where her sin "has been paid for."
- **The reversal:** "I have taken out of your hand the cup... I will put it into the hands of your tormentors."
- **Wake up:** "Awake, awake, Zion... Shake off your dust... You were sold for nothing, and without money you will be redeemed."
- Brent points out how casually the text mentions Assyria's oppression ("Lately, Assyria has oppressed them," or in the NET, "for no good reason"). The empire acts as if this were routine.

#### 7. The Suffering Servant (Isaiah 52:13–53:12)

- **Context matters:** The passage comes after 11 or 12 chapters about exile, the suffering of God's people, and God's purpose for the nations. It should not be read in isolation.
- **Marty's challenge:** Hear it at least once as Isaiah inviting God's people to understand *their own suffering*.
- **Read that way, the message is:**
  - Was the exile suffering wasted? No.
  - Because this generation was willing to suffer, often for what their parents did, and to suffer in a particular way (without redemptive violence), the next generation will be healed.
  - "He was pierced for our transgressions... by his wounds we are healed." The long history of Israel's afflictions is "heaped on the shoulders" of this one chapter of their history.
- **Then Jesus comes back in:** Once the passage is understood this way, Jesus becomes the *perfect fulfillment and example* of faithful suffering. He shows what it looks like to follow God and suffer well, which is the theme of Hebrews. Marty does not want to remove Jesus from the passage. He wants to read the passage on its own terms first, so that a quick jump to Jesus doesn't blur its meaning.
- **Brent's practice:** Go line by line and ask what each phrase meant during the exile. For example, what would "he was assigned a grave with the wicked" have meant to the first hearers?

#### 8. What Perseverance Produces (Isaiah 54:11–17)

- "Afflicted city, lashed by storms and not comforted, I will rebuild you with stones of turquoise..."
- The rebuilt city is better than the original. The first Jerusalem was not made of lapis lazuli and rubies. The language is pictorial, not literal, and it says that what God builds next will surpass what came before.
- **The proof-text problem:** In Western, especially American, Christianity, "no weapon forged against you will prevail" is used as a slogan for triumph. In Isaiah it follows a celebration of faithful suffering and loss, which runs against the usual American way of thinking.
- **Revelation link:** John takes this jeweled-city image further in Revelation 21.

#### 9. Relief: The Great Invitation (Isaiah 55)

- Marty calls this chapter "RELIEF." He sees it as the bridge from the suffering section to what follows.
- **Key themes:**
  - Come, buy, and eat without money. Grace is free.
  - "Why spend money on what is not bread?"
  - An everlasting covenant tied to God's faithful love for David.
  - "My thoughts are not your thoughts."
  - Like rain and snow, God's word "will not return to me empty."
  - "You will go out in joy... the trees of the field will clap their hands." Marty admits this one is stuck in his head from a summer camp song.

### Examples & Applications

- **Suffering with integrity:** Think of a family in which one generation chooses forgiveness instead of revenge after an injustice. Their children grow up in a healthier world than the parents had. Isaiah 53, read collectively, makes this point on the scale of a nation.
- **Reading out of context:** Many Christians quote "No weapon formed against me shall prosper" as a promise of personal success. Reading Isaiah 54 after Isaiah 53 changes that. The promise belongs to people who have *already* gone through suffering faithfully.
- **Losing the plot:** Churches and individuals often drift from their purpose into habit, consumerism, or self-protection. Isaiah's answer, "remember the rock you were cut from," means going back to the founding story of trust and radical hospitality.
- **Two ways to read Scripture:** Marty tries to read Isaiah 53 with his eyes instead of hearing it, because hearing it read aloud pulls him straight to Jesus. Reading a familiar passage in a new translation, or aloud with a different focus, can help you see it fresh.
- **Hope in hard times:** Isaiah 50's question, "When I called, why was there no one to answer?", can be put to anyone in a long season of hardship: are you still expecting God to show up?
- **The rebuilt city:** Recovery after a disaster, like a town rebuilt after a fire or flood, sometimes leaves the place stronger than before. This is a small picture of Isaiah 54's jeweled Jerusalem.

### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

- **The four Servant Songs:** Compare Isaiah 42:1–9, 49:1–13, 50:4–11, and 52:13–53:12. Trace how the servant's identity seems to shift.
- **Jewish interpretation of Isaiah 53:** Read rabbinic commentators such as Rashi and Ibn Ezra on the servant as Israel, and compare them with early Christian readings (Acts 8:26–35).
- **How Isaiah is divided:** Study the scholarly debate over First, Second, Third, and (in Marty's terms) "Fourth" Isaiah, and how dating affects interpretation.
- **Hebrew word study:** *Iyyim* (islands, coastlands) and its first use in Genesis 10. *Goyim* (nations). *Paraclete* in John 14–16 and its background in Isaiah.
- **The cup of wrath:** Follow the image through Jeremiah 25, Psalm 75, Habakkuk 2, Jesus's prayer in Gethsemane ("take this cup"), and Revelation 14–16.
- **Hebrews and suffering well:** Study how Hebrews 2, 5, and 12 present Jesus as the pioneer who was made perfect through suffering.
- **Revelation 21 and Isaiah 54:** Compare the jeweled foundations of the New Jerusalem with Isaiah's restored city.
- **Abraham and radical hospitality:** Revisit Genesis 18 and Abraham's place in Israel's identity.

### Comprehension Questions

1. Why do some Jewish interpreters think the servant in Isaiah 49:5–6 is Isaiah himself, and what in the text supports that reading?
2. In Isaiah 50:1–3, how does God combine accountability for Israel's sin with an invitation to hope?
3. What does it mean that Jerusalem has "drained to its dregs" the cup of wrath, and what does God do with the cup next (Isaiah 51:17–23)?
4. How does reading Isaiah 52:13–53:12 as a description of Israel's exile change your understanding of the passage, and how can that reading deepen your view of Jesus?
5. Why does Marty say "no weapon forged against you will prevail" is often misused, and how does its place after Isaiah 53 change its meaning?

### Personal Summary

This episode asks you to read one of the best-known passages in the Bible in an unfamiliar way. Before seeing Jesus in Isaiah 53, try hearing it as Israel in exile would have. They were a people who had drunk God's cup of wrath, many for sins their parents committed, and they were called to suffer faithfully instead of turning to violence or despair. Isaiah says that suffering is not wasted. It produces healing for the next generation, a rebuilt city more beautiful than the first, and an open invitation for the thirsty to come and eat without paying. Read this way, Jesus does not lose his place in the passage. He becomes its clearest example, the one who shows what persevering faithfully looks like. The practical challenge is to remember where you came from, to keep expecting God to answer, and to trust that God's word, like rain and snow, will not come back empty.
