### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** 366, Let the Man Cook  
**Hosts:** Marty Solomon and Brent Billings  
**Focus:** Isaiah 7–12 (Ahaz and the Syro-Ephraimite crisis, the Immanuel sign, Maher-shalal-hash-baz, "To us a child is born," the shoot from the stump of Jesse, and the songs of salvation)

Marty and Brent continue through Isaiah, moving from the reign of Uzziah straight to the reign of Ahaz. Isaiah says nothing at all about Jotham's reign. Aram (Syria) and the northern kingdom of Israel (Ephraim) join forces against Jerusalem, and Ahaz panics. Through Isaiah, God gives him a simple but very hard invitation: trust the story and do nothing. The hosts read the famous "virgin will conceive" passage (Isaiah 7:14) in its original historical setting, and argue that the "young woman" is most likely Isaiah's own wife and that the sign is really a timeline for coming judgment. They separate this from the openly messianic visions of Isaiah 9 and 11. Those visions picture one faithful leader whose trust in God could bring *shalom*. They were partly fulfilled in kings like Hezekiah and more fully in Jesus. Along the way the episode deals with fear and conspiracy thinking, the Bible's joining of personal morality with social justice, and how Isaiah keeps hope alive in the middle of judgment.

### Key Takeaways

- **Trust the story, with no middle ground.** God tells Ahaz to stay calm and do nothing: "If you do not stand firm in your faith, you will not stand at all" (Isaiah 7:9). There's no way to hedge. Ahaz either trusts or he doesn't.
- **Ahaz's fear was real.** He faced a genuine military threat. The hosts warn against judging biblical kings too easily from a safe distance.
- **God offers to meet Ahaz in his doubt.** God invites Ahaz to ask for a sign, which recalls Gideon's fleece. Ahaz's pious-sounding refusal ("I will not put the Lord to the test") probably hides a reluctance to get an answer that would force him to trust.
- **Read Isaiah 7:14 in its own context first.** The Hebrew word *almah* means "young woman," not specifically "virgin." The sign is mainly about *timing*: before a child born now is grown, the two kings Ahaz fears will be ruined and Assyria will be at the door.
- **Isaiah 8 seems to fulfill the sign right away.** Isaiah's wife, "the prophetess," conceives and bears Maher-shalal-hash-baz, whose birth carries the same "before the boy knows…" timeline. Marty thinks this settles who the young woman of Isaiah 7 is.
- **Don't fear what the people fear.** Isaiah 8:12–13 warns against calling everything a conspiracy. Marty applies this pointedly to evangelical culture's openness to conspiracy theories driven by fear.
- **Isaiah 9 and 11 are messianic in Isaiah's own setting.** They picture a single faithful king who could change everything. Hezekiah partly fulfilled them, and the Gospels see a fuller fulfillment in Jesus.
- **Justice joins personal and social responsibility.** Isaiah 10 shows that the Bible doesn't split "personal morality" (often associated with conservatives) from "social justice" (often associated with progressives). Right personal values naturally show up as justice for the poor and vulnerable.
- **Hope runs through the prophets.** Isaiah never softens judgment, but he keeps offering pictures of *shalom* to push back against cynicism and despair.
- **"Let the man cook."** The episode title borrows a social-media phrase. God was asking Ahaz to step back and let God work.

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. The Historical Setting: Ahaz and the Syro-Ephraimite Crisis (Isaiah 7:1–2)

- **Kings covered:** Isaiah 1:1 names four kings: Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah. Brent points out that Isaiah goes straight from Uzziah's death (Isaiah 6) to Ahaz (Isaiah 7) and gives Jotham no coverage at all.
- **The threat:** Rezin, king of Aram (capital Damascus), and Pekah son of Remaliah, king of Israel/Ephraim (capital Samaria), each attacked Judah and couldn't take Jerusalem. Now they have allied *together*.
- **The plan:** Invade Judah, divide it between them, and put a puppet ruler, "the son of Tabeel," on the throne.
- **The reaction:** "The hearts of Ahaz and his people were shaken as the trees of the forest are shaken by the wind."
- **The extra sting:** Israel supposedly worships the same God as Judah, yet it has allied with a pagan nation against its own brothers.

#### 2. The Invitation to Trust (Isaiah 7:3–9)

- **Shear-jashub:** Isaiah brings his son, whose name means "a remnant shall return." Prophets often gave their children symbolic names. The name shows that Isaiah himself was living by God's promises.
- **The message:** "Be careful, keep calm and don't be afraid." Rezin and Pekah are "two smoldering stubs of firewood." They burned bright and fierce, but now they're spent.
- **A hard request:** God wasn't asking Ahaz to fight better. He was asking him *not to act*: no defensive alliance, no battle, just trust.
- **Only two options:** "If you do not stand firm in your faith, you will not stand at all." The Hebrew wordplay ties *believing* (being made firm) to *being established*. Faith is the only foundation there is.

#### 3. The Offered Sign and Ahaz's Refusal (Isaiah 7:10–13)

- **Literary note:** The phrase "Again the Lord spoke to Ahaz" (v. 10) could mark a pause. Ahaz may have stood there silent, unconvinced. Marty holds this idea loosely, since prophetic and poetic Hebrew can follow different conventions from narrative.
- **God's offer:** "Ask the Lord your God for a sign, whether in the deepest depths or in the highest heights."
- **Testing God vs. being reassured by God:**
  - In the wilderness, Israel *tested* God by demanding proof before they would obey.
  - Here, God *asks* Ahaz to trust and *offers* proof to help him do it.
  - So Ahaz's refusal isn't humble piety. It's avoidance. He probably suspects God will come through, and then he would have no excuse not to trust.
- Isaiah gets frustrated: "Is it not enough to try the patience of humans? Will you try the patience of my God also?"

#### 4. The Immanuel Sign in Context (Isaiah 7:14–17)

- **The text:** "The virgin will conceive and give birth to a son, and will call him Immanuel" ("God with us").
- **Robert Alter's note:** The Hebrew *almah* means "young woman," not technically "virgin." In Proverbs, an *almah* is even described in a sexual context. The sign lies in the child's *name*. Who the woman is has been widely debated. She may be the prophet's wife or a woman of the royal house.
- **The Septuagint shift:** The Greek translation (the Septuagint) used *parthenos*, a word for virgin, which made the meaning narrower. That is the version Matthew quotes. Brent suggests the translators may have wanted to stress how remarkable the sign was, since God gave it unasked.
- **What the sign means in context:**
  - It's a *timeline*. Before a child conceived now is old enough to choose right from wrong, the lands of the two feared kings will be laid waste.
  - It's also a *warning*. God will bring the king of Assyria, "a time unlike any since Ephraim broke away from Judah."
  - Marty connects it to Jesus' "sign of Jonah." You refused to ask for a sign, so here is the one you'll get: destruction coming within a decade.
- **Marty's hermeneutical point:** This isn't taking anything away from Jesus. The Gospels make their own connections for their own reasons. But readers who jump straight to Christmas miss what God was saying to Ahaz in 735 BC or so.

#### 5. Maher-shalal-hash-baz: The Sign Fulfilled (Isaiah 8:1–4)

- Isaiah writes "Maher-shalal-hash-baz" on a large scroll or tablet in front of reliable witnesses (Uriah the priest and Zechariah son of Jeberekiah).
- He sleeps with "the prophetess," his wife, and she conceives a son.
- **The name means** "quick to the plunder, swift to the spoil." Marty jokes that Shear-jashub got the better name.
- **The timeline repeats:** "Before the boy knows how to say 'My father' or 'My mother,' the wealth of Damascus and the plunder of Samaria will be carried off by the king of Assyria."
- **Marty's conclusion:** Two paragraphs after the Immanuel sign, the text describes a young woman conceiving with the same time frame. To him, the young woman of Isaiah 7 is clearly Isaiah's wife.
- **Possible intertextual echoes (still open questions):**
  - Brent notes that the Hebrew word for "pen" or "stylus" here appears in only one other place in the Tanakh: Exodus 32, where Aaron fashions the golden calf with an engraving tool.
  - Marty wonders about a link to Zechariah writing John's name on a tablet in Luke 1.

#### 6. Rejecting the Gentle Waters (Isaiah 8:5–10)

- **The image:** The people rejected "the gently flowing waters of Shiloah," Jerusalem's quiet water supply and a symbol of God's steady provision. So God will bring "the mighty flood waters of the Euphrates," the king of Assyria.
- The flood will reach "up to the neck" of Judah. Judah will be nearly overwhelmed, but not destroyed.
- **"Immanuel" returns** (v. 8, 10). The land belongs to Immanuel, and the nations' plans will fail "for God is with us."
- **Deliberate repetition:** "Prepare for battle and be shattered" appears twice. Brent says it isn't a reading mistake. The repetition is there on purpose and readers should notice it.

#### 7. Don't Fear What They Fear (Isaiah 8:11–15)

- "Do not call conspiracy everything this people calls a conspiracy; do not fear what they fear… The Lord Almighty is the one you are to regard as holy… he is the one you are to fear."
- **Marty's application:** Evangelicals have become known for believing almost any conspiracy theory, usually driven by social and political fear. Isaiah calls God's people to put their security in God, not in fear-driven, panicked thinking, which he calls "what the pagans do."
- **A stone of stumbling for both kingdoms:** God will be a sanctuary *and* a stumbling stone for Israel and Judah. Even for the northern kingdom that is about to be destroyed, there is always a remnant and always a shoot from the stump for anyone who trusts.

#### 8. Prophetic Solidarity and Translation Choices (Isaiah 8:16–22)

- **Poetry vs. prose:** Translations format verses 16–17 differently.
  - The NIV indents them as poetry, part of the prophecy.
  - Robert Alter and the ESV treat them as prose, which makes them read like Isaiah's own personal resolution.
- **Alter's reading:** "Bind up the testimony… I will wait for the Lord… I will put my trust in him." Here Isaiah steps back, seals up his message, and waits to see what God will do.
- **"Here am I, and the children the Lord has given me. We are signs and symbols in Israel"** (v. 18).
- **The Heschel connection:** As discussed in the series' introductory episodes, Abraham Joshua Heschel described the prophet as standing *in solidarity* with the people, not pointing a finger from outside. Isaiah counts himself and his children among the people.
- **Warning against mediums:** "Why consult the dead on behalf of the living?" People who turn away from God's instruction end up in "utter darkness."

#### 9. "To Us a Child Is Born": An Openly Messianic Vision (Isaiah 9:1–7)

- **Fluid chapter breaks:** In the Hebrew text, Isaiah 9:1 is numbered as 8:23. Chapter divisions came later and can hide how continuous the text is.
- **Hope for the north:** Zebulun and Naphtali, the Galilee region, were humbled first by Assyria and will be honored first. The hope reaches beyond Judah to Israel too.
- **The images:**
  - "The people walking in darkness have seen a great light."
  - Joy like harvest and like warriors dividing plunder. Yet every warrior's boot and blood-soaked garment becomes fuel for the fire. Brent loves this reversal: war gear is no longer needed.
  - "Wonderful Counselor, Mighty God, Everlasting Father, Prince of Peace."
- **Messianic in Isaiah's own setting:** Unlike 7:14, this passage looks for *a person*, one king who trusts the story and leads his people so that things are "on earth as it is in heaven."
- **Layered fulfillment:**
  1. **Partial, in Isaiah's day:** Hezekiah's reforms reached from Dan in the far north to Beersheba in the south.
  2. **Incomplete:** Hezekiah's reforms didn't last.
  3. **Fuller, in Jesus:** The Gospel writers are saying, "Remember Hezekiah? Remember how that obedience fell short? Here is an even better obedience and a fuller kingdom."
- **The principle:** Isaiah wasn't wrong and wasn't thinking about Jesus. He spoke to his own people in his own time. Seeing the original meaning first makes the Gospels' use of it make more sense.

#### 10. Woe to Unjust Lawmakers and to Assyria (Isaiah 10)

- **"Woe to those who make unjust laws… to deprive the poor of their rights… making widows their prey."**
- **"Where will you leave your riches?"** The Hebrew word is *kavod*: weight, heaviness, glory. Isaiah isn't just talking about money. He's talking about the thing that makes a person feel significant. The question is whether we build our own name and glory or join ourselves to God's name, which connects to *kiddush HaShem* (sanctifying God's name) from Session Two.
- **Assyria as God's rod:** God uses Assyria as "the rod of my anger," but Assyria's own aim is conquest for its own sake, and it will be judged in turn.
- **Joining the two worldviews:**
  - Modern Western thinking splits *individual responsibility* (often conservative) from *social responsibility* (often progressive).
  - The world of the Bible knows nothing of this split.
  - An individual who truly lives out God's morality *will* pursue justice for the poor, the widow, the orphan, and the foreigner.
  - Each side has part of the truth and needs the other to see God's kingdom fully. Marty admits the phrase "social justice" provokes strong reactions, and says that reaction itself shows why the both/and message is hard to hear.

#### 11. The Shoot from the Stump of Jesse (Isaiah 11:1–9)

- **Netzer:** A *netzer* (shoot or branch) comes up from the stump of Jesse, David's father. Earlier in the series the image was tied to a pruned olive tree. Brent suspects it's the same Hebrew word.
- **Individual focus:** The earlier shoot imagery felt communal. This one points to a single leader filled with the Spirit of wisdom (*chokmah*), understanding (*binah*), counsel, might, knowledge, and the fear of the Lord.
- **His rule:** He doesn't judge by appearances. He judges the needy with righteousness and the poor with justice.
- **The peaceable kingdom:** The wolf lives with the lamb, the lion eats straw like the ox, and a child plays near a cobra's den. Marty reads this as hyperbole that shows complete *shalom*: what creation looks like when justice is pursued, with death itself undone. It looks ahead to Isaiah's later "new heavens and new earth."

#### 12. A World Without Obstacles (Isaiah 11:10–16)

- **The root of Jesse:** The root (*shoresh*) becomes a banner for the nations.
- **Regathering the exiles:** God gathers his people from Assyria, Egypt, Cush, Elam, Babylonia, Hamath, and the islands.
- **Reconciliation:** Ephraim and Judah stop being jealous of and hostile toward each other.
- **A highway:** God dries up the Egyptian gulf and splits the Euphrates into streams people can cross in sandals, a highway like the Exodus road.
- **How to read it:** These are pictures, not literal predictions. God is building a world *without obstacles*, where today's enemies can move freely between each other's lands.

#### 13. Songs of Salvation (Isaiah 12)

- The chapter break hides how directly this follows chapter 11.
- "Surely God is my salvation; I will trust and not be afraid." Notice that Isaiah 12 uses the same trust-and-don't-fear language God offered Ahaz in chapter 7.
- "With joy you will draw water from the wells of salvation." Worship grows out of experiencing *shalom*.
- "Make known among the nations what he has done." The good news is meant for the whole world.

#### 14. Hope Against Cynicism

- Isaiah doesn't play down sin, judgment, or God's pruning. "He pulls no punches."
- He also never loses hope in what God is doing.
- The prophet shares God's anger *and* God's hope.
- Marty admits he is often tempted toward cynicism about the church. Isaiah reminds him: "God will win. Shalom is where this is all headed… God is putting the world back together. God is asking us to join him."

### Examples & Applications

- **"Let the man cook":** The phrase comes from TikTok and similar videos where someone is in the middle of doing something remarkable. God was telling Ahaz, "Don't interfere. Let me do what I'm doing." Today, that might mean resisting the urge to grab control during a crisis at work, a health scare, or a family conflict, and practicing patient trust instead.
- **Two smoldering stubs:** Things we fear often look bigger than they are. A rival, a political opponent, or a scary headline may have already burned out. Isaiah invites us to see threats as God sees them.
- **Accepting God's reassurance:** Ahaz refused help because he didn't want the responsibility that comes with certainty. People do the same thing today, avoiding prayer, Scripture, or wise advice because they suspect they already know what it will tell them to do.
- **Conspiracy culture:** Before sharing a sensational claim or giving in to political panic, Isaiah 8:12–13 asks a question: Am I fearing what the crowd fears, or am I fearing God?
- **Reading Christmas texts well:** When Isaiah 7:14 or 9:6 is read during Advent, start with Isaiah's setting: Ahaz, Assyria, Hezekiah. Then ask why the Gospel writers saw Jesus as the fuller fulfillment. This makes Christmas texts richer, not weaker.
- **Joining personal faith and justice:** A church can teach personal holiness and also stand up for fair housing, fair wages, or care for refugees, treating these as one calling instead of competing agendas.
- **Prophetic solidarity:** Leaders, pastors, and activists can follow Isaiah by speaking hard truths from *inside* their community ("Here am I, and the children the Lord has given me") rather than condemning from outside.
- **Hope as discipline:** When following church scandals or world news feels like a slide into despair, the pattern of Isaiah 11–12 helps: name the brokenness honestly, then deliberately remind yourself where God's story is going.

### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

1. **The Syro-Ephraimite War:** Compare Isaiah 7 with 2 Kings 16 and 2 Chronicles 28. Ahaz ultimately sent tribute to Tiglath-Pileser III of Assyria, the very self-protection Isaiah warned against.
2. ***Almah* vs. *parthenos*:** Study how the Septuagint translated Isaiah 7:14 and how Matthew 1:22–23 uses it. How did first-century Jewish readers understand "fulfillment"?
3. **Symbolic names in the prophets:** Compare Shear-jashub and Maher-shalal-hash-baz with Hosea's children (Jezreel, Lo-Ruhamah, Lo-Ammi).
4. **The stylus/pen connection:** Look into the rare Hebrew word in Isaiah 8:1 and its only other use in Exodus 32. Is there an intended echo of the golden calf?
5. **Translation formatting:** Compare how the NIV, ESV, NET, and Robert Alter lay out poetry and prose in Isaiah 8, and how that changes the meaning.
6. **Hezekiah's reforms:** Study 2 Kings 18–20 and 2 Chronicles 29–32 to weigh how far Hezekiah partly fulfilled Isaiah 9 and 11.
7. ***Netzer* and Nazareth:** Explore the link between the "branch" of Isaiah 11:1 and Matthew 2:23 ("He will be called a Nazarene").
8. ***Kavod* across Scripture:** Follow "weight/glory" from Pharaoh's heavy heart, to the glory filling the tabernacle, to Isaiah 10.
9. **Abraham Joshua Heschel's *The Prophets*:** Read his treatment of prophetic pathos and solidarity.
10. **The peaceable kingdom:** Compare Isaiah 11:6–9 with Isaiah 65:17–25 and Romans 8:19–22 on the renewal of creation.
11. **Biblical justice:** Study the joining of personal righteousness (*tzedakah*) and justice (*mishpat*) across the prophets (Amos 5, Micah 6:8, Jeremiah 22).

### Summary

This episode shows that the hardest kind of faith is sometimes doing nothing. Surrounded by two hostile kings, Ahaz hears God say: keep calm, trust the story, and "let the man cook." Marty and Brent place the famous Immanuel sign back in its setting. It was a timeline of coming judgment, and it may have been fulfilled almost right away in the birth of Isaiah's son, Maher-shalal-hash-baz. They separate it from the openly messianic hope of Isaiah 9 and 11, which pictures a faithful king whose trust in God could bring *shalom* to Judah and Israel alike. Hezekiah partly fulfilled that hope, and Jesus fulfilled it more fully. Along the way, Isaiah warns against conspiracy-driven fear, holds personal morality and social justice together as one calling, and stands with his people instead of condemning them from outside. Above all, Isaiah keeps hope alive. Judgment is real, but where the story is going is a world without obstacles, where the wolf lies down with the lamb and God's people draw water joyfully from the wells of salvation. For anyone tempted by cynicism, these chapters say God is still at work, and they invite us to trust him while he does it.
