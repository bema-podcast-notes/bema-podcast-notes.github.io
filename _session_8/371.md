### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** 371 – The Hulk Factor  
**Hosts:** Marty Solomon and Brent Billings  
**Focus:** Isaiah 34–35

The previous episode ended on a hopeful note, with God inviting his covenant people to choose allegiance to his kingdom. Turn one page and Isaiah is back to the hard reality that people, including us, keep saying no. In this episode Marty and Brent look at the reality of God's anger, using the Hulk's line "You won't like me when I'm angry" as a hook. Isaiah 34 widens the audience to every nation and its armies and describes devastating judgment, focused on Edom. Isaiah 35 then shows the "flip side of anger": the desert blooms, the exiles are encouraged, and the redeemed return to Zion singing. The hosts argue that God's anger is not blind rage. It comes from his love for shalom, it is aroused by injustice, and it aims at restoration. They also point out how heavily these chapters echo Genesis and Exodus. The episode ends with Marty giving an unplanned review of Isaiah 1–39, summed up as "a father disciplining his sons."

### Key Takeaways

- **God's anger comes from injustice, not wounded pride.** Like the Torah warnings about harming widows and foreigners, God's anger is roused by the cry of the oppressed and the tearing of shalom.
- **There are no favorites in Isaiah 34.** God's wrath falls on "all nations" and "all their armies." It condemns the whole world's commitment to the myth of redemptive violence, not one side of a conflict.
- **Violence destroys itself.** God "gives them over to slaughter," which can be read as nations destroying one another through their own violence (a reading in line with Greg Boyd's *Cross Vision*).
- **Isaiah 34 reverses creation.** The phrase *tohu va-vohu* ("formless and void," Genesis 1:2) appears in Isaiah 34:11. Edom is returned to primordial chaos, the opposite of new creation.
- **Isaiah 35 recalls the Exodus.** Water in the wilderness, a highway home, and a rescued people returning to Zion all echo Israel's journey out of Egypt.
- **God's anger is good news for the oppressed.** For people who suffer abuse or misused power, a God who gets angry is a God who cares and will act.
- **Privilege can flatten our theology.** Marty admits that a comfortable life led him to see God's anger in a one-dimensional way. Deconstructing distorted views of wrath is good, but throwing out God's anger altogether would be a serious overcorrection.
- **Isaiah 1–39 as a father disciplining his sons:** loving and merciful, but out of patience because his children are hurting other people and his creation.

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. "You Won't Like Me When I'm Angry": Where God's Anger Comes From

Marty roots the discussion in Torah texts such as Exodus 22:21–27. There God warns Israel not to mistreat the widow, the foreigner, or the poor, and not to keep a poor person's cloak as a pledge overnight, because "when they cry out to me, I will hear."

- God's anger is **relational and protective**. It rises up on behalf of the powerless.
- It starts from **shalom**, the wholeness, peace, and right order God intends for creation.
- It is **not** set off simply by people breaking rules. It responds to the harm that disobedience does to people and to creation.

#### 2. A Global Indictment (Isaiah 34:1–4)

The passage opens with a summons to the whole earth: "Come near, you nations, and listen."

- **Scope:** all nations, all peoples, "the world and all that comes out of it."
- **Target:** "His wrath is on all their armies." Brent notes that this narrows the focus. The guilt lies with the machinery of violence, not with every individual.
- **Cosmic imagery:** the heavens are "rolled up like a scroll" and the stars fall like withered leaves. This is the language of creation coming undone.
- **Today's world:** the hosts recorded during the conflicts in Israel and Gaza and in Ukraine and Russia, amid tensions over Taiwan. The passage challenges the assumption that God has favorite nations he backs in war.

#### 3. Heschel: "Few Are Guilty, All Are Responsible"

Marty returns to Abraham Joshua Heschel's principle:

- **Guilt** belongs to those who actually commit the violence.
- **Responsibility** belongs to everyone. Each of us has to decide which story we will give ourselves to and how we will respond to the world's violence.

#### 4. Edom and Undoing Creation (Isaiah 34:5–17)

The focus narrows to **Edom**, especially **Bozrah** (pronounced "Basra" in the episode), its main city.

- **Sacrificial imagery:** God's sword is "bathed in blood" and "covered with fat," and the slaughter is called a "sacrifice." The language is meant to shock.
- **Connection to Esau:** Edom descends from Esau, Jacob's brother. That ties the passage back to the family conflicts of Genesis.
- **Tohu va-vohu:** Robert Alter's notes on verse 11 ("a line of welter, the weightstones of waste") point out the deliberate echo of Genesis 1:2. The NIV renders it "the measuring line of chaos and the plumb line of desolation."
  - Measuring lines and plumb lines are building tools. Here they are used to **build destruction**, returning the land to its pre-creation state.
  - The point: the world's way of solving problems does not move toward Eden. It moves back toward chaos.
- **Wild creatures take over:** owls, jackals, hyenas, and ravens inhabit the ruins, and thorns overrun the strongholds. Human kingdoms fade and wilderness returns. (Brent jokes about the NIV's repeated footnotes saying the bird identifications are uncertain.)
- **Echoes of Noah (34:16–17):** "None of these will be missing, not one will lack her mate." The animals gathered in pairs recall the ark. Marty notes that hearing the text read aloud brought out connections he had missed when reading silently.

#### 5. The Flip Side of Anger (Isaiah 35)

Isaiah 35 is the other side of the same coin:

- **The desert blooms** (35:1–2) "like the crocus," and the glory of Lebanon, Carmel, and Sharon is given to it. Brent connects this to the flood: after the waters comes new life.
- **Encouragement for the weak** (35:3–4): "Strengthen the feeble hands… Your God will come… with vengeance… he will come to save you." Here vengeance and salvation come in the same breath.
- **Healing** (35:5–6): the blind see, the deaf hear, the lame leap, the mute shout for joy.
- **Water in the wilderness** (35:6–7): streams in the desert and springs where jackals once lay.
- **The Way of Holiness** (35:8–10): a highway for the redeemed. No ravenous beast walks it, and the ransomed return to Zion with everlasting joy.

#### 6. Who Was Isaiah 35 Written For?

Alter's footnote on 35:3 says the call to take heart "speaks to the condition of exiles."

- That suggests the original listeners were **exiles**, whether from Israel after Assyria or from Judah after Babylon.
- This touches on the question of **dating and authorship in Isaiah**, which the next episode takes up.
- For exiles, "God is coming with vengeance" is comfort, not a threat.

#### 7. From Genesis to Exodus

The Jewish Publication Society (JPS) notes highlight the **Exodus parallels** in Isaiah 35:

| Isaiah 34 | Isaiah 35 |
|---|---|
| Genesis echoes: *tohu va-vohu*, Esau/Edom, the flood and the ark | Exodus echoes: wilderness, water from the desert, a highway home, liberation |
| Creation unraveling into chaos | God leading his people out into something new |

Marty wonders whether the pattern continues into Leviticus and the rest of Torah, and leaves that question as homework.

#### 8. Where Isaiah Is Headed

- Chapters 34–35 close what scholars often call **First Isaiah** (the oracles of chapters 1–35).
- Chapters 36–39 are a **historical bridge**, largely parallel to 2 Kings, linking First and Second Isaiah. Marty calls these the redaction sections.
- Brent jokes that "poetic prose" is an oxymoron, since prose is by definition not verse.

#### 9. Review of Isaiah 1–39: "A Father Disciplining His Sons"

Marty's unplanned review of Isaiah so far:

1. **Opening provocations (ch. 1–12):** "You rulers of Sodom… I hate your worship." Isaiah opens with a hook. Isaiah confronts kings like Ahaz: it takes only one child, one messianic figure, choosing God's way.
2. **Burdens on the nations (ch. 13–23):** it is falling apart everywhere, in Moab, Ammon, Edom, Philistia, Damascus, Tyre, Egypt, Babylon, Israel, and Judah.
3. **It is falling apart with you too, my people:** pride and arrogance blind God's covenant people.
4. **The invitation:** come home and choose where your allegiance lies, because the story is heading somewhere beautiful.
5. **When the answer is no (ch. 34–35):** God's anger moves to put the world back together. This leads into Second Isaiah: "I'll see you in exile, but I'm not forsaking you."

**The road trip image:** a parent driving to Disneyland tells the kids that if they don't stop hitting each other, "I'm going to pull this van over." The destination doesn't change, but behavior decides how the trip goes.

### Examples & Applications

- **Torah protections for the vulnerable:** Exodus 22 grounds God's anger in concern for widows, orphans, and foreigners. Today that could mean caring about workers who are exploited, refugees, or abuse survivors whose cries are ignored.
- **Modern wars:** Isaiah 34 asks us to stop casting God as a supporter of "our side" in any conflict and to grieve all violence carried out by "all their armies."
- **The survivor's perspective:** Marty describes how people who have been on the receiving end of abuse showed him that a God who gets angry is a God who sees them. Hearing voices from outside our own social position can correct blind spots.
- **Jonah's complaint:** Jonah was frustrated that God seemed too forgiving. That shows the real tension people feel when God's mercy appears to delay justice.
- **Hearing Scripture aloud:** Marty only caught the Noah echoes when he heard Brent read. Reading the text aloud, especially in a group, can surface connections that silent reading misses.
- **Careful study tools:** the hosts use Robert Alter's translation and *The Jewish Study Bible* (2nd edition, Oxford, with JPS notes) to see literary connections that standard English translations can hide.
- **Two ways to misread the prophets:** Marty warns that "intellectualizers" should let the text change them, not only inform them. "Internalizers" should remember that Isaiah was not written to them personally and has its own historical setting.

### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

- **Burdens on the nations:** go back to Episode 367 and compare what other prophets (Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Amos, Obadiah) say about the same nations.
- **Torah parallels in Isaiah:** keep reading past chapter 35 to see whether the Genesis and Exodus echoes continue into Leviticus, Numbers, and Deuteronomy.
- **Tohu va-vohu elsewhere:** Marty recalls the phrase appearing in full somewhere else in the prophets. (Hint: try Jeremiah 4:23.)
- **Edom in the prophets:** study Obadiah, Ezekiel 35, and Isaiah 63:1–6 ("Who is this coming from Edom… from Bozrah?") to see why Edom stands for hostile nations.
- **Greg Boyd's *The Crucifixion of the Warrior God* / *Cross Vision*:** how violent divine imagery can be read through the cross.
- **Heschel's *The Prophets*:** especially his idea of divine pathos, God's emotional involvement in human affairs.
- **Authorship and redaction of Isaiah:** the "First, Second, and Third Isaiah" theory and the role of chapters 36–39, which the next episode covers with a guest.
- **The myth of redemptive violence:** Walter Wink's critique, and how it relates to Isaiah's indictment of the nations' armies.

### Comprehension Questions

1. According to Marty, what is the source of God's anger, and how do Torah texts about widows and foreigners support that view?
2. Isaiah 34 begins by addressing "all nations" and "all their armies." What does this universal scope say about whether God takes sides in human conflicts?
3. What is the significance of the phrase *tohu va-vohu* in Isaiah 34:11, and how does using building tools (measuring line, plumb line) for destruction add to its meaning?
4. How does Isaiah 35 show the "flip side" of God's anger, and what Exodus imagery do the JPS notes point out in the chapter?
5. Why did Robert Alter's note about an exilic audience change how Marty thinks about divine anger, and why can God's anger be good news for those who suffer injustice?

### Personalized Summary

This episode asks you to rethink a part of God's character that is easy to avoid or distort. Isaiah 34 is hard to read: it describes cosmic upheaval, blood-soaked sacrifice imagery, and Edom collapsing back into the formless chaos of Genesis 1. But it is not a picture of an irritable deity losing his temper. It is a verdict on the world's addiction to violence. Every nation and every army is included, and the result of our choices is creation coming undone. Isaiah 35 then shows where God's anger is aimed: deserts in bloom, the disabled healed, streams in the wilderness, and a highway home for the redeemed. Anger and restoration belong to the same story.

The main challenge is about perspective. If your life has been comfortable, God's wrath may seem like something to explain away. If you have been the one crying out under injustice, a God who does not get angry would be no comfort at all. Isaiah 1–39 ends up as the story of a loving father disciplining his children because they are hurting each other and his good world. He has not given up on the destination. He is warning that the way there matters.
