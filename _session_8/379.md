### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** BEMA Episode 379: An Introduction to the Psalms  
**Hosts:** Marty Solomon and Brent Billings, with guest host Reed Dent  
**Focus:** The Book of Psalms: an introduction to Hebrew poetry, the role of the Psalms in faith and theology, and ways of grouping the Psalms (Proverbs 29:11; Psalms 8, 13, 51, 137, 139)

Reed Dent starts a new series on the Psalms by asking Marty and Brent why they find this part of the Bible hard to connect with. That leads into a discussion of what poetry is, why Hebrew poetry is so hard to translate, and why the Psalms should not be mined for proof texts. Reed argues that the Psalms give us words for every human experience: grief, doubt and anger, but also hope, praise and joy, which can be harder to say in a cynical age. He then offers two ways of grouping the Psalms. The first borrows Anne Lamott's "Help, Thanks, Wow" and adds lament, trust, critique and reflection. The second is Walter Brueggemann's framework of orientation, disorientation and new orientation, which will guide the rest of the series. The main point is that God is the right conversation partner in every season of life.

### Key Takeaways

- **The Psalms are poetry from start to finish.** Apart from a few headings and instructions, the book is verse and song. They work by stirring up feeling and expressing it, not by explaining or arguing.
- **Good poetry puts "greater pressure per word."** It is not just decoration. It uses words in surprising, compact ways that make "finely tuned adjustments at deep and critical places in our imagination" (Thomas Long, quoting Laurence Perrine).
- **Don't turn single lines of a Psalm into doctrine.** Psalm 51:5 ("I was sinful at birth") and Psalm 139:14 ("I am fearfully and wonderfully made") are honest expressions from different moments of life, not competing doctrinal claims.
- **The Psalms are as much anthropology as theology.** Their first-person voice ("I," "me," "my") shows us the human experience of God and helps us understand ourselves.
- **The Psalms give us words for what we struggle to say.** That includes feeling God is absent ("How long, O Lord?"), and in a cynical culture it equally includes joy and praise ("The whole earth is full of his glory").
- **"Setting aside decorum for the sake of honesty."** The Psalms invite us to "speak what we feel, not what we ought to say" (King Lear). God already knows what is going on inside us.
- **There is no nihilism in the Psalms.** Even in deep anguish, God stays at the center of the conversation. Suffering pushes the psalmist toward God, not away.
- **Brueggemann's framework (orientation, disorientation, new orientation)** maps the Psalms onto the seasons of life. It reminds us that it is "okay to be okay."
- **God is the right conversation partner in every season**, whether things are good, falling apart, or newly restored.
- **No categories capture the Psalms neatly.** They form "a really big, messy Venn diagram," just as human life does.

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. Why the Psalms Are Hard for Some Readers

- Marty admits the Psalms are the genre he finds hardest to connect with, even though he loves music. He thinks his fundamentalist evangelical upbringing, which treated exegesis (detailed analysis of the text) as "king," pushes him to dissect rather than experience.
- Brent feels the same. Once he opens his Bible, he goes into "study mode." He notes that **the Psalms are meant more to be experienced than studied**, and that studying them on a podcast is a bit like "dissecting a swallow."
- Reed describes himself as a "Proverbs and Ecclesiastes kind of guy." As a teenager he took Proverbs 29:11 ("Fools give full vent to their rage") as a reason to always stay rational and calm. So Psalms like Psalm 137, with its violent cry for revenge, made him uncomfortable.
- Through college apologetics he became "a brain in a jar," cut off from his heart. Reading poetry helped reconnect him with his emotions and opened up the Psalms.

#### 2. Translation and the Problem of Hebrew Poetry

- **Robert Alter's observation:** The King James Version had "an excellent grasp of English and a terrible grasp of Hebrew." Modern translations have flipped that: excellent Hebrew, weak English.
- Alter's 25-year translation project tries to stay true to the Hebrew's vocabulary *and* its syntax, rhythm and meter.
- **Comparing translations of Proverbs 29:11:**
  1. NIV: "Fools give full vent to their rage, but the wise bring calm in the end."
  2. NET: "A fool lets fly with all his temper, but a wise person keeps it back."
  3. KJV: "A fool uttereth all his mind: but a wise man keepeth it in till afterwards."
  4. Alter: "The fool gives vent to his whole spirit, but the wise man quells it."
- Brent asks whether experiential texts like the Psalms call for a different translation approach. English readers expect rhyme, but Hebrew poetry relies on **parallelism** (pairing lines), juxtaposition and wordplay, which rarely survive translation.
- Interest in the Psalms *as poetry* is fairly recent. Brent notes that the first academic study of biblical poetry dates only to the 1750s (likely Robert Lowth's lectures on Hebrew poetry).

#### 3. What Poetry Is (and Isn't)

- **A misconception:** Poetry is just "flowery" or decorative language. Reed calls that *bad* poetry.
- **Good poetry** puts words together in unexpected ways to create new meaning.
- **Thomas G. Long** (*Preaching and the Literary Forms of the Bible*), quoting Laurence Perrine: poetry is "more condensed and concentrated," has "a higher voltage than other literary forms," and "applies greater pressure per word."
- Long goes on: "Poems change what we think and feel not by piling up facts we didn't know or by persuading us through arguments, but by making finely tuned adjustments at deep and critical places in our imagination."
- Marty calls this the **strategy** of poetry: every word is chosen on purpose. Poetry speaks to human experience at a "deep-down-in-your-bones" level through how it is packaged, not only through what it says.
- **Prose vs. poetry:** Prose is *discursive* and *propositional*: it explains and makes claims. Poetry is *evocative* and *expressive*: it creates experiences.

#### 4. Theology and the Psalms

- Marty separates **theology** in the broad sense (the study and expression of experiencing God) from **doctrine** formed by Western proof-texting. The Psalms belong in theology but fit badly with certain ways of *doing* theology.
- **The Psalm 51 vs. Psalm 139 tension:**
  - Psalm 51:5: "Surely I was sinful at birth, sinful from the time my mother conceived me." This is often used as a proof text for original sin.
  - Psalm 139:13–14: "You knit me together in my mother's womb. I praise you because I am fearfully and wonderfully made."
  - Which is "true"? The point is that each honestly expresses what the psalmist is living through. Readers gain more by letting the two speak to each other than by building doctrine from either one.
- Marty sees the contemplative and critique Psalms as plainly theological, but theology rooted in **concrete experience**, unlike the abstract method common in Western theology.

#### 5. The Psalms as a Vocabulary for the Soul

- Many Psalms grow out of specific, concrete life situations. Even reflective Psalms come from lived experience, not "pontificating."
- Reed, a campus minister, turns to the Psalms when college students have hard questions. They don't supply answers. **They supply ways to ask the questions.**
- Readers are invited to make the psalmist's "I" their own.
- Marty notes how rare this is in scripture. You can't easily find words for grief in Philippians or Exodus, and turning to the wrong part of Isaiah might only bring words of judgment.
- Narratives like Abraham and Isaac rarely tell us what the characters *felt*. The Psalms put feelings into words directly.

#### 6. Honesty About Hope in a Cynical Age

- An earlier generation needed the Psalms to give them permission to doubt and question. That is part of BEMA's own story.
- Today **cynicism comes easily**. Anything can be fact-checked in seconds, and any hopeful statement draws an instant "yeah, but."
- Reed points to the wide popularity of *Ted Lasso* as evidence that people are hungry for hope, "like water in a desert."
- So the Psalms of praise and wonder may now be the *harder* and *more necessary* voice: "The whole earth is full of his glory" (compare Psalm 72:19 and Isaiah 6:3).
- **"Setting aside decorum for the sake of honesty"**: honesty includes being honest about hope. Lament is not meant to be flippant or "hipster ironic." It means facing what is really going on inside us before a God who already knows.

#### 7. Scholarship and Personal Devotion

- **Two dangers:** ignoring scholarship altogether, or letting it "suck all the life out of" the text.
- What scholarship has offered:
  - The Psalms interact closely with, and sometimes borrow from, other ancient Near Eastern religious songs and poems. Some divine titles come from neighboring cultures.
  - Many Psalms are among the older biblical material.
  - Theories (not proofs) about their use in temple worship, festivals and coronations.
  - Alter's work on how Hebrew poetry works.
  - Frameworks for grouping Psalms and relating them to each other.
- Reed's advice: use scholarship, but "don't let it kill the Psalms for you."

#### 8. Content Categories (Building on Anne Lamott's *Help, Thanks, Wow*)

Reed also notes that you should always ask whether a Psalm is **individual** ("I/me") or **communal** ("we/us"). Americans tend to assume every Psalm is about them personally.

1. **Help (supplication):** Cries like "Answer me!", prayers for sleep (for example Psalm 4:8), and pleas about enemies. Some are **confessional**, where the psalmist faces their own sin and asks to be restored. Many also **remember God's past help**, which shows salvation as an ongoing cycle rather than a one-time fix.
2. **Thanks:** Gratitude for God's blessings.
3. **Wow (praise and wonder):** Awe at God as seen in **creation, the law (Torah), or Israel's history**.
4. **No! (lament or complaint):** "How long will you hide your face?" (Psalm 13). Marty calls it a "refusal of acceptance," like Jacob saying "I will not let you go unless you bless me" (Genesis 32:26).
5. **Trust:** Self-talk that encourages the psalmist to remember God and keep trusting, often without asking for anything.
6. **Critique:** Prophetic social observation, similar to Amos or Isaiah: "There is no one who does good" (compare Psalms 14 and 53).
7. **Reflection or contemplation:** Thinking about the nature of God or humanity: "When I consider your heavens... what is man that you are mindful of him?" (Psalm 8); "The heavens declare the glory of God" (Psalm 19); "Who is this King of glory?" (Psalm 24).

#### 9. Walter Brueggemann's Framework: Orientation, Disorientation, New Orientation

Brueggemann's categories (*The Message of the Psalms*) describe broad **modes or seasons of life** rather than specific content.

| Mode | Life Experience | Typical Psalm Types | Seasonal Image (Reed) |
|------|----------------|---------------------|----------------------|
| **Orientation** | You know where you are and where you are headed. Peace, security, well-being. | Thanksgiving, praise ("wow") | Summer evening on the porch with lemonade |
| **Disorientation** | Calamity, loss, alienation; things fall apart. Anger, resentment, sadness, self-pity. | Lament, complaint, help | Winter, when it plunges below zero |
| **New Orientation** | Joy breaks in unexpectedly. God's gifts arrive with surprise after hardship. | Thanksgiving for deliverance, confident praise | Spring: the first warm day after a long winter |

- **Orientation:** These Psalms say it is "okay to be okay." Nothing is wrong when life is simply good.
- **Disorientation:** This is not abstract doubt about God. It is the response to real suffering.
- **New orientation:** This is not just a return to the old normal. It is a changed outlook shaped by having been rescued. It echoes Romans 8:31 ("If God is for us, who can be against us?") and is pictured as the "Mario star": nothing can touch me now.
- **A Christological reading (optional):** Moving into disorientation mirrors **crucifixion**, and moving into new orientation mirrors **resurrection**. Reed jokes about the plot to kill Lazarus after he had already been raised (John 12:10). Why would he fear death now?

### Examples & Applications

- **Music and memory:** Reed remembers playing DC Talk's *Free at Last* on repeat on his brother's Discman in 1996–97. Most people know how strongly music connects to emotion, and the Psalms were Israel's songs. Christian artists often set Psalms to music for worship.
- **The "mental chess match":** Reed describes retreating into silent strategizing during arguments so he wouldn't "give full vent" to anger. Overusing one wisdom principle (Proverbs 29:11) without the Psalms' emotional honesty can make you rigid and cut off from your heart.
- **Campus ministry:** A college student who feels "God is nowhere" but was taught God is always present can pray Psalm 13 ("How long, O Lord? Will you forget me forever?"). That turns forbidden feelings into prayer.
- **Answering "How are you doing?":** Marty says this question is frustrating when life is simply *fine*, in a season of orientation. The Psalms of orientation give that season its own song and permission to be content.
- **The Chosen:** Marty recalls an opening scene showing David writing a Psalm, an example of how the packaging of poetry, not just its content, reaches people deeply.
- **Daily prayer:** The Psalms have long been part of the church's lectionary and liturgical calendar. Praying one Psalm a day is a simple practice that works for any believer.
- **Read beyond the Bible:** To read the Bible better, Reed suggests reading things that *aren't* the Bible, especially poetry, rather than only social media, news and sports.

### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

1. **Hebrew poetic devices:** Study parallelism (synonymous, antithetic, synthetic), chiasm, acrostics (for example Psalm 119), and wordplay. Start with Robert Alter's *The Art of Biblical Poetry*.
2. **The ethics of translation:** Read Alter's *The Art of Bible Translation* and compare several translations of one Psalm to see what each gains and loses.
3. **Imprecatory Psalms:** Explore how Psalm 137 and other Psalms that curse enemies can be read as honest lament rather than prescribed behavior.
4. **Ancient Near Eastern parallels:** Compare Psalm 29 and Psalm 104 with Canaanite (Ugaritic) and Egyptian hymns (for example the Hymn to the Aten).
5. **Original sin and Psalm 51:5:** Look at how this verse has been used in church history (for example Augustine) and how genre-aware reading might change its use.
6. **The structure of the Psalter:** The five "books" of Psalms (mirroring the Torah), the superscriptions, and the shift from lament toward praise across the collection.
7. **Psalms in Jesus' life:** Jesus quotes the Psalms often, including Psalm 22:1 on the cross and Psalm 110 in debate.
8. **Recommended resources:**
   - Walter Brueggemann, *The Message of the Psalms*
   - Robert Alter, *The Art of Biblical Poetry*, *The Art of Biblical Narrative*, *The Art of Bible Translation*, and *The Book of Psalms* (translation and commentary)
   - Thomas G. Long, *Preaching and the Literary Forms of the Bible*
   - Anne Lamott, *Help, Thanks, Wow*
   - Eugene Peterson, *Answering God: The Psalms as Tools for Prayer*
   - Thomas Merton, *Praying the Psalms*

### Comprehension Questions

1. According to Laurence Perrine, as quoted by Thomas Long, what makes poetry different from other literary forms? How does that change the way we should read the Psalms?
2. Why does Reed caution against using Psalm 51:5 as a doctrinal proof text? How does Psalm 139 help make the point?
3. Reed argues that in today's cynical culture, the Psalms of praise and wonder may be harder and more necessary than the Psalms of lament. What reasons does he give?
4. Name and describe the three modes in Walter Brueggemann's framework. Why does Reed prefer "new orientation" to "reorientation"?
5. What does the phrase "setting aside decorum for the sake of honesty" mean, and how does it differ from using lament in a flippant or ironic way?

### Personalized Summary

This episode starts the Psalms series by facing an honest problem: many thoughtful readers, Marty and Brent included, find it hard to connect with the Psalms, because years of Bible study have trained them to dissect rather than experience. Reed's answer is to read the Psalms as poetry, words carrying "greater pressure per word," meant to reshape our imagination rather than supply proof texts. Read that way, the Psalms become a vocabulary for the soul. They give us words for grief and doubt, and just as importantly for joy and wonder in an age that finds hope hard to voice.

The categories of help, thanks, wow, lament, trust, critique and reflection, along with Brueggemann's orientation, disorientation and new orientation, are not neat boxes but a messy Venn diagram that mirrors real life. Whether you are on a summer porch with lemonade, stuck in a bitter winter, or seeing the first warm day of spring, there is a Psalm for that season. The deepest point is that God is the right conversation partner in all of them, and the Psalms invite you to "speak what you feel, not what you ought to say" to the God who already knows your heart.
