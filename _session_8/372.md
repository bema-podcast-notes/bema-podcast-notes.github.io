### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** BEMA 372 — Isaiah 36–39: Voices and Redaction  
**Hosts:** Marty Solomon and Brent Billings, with guest Elle Grover Fricks (plus a closing football segment with Reed)  
**Focus:** Isaiah 36–39, the historical narrative that bridges "First Isaiah" (chapters 1–39) and "Second Isaiah" (chapters 40–66), along with source criticism, redaction, and the Documentary Hypothesis

In this episode, Marty and Brent use the narrative section of Isaiah 36–39 to start a wider and often playful debate with Elle Grover Fricks about source criticism and redaction. Elle studied with Baruch Schwartz at Hebrew University of Jerusalem. She explains where the Documentary Hypothesis and the Neo-Documentary Hypothesis came from, what they claim, and what they have been useful for. She then gives six objections to how the theory is used in practice. Marty is more open to the idea of multiple sources and voices. Both agree that thinking critically about where texts came from is worthwhile, and that the theory becomes harmful when it is used to explain away the literary artistry of the text. The conversation then turns to Isaiah itself. Elle argues for a two-part reading, where the "song of the *baru*" (diagnosis and judgment) is followed by the "song of the *ashipu*" (curse-breaking and healing). Marty explains why he sees four sections or voices.

### Key Takeaways

- **"Redaction" means putting texts together, not cutting them out.** In biblical studies, a redactor or compiler stitches sources together into one text. This is the opposite of the FBI picture of blacking out lines with a Sharpie.
- **"Source criticism" means thinking critically *about* sources, not criticizing Scripture.** It asks who wrote a text, when, where, how, and from which manuscripts.
- **The Documentary Hypothesis came from Julius Wellhausen and other German scholars.** It proposes four sources behind the Torah: J, E, D, and P. It was dominant for about 70 years and fell out of favor in the late 1970s.
- **The Neo-Documentary Hypothesis, linked with Baruch Schwartz and Joel Baden, proposes one compiler.** In this view, a single compiler or group combined the four sources in one act of compilation. The field has also moved from mostly German Protestant scholarship to mostly Jewish scholarship.
- **The theory has real benefits.** It shows that biblical texts are in conversation with each other, and it breaks down a rigid "descended from on high" picture of how Scripture was written.
- **Elle gives six objections:**
  1. It becomes art rather than science.
  2. No J, E, D, or P manuscript has ever been found.
  3. Academia rewards endless re-dividing of the text.
  4. The work is very granular and has little practical payoff.
  5. It becomes a quick fix for any difficulty in the text.
  6. It hides ancient literary forms behind modern, ethnocentric expectations.
- **Who you learn from matters.** Philology and Assyriology have advanced a lot in recent decades. Look for recent scholarship from people who actually read the languages.
- **Isaiah has a clear break at chapter 40.** Even most conservative scholars see at least two sections, 1–39 and 40–66, and chapters 36–39 sit between them as narrative prose.
- **Elle reads Isaiah as a story of diagnosis followed by healing.** Judgment is not the end of the story. Redemption, restoration, and God's faithfulness are.
- **Science and art always work together in interpretation.** We are always "painting a picture" with Scripture, so we should ask what picture we are painting and why.

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. What Redaction Is (and Isn't)

- **Common misunderstanding:** "Redacted" brings to mind government documents with sections blacked out.
- **The scholarly meaning:** A redactor *combines* material. They take separate stories or manuscripts and stitch, rearrange, or splice them into one work.
- **Marty's loose use of the word:** He often means something as simple as editing, like the way Brent edits podcast episodes together. The hosts point out that the same words get used in very different ways, and that this makes these conversations hard. Labels and categories help until they stop helping.

#### 2. Source Criticism

- **Definition:** Asking critical questions about a text's origins, such as which manuscripts we have, where they came from, and how old they are.
- **Why it sounds threatening:** In Marty's Bible college training, "criticism" was treated as a warning sign for people who "don't believe in the inspiration of the Bible."
- **Reframe:** Asking who, when, where, and how is simply good reading. Marty connects this to the theme of his book *Asking Better Questions of the Bible*.

#### 3. The Documentary Hypothesis (Wellhausen)

- **Origin:** Julius Wellhausen (born 1844), a German scholar who said he became a theologian because "the scientific treatment of the Bible interested me."
- **Focus:** Mainly the Torah (Pentateuch or Chumash).
- **The problem it tries to solve:** Apparent inconsistencies in the text. One classic example is that Moses's father-in-law goes by three or four different names (Jethro, Reuel, Hobab, and others).
- **The four sources:**

  | Letter | Name | Traditional marker |
  |---|---|---|
  | **J** | Jahwist (German spelling of "Yahwist") | Uses the divine name YHWH |
  | **E** | Elohist | Uses *Elohim* for God |
  | **D** | Deuteronomist | Deuteronomy, and the "school" behind much of the historical books |
  | **P** | Priestly source | Priestly and ritual concerns, usually dated later |

- **Beyond the Torah:** Most of the rest of the Hebrew Bible is usually credited to one or two of these groups, especially the Deuteronomist. Scholars often describe these as *schools* of writers rather than single authors.
- **Weakness:** Many verses use YHWH and Elohim interchangeably, so dividing the text by divine name quickly becomes very complicated.
- **Status:** It dominated for about 70 years, especially in America, and declined in the late 1970s. It is still taught, but usually in its updated form.

#### 4. The Neo-Documentary Hypothesis

- **Main difference:** It proposes *one compiler*, either a single person or one group working at one time, who took the four complete sources and combined them. The older view pictured gradual accumulation over a long period.
- **Key figures:**
  - **Baruch Schwartz** (Hebrew University of Jerusalem), Elle's former professor.
  - **Joel Baden** (Yale), whose 2012 article lists differences between the old and new versions.
- **Characteristics:**
  - It focuses less on the absolute dating of the compiler.
  - It aims for objectivity using a "scientific" approach.
  - The leading scholarly voices shifted from German to Jewish scholars. Elle sees this as a healthy change, since it means listening to Jewish voices about Jewish texts.
- **Where it sits:** It is the dominant approach at major American universities. It is less dominant in Europe and largely absent from traditional Jewish study such as yeshiva or *chavruta* learning, where saying "that's J" would get blank looks.
- **Why it appeals in academia:** It partly reacts against traditional interpretive tools like *gematria*. Elle notes that Schwartz would openly say those approaches were not welcome in his classroom.

#### 5. An Analogy: The US Constitution

Brent asked for a modern comparison, and Elle offered one. Some people read the Constitution in layers: "that line came from a Jeffersonian idea of freedom, and it has been changed since." Others read it as one cohesive, finished document. The same tension shows up in how people read the Bible.

#### 6. What Source Criticism Offers

- **Texts in conversation:** Elements of the text can be heard responding to one another. Later writers can respond to earlier ones even without the Documentary Hypothesis, but the hypothesis makes this especially easy to see.
- **Breaking rigidity:** It challenges the picture of Scripture falling from heaven fully formed. That picture can hide the conversations that Jesus and the prophets were actually part of.
- **Context changes meaning:** Whether a prophet wrote before or after the Babylonian exile changes everything. The words are either a warning about the future or a reflection looking back.
- **Common ground:** Even a plain reading shows Moses could not have written the account of his own death at the end of Deuteronomy. The early rabbis noticed this. Elle jokes that this is "where we got the idea of ghost writing."
- **Connection to the "prophetic table":** Episode 71 drew on Walter Brueggemann to hear the prophets as different inspired voices in conversation. Marty finds a similar appeal in hearing multiple voices in the Torah.

#### 7. Elle's Six Objections ("The Six Pillars of Elle-inism")

1. **It is more art than science.** Assigning verses to sources is circular. "J likes this word, so this verse is J." But how do you know what J likes? The verses you already assigned to J. In the end, scholars shape a puzzle based on what feels "dubious" or "far-fetched."
2. **There is no archaeological evidence.** No one has ever found a manuscript of J, E, D, or P. We only have the text in its final form.
3. **Academic incentives.** Doctoral students need original theses, and there are endless ways to divide a few verses ("verse 2a is P, verse 2b is D"). This makes the hypothesis an easy, low-risk research area that does not require hard manuscript evidence.
4. **Too granular, too little payoff.** Elle quotes **Meir Sternberg** (Tel Aviv University): "Rarely have such grandiose theories of origination been built and revised and pitted against one another on the evidential equivalent of the head of a pin, and rarely have so many worked so long and so hard with so little to show for their trouble." Working out which father-in-law name belongs to which source does not deepen the story or serve anyone.
5. **A fix-all.** Whenever a text is confusing, people can say "documentary hypothesis" and stop thinking. Elle disagrees with Baden's framing that the central question is "Why is the Pentateuch incoherent?" She does not think the Pentateuch is incoherent.
6. **Ethnocentric blindness (the most serious objection).** Modern readers expect stories told in chronological order. When a text does not fit that expectation, for example two accounts of humanity's creation in Genesis 1–2, people assume two authors. This hides chiasms, literary echoes, and intentional design, which are the very "treasures" that careful reading uncovers. Answering "that seems dubious" is a preference, not an argument.

#### 8. Marty's Response

- He agrees all six dangers are real and come from experience, not just theory.
- He does not think they justify rejecting the theory completely. Subjectivity cuts both ways: people who reject source theories can also weave together convenient explanations.
- There is objective data underneath, such as word usage and the use of divine names, even if conclusions go beyond what the data supports.
- Everyone has different thresholds for when enough evidence "tips" the burden of proof.

#### 9. Elle's Clarification: Being Consistent Matters

- Elle is not "team doc hypo," but she does not think Scripture appeared all at once on a single date.
- **Example:** The Book of Jonah contains Persian loanwords, which points to a later date. Arguing otherwise is fruitless.
- **Her standard:** Be scientifically consistent. If you date a text late for thematic reasons, but it contains none of the language changes you would expect from that period, you need a strong explanation. "They were writing in an archaic style" is not convincing to her.

#### 10. Choosing Trustworthy Sources

- On philology (how words developed over time), scholarship after 2000 is generally more reliable. This is not because of any single event, but because the science keeps improving.
- Assyriology (the study of Akkadian and Mesopotamian sources) has grown dramatically.
- Check whether the scholar actually reads the language before trusting claims like "this verb proves…"
- Older authors can still be excellent in other areas. This caution applies specifically to linguistic and philological claims.

#### 11. The Structure of Isaiah

**Isaiah 36–39** is a narrative section placed between the poetic sections. Much of it closely parallels 2 Kings 18–20, covering Sennacherib's siege, Hezekiah's illness, and the Babylonian envoys. The Hebrew style and the prose presentation are noticeably different from the surrounding chapters.

**Common scholarly views:**
- **Two parts (most common):** chapters 1–39 and 40–66
- **Three parts:** 1–39, 40–55, and 56–66
- **Four to six parts or voices:** smaller divisions within the sections

**Elle's view: two parts.**
- She is not convinced by the argument that "a poet can't write narrative."
- She admits her bias openly: the split supports what she sees the book doing theologically.
- **First Isaiah** is the "angsty prophet" full of woes: *you have been weighed, measured, and found wanting*. This is not fortune-telling. It is an announcement that you will reap what you have sown.
- **Second Isaiah** deliberately writes *in Isaiah's voice* rather than as a new prophet, to say that Isaiah's judgment is not the end of the story. The desert becomes a garden, and the ruin becomes a fountain.
- **Her reframe of hope:** Hope is not a light sprinkling inside the prophets. Chapters 40–66 are the reality, and the darkness was the partial view.

**Marty's view: four sections.**
1. **Chapters 1–11/12:** the setup and situation at hand
2. **Chapters 12–39:** a poetic collection of woes and curses
3. **Chapters 40 onward:** a voice in captivity after Babylon ("your sins have been paid for"), reflecting on what it means to live in exile
4. **A final section:** a prophetic picture of the end times, showing what God can do

He depends on scholars he respects, including Brueggemann, who splits Second Isaiah into two parts, because he does not yet have the tools to evaluate this independently.

#### 12. Baru and Ashipu: Elle's Hypothesis

This idea builds on an earlier episode with Josh about Isaiah 6. Elle draws on two kinds of Mesopotamian (Akkadian) priest who would have been familiar to Isaiah's audience:

| Priest | Role | Elle's link to Isaiah |
|---|---|---|
| ***Baru*** | Diviner or seer. Reads omens to tell whether the gods are for or against you and what will happen. | **Isaiah 1–39:** "God's *baru*." It diagnoses where the people are and what their choices will produce. |
| ***Ashipu*** | Exorcist or healer. Breaks curses, brings divine healing, and reverses bad destinies. | **Isaiah 40–66:** "God's *ashipu*." God chooses to break the curse and replace the destiny the people wrote for themselves with a resurrected one. |

**How chapters 36–39 fit:**
- First Isaiah opens with the death of **King Uzziah**, a name Elle connects with *strength* (in her reading, the fierce strength of a goat).
- The narrative bridge centers on **King Hezekiah**, whose name contains *chazaq*, meaning strength. Elle describes it as a strength that can *bind* something.
- This fits the *ashipu* theme: the bad destiny is coming, but God will bind it so it cannot exert its power.
- Elle presents this openly as her own hypothesis: "I take culpability if it's totally off the chain."

**Marty's response:** There is more "science" in this than Elle gives herself credit for. The historical context is real. Following Abraham Heschel's work on the prophets, Marty notes that God meets people through forms they already know and then does something captivating with them.

#### 13. Art and Science Together

- **Brent:** Without the art, how would any of this change our lives? He also notes that missing evidence is not final. For a long time there was no archaeological evidence for some biblical figures until discoveries were made. Some things we may simply never know.
- **Peter Enns and Bruce Feiler:** We are always "painting a picture" with Scripture by choosing which stories to tell, how, and when. The question is what picture we paint and why.
- **Ilana Kurshan (*If All the Seas Were Ink*):** She describes the *hadran* prayer said at the end of a Talmud tractate. In her telling, we study Talmud and Talmud studies us, and we are the ones who give the material its color and expression.
- Elle adds that the point is not to stockpile rabbinic sayings as ammunition to prove how sophisticated we are.

### Examples & Applications

- **Reading the Constitution:** The debate between reading it in historical layers and reading it as one finished document mirrors how people approach biblical composition. It helps explain why people feel so strongly about both.
- **Editing a podcast:** Brent combining recorded segments into one episode is a simple, familiar picture of redaction: separate material shaped into a whole.
- **Moses's death in Deuteronomy 34:** Moses cannot have written his own obituary. This shows that even the most traditional reader already accepts some editorial work, which was the starting point for rabbinic discussion of authorship.
- **Genesis 1 and 2:** Two creation accounts can be read as two sources, or as an intentional literary structure. The choice changes whether we look for meaning in how the accounts relate.
- **Persian words in Jonah:** This is a concrete example of linguistic evidence that does support a later date. It shows what responsible source work looks like.
- **Checking the publication date:** Marty now looks at the copyright page of a book making source-critical claims. The 1960s–70s are a warning sign for philological arguments.
- **Applying Isaiah's two movements:** In personal or community darkness, Isaiah 1–39 names honestly what we have reaped, and Isaiah 40–66 insists that this is not the end. A group might ask where it is "found wanting" and where God is "breaking the curse."
- **Hezekiah's closing words (Isaiah 39:8):** "The word of the Lord you have spoken is good… for he thought, 'There will be peace and security in my lifetime.'" Brent closes with "May it be so for us." The verse can also be read as warning against complacency: Hezekiah settles for peace in his own lifetime while exile hangs over the next generation.
- **Studying in community:** Brent encourages joining a group to wrestle with these questions, because working through the different voices of Scripture is not something to do alone.

### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

1. **Isaiah 36–39 compared with 2 Kings 18–20:** Compare the parallel accounts. What does each context emphasize, and why might the material appear in both?
2. **Joel Baden's 2012 article:** Study the seven differences he lists between the classic and Neo-Documentary Hypotheses.
3. **Baruch Schwartz's papers:** Available on Academia.edu, these show the Neo-Documentary method in practice.
4. **Meir Sternberg, *The Poetics of Biblical Narrative*:** A major literary approach that challenges source-critical fragmentation.
5. **Mesopotamian *baru* and *ashipu* priests:** Look into Assyriological scholarship on divination and exorcism and test how well Elle's framework holds up.
6. **The names Uzziah and Hezekiah:** Do a word study of *oz* and *chazaq* in Hebrew and see where these words appear in Isaiah.
7. **Two, three, or four Isaiahs:** Compare the arguments for each division, including Brueggemann's commentaries on Isaiah.
8. **Literary forms the hypothesis may hide:** Study chiasm, repeated summary-and-expansion patterns, and intertextual echoes in Genesis and Exodus.
9. **Linguistic dating:** Research how scholars use loanwords and language change, the distinction between Classical and Late Biblical Hebrew, to date texts.
10. **Related BEMA episodes:** Episode 82 (multiple authorship and Torah), Episode 71 (the prophetic table), and the Isaiah 6 episode with Josh and Elle.
11. **Abraham Heschel, *The Prophets*:** Explore how Israel's prophets compare with, and differ from, prophetic figures in surrounding cultures.

### Summary

This episode was less an exposition of Isaiah 36–39 than a friendly argument about how to read texts like it. Elle explained the history of the Documentary and Neo-Documentary Hypotheses and gave them credit for showing that Scripture is a conversation between voices, not something dictated in a vacuum. She was also clear about where these theories go wrong. They drift from science into art, they rest on no manuscript evidence, they reward endless academic re-dividing, and, most seriously, they impose modern Western expectations on ancient literature and erase the artistry that close reading reveals. Marty agreed with the dangers but stayed open to multiple voices, pointing out that subjectivity affects both sides.

Applied to Isaiah, the discussion lands on something hopeful. Whether you see two sections or four, the book moves from diagnosis to healing. Elle's picture of God first acting as *baru*, naming what the people's choices will bring, and then as *ashipu*, breaking the curse and rewriting their destiny, gives a memorable way to hold the book together, with Hezekiah's "strength that binds" standing between the two halves. Above all, the episode argues that careful, honest scholarship and creative, faithful interpretation need each other. We are always painting a picture with the text, and the important questions are what picture we are painting and why.
