### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** BEMA 376: Maternal Images in Isaiah  
**Hosts:** Brent Billings, with guests Elle and Reed (Marty Solomon is absent this week)  
**Focus:** Isaiah 42:13–14, Isaiah 49:13–18, and Isaiah 66:6–13, with related texts from Genesis 1–2, Exodus 33, 1 Thessalonians 2, and the Psalms

This episode adds to the series on Isaiah by looking at the maternal images for God in the second half of the book. Elle leads the discussion and says she wants it to be practical as well as philosophical. It opens with two basic questions: does God have a sex, and does God have a gender? The group then works through three passages: God as a woman crying out in childbirth next to God as a warrior (Isaiah 42), God as a nursing mother who cannot forget her child (Isaiah 49), and God as a mother who nurses, carries, and plays with her child on her knees (Isaiah 66). Along the way they look at the Hebrew word *racham* (womb, compassion), the creative power of breath and voice, what these images mean for masculinity and parenting, and the invitation to experience God's closeness, comfort, and playfulness rather than keeping faith only in the head.

### Key Takeaways

- **God has no body, and that includes no sex.** In the catechism's words, God is "a spirit, and hath not a body like man" (or woman). Gendered language for God is imagery and metaphor, not anatomy.
- **The text uses masculine grammar, but God's names are less clear-cut.** Hebrew has no neuter, so God's pronouns are masculine. Elle argues that both *YHWH* and *Elohim* mix masculine and feminine elements.
- **Words like "masculine" and "feminine" are largely set by culture.** Reed points out that we have to ask what we mean by them. God has the whole range of qualities that cultures divide into the two.
- **Isaiah 42 deliberately puts the warrior next to the woman in labor.** Both are fully present in the moment, both risk life (one to defend it, one to bring it in), and both do their work through *voice* and *breath*.
- **God creates by voice and breath.** God speaks creation into being and breathes life into Adam. In Hebrew poetry, breath is often fierce, not gentle. This invites us to pay attention to how we use our own words.
- **The word usually translated "mercy" is *racham*, which literally means "womb."** Elle prefers "nurturing" or "compassion." These words capture the womb's expansiveness, provision, and safety better than the legal sense of "mercy."
- **Nurturing is not "unmasculine."** If God is spoken of with masculine pronouns and also has *racham*, then nurturing belongs to fathers and men as much as to mothers and women.
- **Isaiah 49 shows a love that won't let go.** God's commitment is stronger than even a nursing mother's bond with her baby.
- **Isaiah 66 shows God's closeness ("withness") and playfulness.** A nursing, carried, knee-bounced baby has nothing to offer that would earn love. God wants closeness with us for its own sake.
- **The invitation is to experience God, not only analyze God.** Listeners are challenged to let themselves be comforted, carried, and delighted in by God.

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. Does God Have a Sex or a Gender?

**Sex: no.** Elle starts with the catechism: God is spirit and has no chromosomes, hormones, or body. This sets the Bible apart from its ancient Near Eastern setting. Other creation myths describe the gods in explicitly sexual and violent terms, while Genesis 1 has neither.

**Gender: more complicated.** The hosts take three angles:

- **Genesis 1:27 (Brent).** "In the image of God he created them; male and female he created them." It takes both male and female to reflect the image of God fully.
- **Grammar (Elle).** Biblical Hebrew has only masculine and feminine. There is no neuter, as Greek has. So the pronouns for God are masculine except in poetry and metaphor. She also notes that the Spirit (*ruach*) is grammatically feminine in Hebrew, while *pneuma* is neuter in Greek.
- **Culture (Reed).** "Masculine" and "feminine" are cultural labels. If masculine means "rough and tumble" and feminine means "gentle and nurturing," God is both.

#### 2. Ambiguity in God's Names

Elle offers a reading of the Hebrew forms of God's two main names. She admits that scholars may disagree:

| Name | Masculine element | Feminine element |
|------|-------------------|------------------|
| **YHWH** | The *yod* prefix (like a masculine future-tense verb, tied to "I will be") | The final *hey*, typically a feminine ending |
| **Elohim** | The *-im* plural ending (masculine) | The *hey* within the word, compared with the shorter *Elim* |

**Her point:** The biblical authors consistently chose masculine pronouns, and she takes that seriously. But the names themselves hold some ambiguity. She says unusual gender forms like this are rare and usually *mean something*. Her example is **Qohelet**, the name of the speaker in Ecclesiastes, which has a feminine form. She reads it as deliberate, even satirical.

#### 3. Language as Pointing Toward Experience

Reed adds a point about language: words signify something underneath them. Gendered language for God tries to express a *range of experiences* of the divine. Sometimes "masculine" imagery fits, and sometimes "feminine" imagery does. Both help God's people look back on their experience and live faithfully going forward.

#### 4. Isaiah 42:13–14 – The Warrior and the Woman in Labor

> "The LORD will march out like a champion, like a warrior he will stir up his zeal... But now, like a woman in childbirth, I cry out, I gasp and pant."

**Hebrew parallelism:** Hebrew poetry often pairs images side by side to explain each other. Here the warrior and the woman in labor are set right next to each other.

**Why pair them?**

- **Power and nurture together.** Reed's wife, Leigh Ann, said she never felt more powerful, or more nurturing, than when she gave birth.
- **Total presence.** Soldiers in combat and women in labor are both completely focused on the moment.
- **Risking life:**
  - The warrior *risks life to defend life*.
  - The mother *risks life to bring in new life*.
- **Voice and breath.** Both rely on breath and voice. Martial arts depend on breath control and the shout, and labor depends on breathing and crying out. Reed stresses that the cry of labor is guttural and primal, closer to a battle cry than a whimper.

#### 5. How God Creates: Voice and Breath

- **Genesis 1:** God *speaks*, and creation comes into being. There is no birth and no battle, unlike the Babylonian *Enuma Elish*, where Marduk splits the dragon Tiamat to make the world.
- **Genesis 2:** God forms Adam from the dust and *breathes* life into him.
- **Breath as fierce.** In Hebrew poetry, breath is often powerful rather than soft:
  - Psalm 18:15: "the blast of breath from your nostrils"
  - Isaiah 30:33: the breath of the LORD sets the fire burning
  - Isaiah 42, the same passage: God withers vegetation and lays kingdoms waste
  - The KJV of Isaiah 42:14 reads, "I will destroy and devour at once"

Reed says this changed how he pictured Adam's creation. He had imagined a quiet, intimate breath, and now considers a "gale-force" one.

#### 6. *Racham* – Womb, Compassion, Nurture

Drawing on Rabbi David Fohrman's teaching (Aleph Beta), Elle looks at a common Hebrew word pair:

- ***Chesed*** – steadfast love, loyal covenant love
- ***Racham*** – usually translated "mercy." The same root gives *rechem*, "womb." It is the word used in the Genesis stories about Leah's and Rachel's fertility.

**Why "mercy" falls short:** "Mercy" suggests a courtroom, where guilt meets a pardon. The Hebrew word comes from the *womb*. Elle suggests **"nurturing"** or **"compassion"** instead, because the womb is:

1. **Expansive.** It grows from the size of a fist to the size of a watermelon.
2. **The first provider.** Blood volume rises about 50% during pregnancy to supply the baby.
3. **A safe place.** It seals itself to protect the baby.

Key verses: Exodus 33:19 ("I will have compassion on whom I will have compassion"), Isaiah 49:13 ("the LORD comforts his people and will have compassion on his afflicted ones"), and the idea, echoing Lamentations 3:22–23, that God's compassion never runs out and is new every morning.

Reed adds that everything the womb does is directed toward the life of the child, and it never stops, even while the mother sleeps.

#### 7. Implications for Masculinity and Parenting

Elle's "radical" conclusion: if God has *racham*, and God's pronouns are masculine, then **there is nothing unmasculine about nurturing.** If men model their masculinity on God rather than on internet culture, fatherhood can't be limited to tough love, sports, and hard lessons.

- **This may be freeing** for men who never fit a hardened version of masculinity.
- **This may be a challenge** for those who find toughness easy.
- **Reed's marriage point:** Rigid roles cause breakdown. Healthy spouses balance and grow each other until they "become more like one."
- **1 Thessalonians 2:7, 11:** Paul describes his ministry as being like a nursing mother *and* like an exhorting father, both in the same person.

#### 8. Isaiah 49:15–16 – The Mother Who Cannot Forget

> "Can a mother forget the baby at her breast...? Though she may forget, I will not forget you! See, I have engraved you on the palms of my hands."

- **Uniqueness.** There is no fatherly equivalent of nursing (Reed).
- **Bond.** Reed mentions *fetal-maternal microchimerism*: cells from the baby can remain in the mother's body for decades. A mother's sense that part of her child lives in her is literally, biologically true.
- **The character of God:** fierce, committed, warm, affectionate. It is a deep, almost biological love that does not depend on performance. It is the opposite of a transactional relationship.
- **Room for pain.** The text admits that human mothers *may* forget. For people with difficult mothers, God says, "I am not that."

#### 9. Isaiah 66:6–13 – Nursing, Carrying, and Dandling

The passage looks back to Isaiah 40:1 ("Comfort, comfort my people") and pictures Zion giving birth instantly, before labor pains come. It builds three tender images:

1. **Nursing.** It is about more than food. It also calms, comforts, helps a baby rest, and creates closeness.
2. **Carried on the hip.** This is constant "withness": where the mother goes, the baby goes. The baby does nothing impressive and doesn't need to.
3. **Dandled on the knees.** This is play. God's love is *playful*, not only corrective.

**Theological point:** God's presence is not just abstract omnipresence ("God is everywhere, logically"). It is also not a bodyguard who follows us around counting our mistakes. It is the closeness of a mother who simply loves being with her child.

### Examples & Applications

- **Using our words to give life.** God creates by speaking, and Jesus heals by speaking ("Lazarus, come out!"). Elle asks: are we complaining, gossiping, and forecasting the worst, or are we praying, praising, encouraging, and speaking life?
- **Wedding vows.** Reed, who officiates weddings, sees vows as speech that creates something new: "Now there is this family, and before there wasn't."
- **Promises to children.** A promise like "we're getting ice cream" builds a small world for a child. Breaking it "does a kind of destructive violence" to that world.
- **Coaches and teachers.** Words spoken decades ago, kind or cruel, still echo. Reed has pastored students wounded by high school coaches. This connects to James 3 on the power of the tongue.
- **"Get up."** Brent's lighthearted story: one firm phrase (borrowed from Trinity in *The Matrix*) motivated his wife, Maggie, to wash her very long hair. She was still talking about it days later. Intentional words have power.
- **Nurturing fatherhood.** Brent says it's frustrating when nurturing his boys doesn't "work." Elle suggests that his kids remembering him as nurturing may matter more than the result in the moment. She also points to Romans 2:4: God's kindness is meant to lead to repentance.
- **Comfort before correction.** When Elle's three-year-old ran in saying, "Daddy took all my cars away!", she didn't start with "What did you do to deserve it?" She started with "Oh no, what happened? Tell me about it." Then she moved to how to do better next time. This is the maternal comfort of Isaiah 66.
- **"Babies in God's nursery."** Reed jokes that we sing about being soldiers in God's army. What if we wrote songs and made T-shirts about being babies in God's nursery?
- **Benevolent condescension.** Like a parent delighting in a baby's coo, God bends down to delight in us without asking us to perform.
- **Getting out of the "brain cage."** Elle challenges BEMA listeners, who may prefer keeping faith intellectual, to go "further up and further in" (C.S. Lewis). That means being open to being comforted, carried, and delighted in by God.
- **Processing in community.** Brent notes that birth and parenting images may be painful for some people, for example because of infertility, a hard birth, or difficult parents. He encourages working through them with others.

### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

1. **Other maternal images of God in Scripture.** Examples include Deuteronomy 32:18 ("the God who gave you birth"), Hosea 13:8 (a mother bear), Psalm 131 (a weaned child), and Matthew 23:37 (a hen gathering her chicks).
2. **Hebrew grammar and divine names.** Look at scholarly views on the forms of *YHWH* and *Elohim*, and how much weight grammatical gender should carry.
3. ***Chesed* and *racham* across the Old Testament.** Trace how translations render *racham* and how "compassion" or "nurture" changes key passages such as Exodus 34:6 and Psalm 103:13.
4. **Breath and Spirit (*ruach*).** Follow the theme from Genesis 1:2 and 2:7 through Ezekiel 37 to John 20:22 and Acts 2.
5. **Ancient Near Eastern creation myths.** Compare Genesis with the *Enuma Elish* and other myths, and look at how the Bible deliberately reworks them.
6. **Medieval Christian mystics.** Explore Julian of Norwich, Anselm, and Bernard of Clairvaux on "Mother Jesus" and nursing imagery.
7. **Qohelet's feminine name.** Look into theories about the name and its rhetorical purpose in Ecclesiastes.
8. **Gender roles and the image of God.** Consider how Genesis 1:27 and 1 Thessalonians 2 might reshape ideas of masculinity, femininity, and parenting.
9. **Zion as mother.** Study how Isaiah moves between God as mother and Jerusalem/Zion as mother in chapters 49–66.

### Personalized Summary

This episode looks at Isaiah's most tender images for God. In Isaiah 42, God cries out like a woman in labor, pictured next to a warrior shouting into battle. The pairing shows that fierce power and life-giving nurture are not opposites, and that God works, like both of them, through voice and breath. In Isaiah 49, God's love outlasts even a nursing mother's bond and engraves us on God's palms. In Isaiah 66, God nurses, carries, and bounces us on the knee, wanting closeness and play rather than performance. The Hebrew word *racham* (womb) runs underneath all of this and reframes "mercy" as an expansive, providing, protective kind of nurture. That in turn shows nurture is as fitting for fathers as for mothers. The hosts close with an invitation: use your words to give life, and let yourself be held, comforted, and delighted in by God, not just think about God.
