### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** 367: The Nations in Prophecy (Isaiah 13–23)  
**Hosts:** Marty Solomon and Brent Billings  
**Focus:** Isaiah 13–23, the oracles against Babylon, Philistia, Moab, Damascus, Egypt, Edom (Dumah), Jerusalem ("the Valley of Vision"), and Tyre

For twelve chapters Isaiah has spoken almost entirely to God's covenant people, Israel and Judah. In this episode Marty and Brent look at the section where his focus widens to the nations around them. Readers often find these "oracles against the nations" depressing, like a long list of everyone who has been naughty. The hosts argue that they show how complete God's care is: nothing goes unnoticed and nobody is left out. God addresses superpowers like Babylon and Egypt and small places like Philistia. In the middle of the list he also addresses his own city, Jerusalem. Along the way the hosts discuss whose voice is speaking in prophecy, Gregory Boyd's *Cross Vision*, the Jewish "both/and" view of God's action and human choice, and an unplanned look at what the name "Jerusalem" means. They close by asking what it means today to stand on the side of *shalom*.

### Key Takeaways

- **God cares about every nation, not just the ones that bear his name.** God has a covenant relationship with Israel, but his concern for justice and shalom reaches Babylon, Philistia, Moab, Egypt, and everyone else.
- **There are two incomplete ways to read the prophets.** One hears only a vague message that "God is angry and people are sinful." The other hears only a personal message between God and his own people. Isaiah 13–23 corrects both by showing God's concern is wide-ranging and also specific.
- **Size doesn't decide God's attention.** Empires like Babylon and Egypt get oracles, and so does small Philistia, roughly today's Gaza Strip.
- **God's people get no exemption.** Jerusalem shows up in the middle of the oracles against the nations in Isaiah 22. God has no favorites who get a free pass.
- **Judgment comes from both God's action and human choice.** The king of Babylon is condemned because "you have destroyed your land and killed your people" (Isaiah 14:20). God is also described as "mustering an army." Both are true at once.
- **These oracles aren't "sin whack-a-mole."** Kingdoms built on the backs of the oppressed can't last. God works actively to dismantle systems that cause destructive injustice.
- **Prophets are inspired but write from a human point of view.** Each prophet sees from a limited vantage point. Other biblical voices, and finally Jesus, help complete the picture.
- **The application is to stand on the side of shalom.** Christians shouldn't pick a nation or a team. They should celebrate peace and justice wherever they appear and heed the warning wherever injustice is found, including among themselves.

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. The Nations in Prophecy Table

Marty points to a table titled "The Nations in Prophecy" from the out-of-print *NIV Archaeological Study Bible* (p. 1078). It lists nations down one side (Babylon, Philistia, Moab, Damascus/Syria, Egypt, Edom, Tyre, Ammon, Nineveh) and the prophets who address them across the top (Amos, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and others).

What the table shows:

- **Philistia** is addressed by Amos, Isaiah, Jeremiah, and Ezekiel.
- **Babylon** is addressed by Isaiah and Jeremiah, and also by Habakkuk.
- **Edom** is the only nation that "makes it all the way across." Amos, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and Obadiah all address it, and Obadiah is written entirely against Edom.
- **Nineveh** is addressed in Jonah and Nahum.
- In chapters 13–23, Isaiah covers every nation on the table except Ammon and Nineveh.

The table makes visible something that's easy to miss in the text: God's attention to the nations runs through all of the prophets.

#### 2. Two Incomplete Ways of Reading the Prophets

| Reading | What it hears | What it misses |
|---|---|---|
| **General/abstract** | "God is angry; all have sinned." | The specific relationship and concrete injustices God names |
| **Intimate/insider** | "You are my people and you have sinned against me." | God's care for everyone outside the covenant community |

Isaiah holds the two together. Chapters 1–12 are pointed covenant talk with Judah and Israel. Chapters 13–23 show that God sees everyone else with the same care. Marty compares this to Jonah, where God cares about every person in Nineveh "and also many animals" (Jonah 4:11).

#### 3. Whose Voice Is Speaking?

In Isaiah 13:3 ("I have commanded my holy ones…") it's unclear whether Isaiah or the LORD is speaking.

- Prophets often move in and out of God's first-person voice without marking the change.
- Robert Alter's translation of the Hebrew Bible often notes this ambiguity in Isaiah and Micah.
- Translators have trouble placing quotation marks. A phrase like "declares the LORD Almighty" (Isaiah 17:3) shows where a quote ends but not always where it began.
- Even when Isaiah is the one speaking, the one "moving the pieces of the cosmos" is clearly the LORD.

#### 4. Gregory Boyd's *Cross Vision*

Brent has been reading Boyd's *Cross Vision*, a 250-page condensed version of his 1,500-page *The Crucifixion of the Warrior God*.

- **Main idea:** Jesus, and especially Jesus on the cross, is the clearest and purest picture of God. Violent portrayals of God in the Hebrew Bible should be read in that light.
- **Applied to Isaiah:** Is "the LORD Almighty mustering an army for war" a literal description of God's action, or the prophet's perspective on what is happening? Boyd suggests that moments of God's true character "break through" the prophet's perspective.
- **Example tension in Isaiah 14:19–22:** God seems to be destroying Babylon, yet verse 20 says the king is condemned "for you have destroyed your land and killed your people." So who is doing the destroying?
- **God partners with people anyway.** Even when a human perspective is incomplete, God keeps working through people toward a fuller revelation of who he is.

**Marty's response and clarifications:**

- Language matters here. Boyd wouldn't say the prophet made *mistakes*. He would talk about inspired writers who still speak from a limited human perspective. (Brent clarifies that the word "mistake" was his, not Boyd's.)
- This fits the "prophetic table" idea from Session 2. Inspired, infallible authors each have their own vantage point, so readers need other perspectives, from later in history and from "the other side of the table," to get the full picture.
- Marty appreciates Boyd's Christ-centered approach. Jesus quotes the prophets, keeps what he loves, and reframes the rest because "this is the perspective we need right now."
- **Critique:** Brent and Marty both note that Boyd's theology centers so heavily on the cross that it can underplay the rest of Jesus's life and teaching, which was a major theme of Session 3.

#### 5. Both/And: God's Action and Human Choice

Western thinking tends to frame things as either/or, for example determinism versus free will. The biblical and Jewish world tends to hold paradoxes as both/and.

- **Human responsibility:** Babylon's guilt lies squarely in its imperial injustice, its immorality, and how its king leads.
- **Divine involvement:** God "rises up," raises a banner, and acts.
- **Why it can't be untangled:** Marty compares it to two colors of play-dough smashed together that will never come apart again. He also refers to Reed's earlier image of God as a frustrated parent or scorned husband: God's response is bound up with the people's behavior.

#### 6. Unsustainable Empires

Marty reframes judgment away from tit-for-tat punishment:

- The world human empires build "is not built for shalom." It's built on people who are oppressed, marginalized, and exploited.
- That makes it unsustainable, and it eventually collapses.
- It also collapses because God is actively dismantling what causes destructive injustice.
- **At best**, an unjust system can't last. **At worst**, it draws God's focused attention "in a very, very bad way."

#### 7. Survey of the Oracles in Isaiah 13–23

| Nation | Passage | Key Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Babylon** | 13–14 | God musters an army; the king is condemned for destroying his own land and people (14:20) |
| **Philistia** | 14:28–32 | Given "in the year King Ahaz died"; "the poorest of the poor will find pasture"; Zion is a refuge for the afflicted |
| **Moab** | 15–16 | Mourning in Ar, Kir, Dibon, Nebo, Medeba, Heshbon; Moab's "overweening pride" and empty boasts (16:6); "My heart cries out over Moab" (15:5) |
| **Damascus** | 17:1–3 | Damascus becomes "a heap of ruins"; the oracle ends with "declares the LORD Almighty" |
| **Egypt** | 19:1–4 | The LORD rides on a swift cloud; Egyptian is turned against Egyptian; a "fierce king" will rule them |
| **Edom (Dumah)** | 21:11–12 | "Watchman, what is left of the night?… Morning is coming, but also the night" |
| **Jerusalem** | 22 | "The Valley of Vision"; leaders fled before the enemy arrived; houses torn down to strengthen the wall |
| **Tyre** | 23 | "Wail, you ships of Tarshish"; Tyre was "the marketplace of the nations" |

**Cush** (Isaiah 18) also appears. Scholars debate exactly where it was, but it's generally in the region of Egypt.

#### 8. Jerusalem in the Middle of the Nations (Isaiah 22)

Brent notices that Isaiah 22 is about Jerusalem, and this becomes one of the main points of the episode.

- **God's own city is judged alongside the pagan nations.** God "doesn't have favorites" and sees both insiders and outsiders.
- **There's a strange detail:** the leaders "fled while the enemy was still far away" and were captured "without using the bow."
- **Marty's reading:** This is a lament about lack of trust, not mainly a rebuke of cowardice. It echoes Isaiah's earlier challenge to King Ahaz in Isaiah 7 to stand firm in faith. God's grief is roughly: "You didn't even give me a chance to show up."
- **Practical details fit the history:** Storing water in the lower pool and tearing down houses to strengthen the wall (22:9–10) match what's known about ancient siege preparations, and those patterns would repeat for centuries.

#### 9. Unplanned Detour: What Does "Jerusalem" Mean?

Marty had been taught that "Jerusalem" means "valley of vision." He and Brent checked on the spot:

- The NET Bible note says it's uncertain why Jerusalem is called the "Valley of Vision." It suggests the Hinnom Valley may be in view, possibly because the destruction there is the focus of the prophecy.
- Lexicons (via Blue Letter Bible) give options like "foundation of peace," "possession of peace," or "teaching of peace." These link *shalem* to *shalom* and *yeru-* to roots meaning "to found," "to teach" (related to *Torah*), or "to possess."
- None of the sources linked the name to "vision." The phrase "Valley of Vision" (Hebrew *gei chizzayon*) appears to be Isaiah's poetic label for the city, not the meaning of the name itself.
- Marty laughs at himself for repeating something he'd never checked, and expects a message from Elle correcting him.

### Examples & Applications

- **Current events:** Marty applies the passage to the time of recording. God cares about what's happening in America, Mexico, Venezuela, Ukraine, Israel, and Gaza, all at once and with the same care.
- **Church and world:** God cares about the church and about Christians. He cares "literally just as much" about people who don't bear his name.
- **Choosing sides:** When people ask which side they should be on, the answer from Isaiah is the side of shalom. Celebrate peace wherever it happens, grieve where it's missing, and listen to the warning when we're the ones causing harm.
- **No free pass:** "No corner, no flag, no nation, no people group, no church" is exempt from God's concern for justice.
- **Philistia and modern Gaza:** Biblical Philistia's cities, such as Gaza, Ashdod, and Ekron, are in roughly the same area as the modern Gaza Strip. The connection makes God's care for "the poorest of the poor" there feel immediate.
- **Studying together:** Brent encourages listeners to read all of Isaiah 13–23 with friends or a discussion group, since the episode couldn't cover every verse.
- **Study resources:**
  - *NIV Archaeological Study Bible* (out of print; look for used copies), for the "Nations in Prophecy" table
  - *NIV Cultural Backgrounds Study Bible*, which has a map of these nations but not the table
  - Robert Alter, *The Hebrew Bible: A Translation with Commentary*
  - Gregory Boyd, *Cross Vision* and *The Crucifixion of the Warrior God*
  - NET Bible notes and the Blue Letter Bible lexicon tools

### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

1. **Edom across the prophets:** Why does Edom get so much attention (Amos 1, Isaiah 21 and 34, Jeremiah 49, Ezekiel 25 and 35, Obadiah)? How does the Jacob/Esau "brother against brother" story shape these oracles?
2. **Tyre and the "Satan" backstory:** How did Isaiah 14 (the "morning star") and the Tyre oracles in Isaiah 23 and Ezekiel 28 come to be read as descriptions of Satan? Is that reading justified?
3. **Prophetic voice and quotation marks:** Compare how different translations (NIV, ESV, NET, Alter) mark divine speech in Isaiah 13–23.
4. **Boyd's cruciform hermeneutic:** Read *Cross Vision* critically. What does it explain well, and where does a cross-only focus leave out Jesus's life and teaching?
5. **Etymology of "Jerusalem":** Look into the meanings proposed for *Yerushalayim* and why Isaiah calls the city the "Valley of Vision."
6. **Isaiah 22 and Isaiah 7:** How does the failure of trust in chapter 22 connect to Isaiah's call for Ahaz to "stand firm in faith"?
7. **Isaiah 19:18–25:** These verses, not covered in the episode, end with a surprising vision of Egypt and Assyria worshiping alongside Israel ("Blessed be Egypt my people"). How does that reshape the whole section?
8. **Unsustainable empire:** Trace the theme of imperial injustice from Babel to Babylon to Revelation 18.

### Comprehension Questions

1. What two incomplete ways of reading the prophets does Marty describe, and how does Isaiah 13–23 correct both of them?
2. In Isaiah 14:20, why is the king of Babylon condemned? How does this verse show the "both/and" relationship between God's action and human choice?
3. Why is it significant that small Philistia gets an oracle alongside superpowers like Babylon and Egypt?
4. Why does the prophecy against Jerusalem ("the Valley of Vision") in Isaiah 22 appear among the oracles against the nations? What does Marty think its main complaint is?
5. According to Marty, what is the difference between seeing these oracles as "tit-for-tat" punishment and seeing them as the collapse of an "unsustainable" world?

### Personal Summary

On a first read these chapters look like a grim list of nations in trouble. This episode shows they're really about how far God's care reaches. After twelve chapters focused on his own people, Isaiah turns to Babylon, Philistia, Moab, Damascus, Egypt, Edom, and Tyre, and drops Jerusalem into the middle of the list. The message is that God sees all of it. He doesn't care about the poor in Philistia any less than the powerful in Babylon, and his own people get no exemption. Kingdoms built on oppression collapse because they can't last and because God works to dismantle them. Human choices and God's action are tangled together like play-dough that won't separate again. Prophets speak with inspired but human vision, and Jesus completes the picture. For us the question isn't which nation or team to back. It's whether we're on the side of shalom, celebrating it wherever it shows up and listening to the warning when we're the ones working against it.
