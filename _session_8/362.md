### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** BEMA 362: Heschel and the Prophetic Pathos  
**Hosts:** Marty Solomon and Brent Billings, with Reed Dent  
**Focus:** An introduction to Isaiah (Isaiah 1–39 and 40–66, with references to Isaiah 5, Isaiah 7 and the Suffering Servant passages) through Abraham Joshua Heschel's *The Prophets*

This episode opens Season 8 and the start of a long series on Isaiah. The episode is not a verse-by-verse study. Instead, the hosts first set up a way to read the prophets, using Abraham Joshua Heschel's classic two-part work *The Prophets* as a guide. They explain why Isaiah still matters after studying Jesus. They name the two common mistakes scholars make when reading the prophets: reading them only as predictions of the future, or picking the text apart as literature with no life left in it. Heschel's main idea is **pathos**. A prophet is someone who shares God's own feelings about what is happening in the world, and who also stands with the people. Along the way the hosts cover the history around Isaiah, the phrase "few are guilty, all are responsible," God's wrath as something meant to correct rather than destroy, and how the prophets join personal moral responsibility to social responsibility.

### Key Takeaways

- Isaiah is the book Jesus quotes most from the Prophets (*Nevi'im*). Jesus also quotes Psalms most from the Writings (*Ketuvim*) and Deuteronomy most from the Torah. Coming to Jesus does not make Isaiah unnecessary. Isaiah helps us understand Jesus.
- Most books on the prophets fall into one of two traps. Fundamentalist readings treat the prophets mainly as forecasts of the future, seen through evangelical theology. Liberal textual-critical readings analyze the text as literature but do not take it seriously as a word from God.
- Heschel's phrase **"few are guilty, all are responsible"** sums up the prophetic call. Prophetic words may target people with power, but every listener is invited to see their own part in what is wrong.
- The prophet speaks **with** the people, not down at them. Their anger comes from compassion and solidarity, not from feeling morally superior.
- **Pathos** is the center of Heschel's view. The prophet feels what God feels (grief, longing, anger, disappointment) and speaks out of that shared experience. In Heschel's words, "the prophet is a man who feels fiercely."
- In Isaiah's day, Ahaz assumed that powerful kings were shaping history. Isaiah insists that **God is in charge of history**, so "what seemed a terror to Ahaz was a trifle to Isaiah."
- Both scarcity and abundance can lead people to forget God. Fear pushes us to grab for false security, and comfort leads us to forget where our blessings came from.
- God's **wrath is meant to correct, not destroy**. It is something God feels in response to events, not God's basic nature. There is a difference between "an angry God" and "a God who gets angry."
- **"No word is God's last word."** Even words of judgment are followed by a future and hope.
- The prophets hold together **personal moral responsibility** and **social responsibility** (and also **idolatry** and **injustice**). These are not competing concerns but two sides of the same faithfulness.

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. Why Return to Isaiah?

Brent asks the question many listeners may have: if we've already reached Jesus, and we know he fulfilled Isaiah, why go back? The hosts answer that the Bible is an ongoing conversation that you keep wrestling with. Marty says that after reading his whole library on the prophets, Isaiah and Hosea were the only two prophets where he felt there was still more to say.

- **The series plan:** Not verse by verse. A John-style approach would take about three years, since Isaiah has roughly three times as many chapters. The plan is to go one layer deeper than Session 2, spending two or three episodes on each major section.
- **Jesus' most-quoted books, one per section of the Tanakh:**
  1. Torah: Deuteronomy
  2. Nevi'im (Prophets): Isaiah
  3. Ketuvim (Writings): Psalms

#### 2. Two Common Mistakes in Reading the Prophets

| Approach | Tendency | Problem |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Conservative / fundamentalist | Reads the prophets as predicting the future, through evangelical theology | Misses what the prophets meant in their own Old Testament setting |
| Liberal / textual-critical | Treats Scripture only as literature to dissect | Useful insights, but leaves the text "lifeless" and doesn't take it seriously as theology from God |

Marty mentions theologian **Dr. Ephraim Radner**, who urges academic readers to "take Scripture seriously." That is the middle path the series is aiming for.

#### 3. Heschel's *The Prophets*: How the Book Is Built

The book is about 630 pages and was first published in two volumes.

- **Part One** starts with "What Manner of Man Is the Prophet?" Reed calls it one of the best chapters of scholarship he has ever read because it has "a fire" in it. Part One then works through Amos, Hosea, Isaiah (split into First and Second Isaiah), Micah, Jeremiah and Habakkuk. It closes with chapters on history, chastisement and justice.
- **Part Two** (about pages 285–627) examines pathos theologically and philosophically. It covers anthropopathy and wrath, then takes apart common ideas of what prophecy is, and ends with inspiration, "event and experience," and "prophet, priest, and king."
- Subheadings from the opening chapter that Marty enjoys include "Luminous and Explosive," "The Highest Good," "One Octave Too High," "The Prophet Is an Iconoclast," "Austerity and Compassion," "Sweeping Allegations," and **"Few Are Guilty, All Are Responsible."**

#### 4. How Isaiah Is Divided

- Heschel follows the common academic split: **First Isaiah (chapters 1–39)** and **Second Isaiah (chapters 40–66)**.
- In Session 2, Marty described *four voices* in Isaiah. Scholars argue about how many authors or editors there were. Nearly everyone agrees on the 1–39 / 40–66 break, and almost everything else is disputed.

#### 5. Solidarity

Solidarity runs in more than one direction:

- **Prophet with people:** The prophet counts himself among those who are guilty. He speaks "as one of you," not like a street preacher pointing fingers.
- **Prophet with God:** The prophet shares God's sorrow. Heschel even talks about the prophet's *sympathy toward God*. Marty admits he first disliked this idea theologically.
- **A priest-like role:** The prophet stands in the gap between God and the people, at a specific moment in history.
- Brent adds a point from the *Cultural Backgrounds Study Bible*. Prophets in neighboring nations usually spoke only to the king. Israel's prophets spoke to the **whole nation**.

#### 6. First Isaiah in Its Historical Setting

- **Judah's situation:** Unlike the northern kingdom (Israel and Samaria), Judah had not been as shaped by commercial wealth gaps. Heschel warns against **presentism**, which means assuming Isaiah was talking about the same problems we face today.
- **A turning point:** Heschel calls this "the most critical moment in Judah's history to date." Samaria was doomed, and Judah's future was uncertain.
- **The crisis under Ahaz (Isaiah 7):** Pekah of Israel and Rezin of Aram had formed an alliance against Judah, and Assyria under Tiglath-Pileser III loomed behind them. Isaiah calls Ahaz to believe that none of these powers controls history: "The world was in the hands of God."
- **The two-sided prophetic message:**
  - *In despair:* God has not forgotten you.
  - *In celebration* (for example, the later Hezekiah stories): Be warned, because you don't see what's really going on.
- **The prophetic warning:** If you don't trust the story, you will not last. Your current lack of trust can't hold up.

#### 7. God as Wounded Family, Not a "Holiness Whack-a-Mole"

Reed contrasts two pictures of how God responds to sin:

- **Holiness whack-a-mole:** God waits over the board with a divine hammer and smashes each sin as it pops up. It is mechanical and binary.
- **The prophetic picture:** God is like a **spurned husband** or a **father dealing with children who won't listen**. God says "I love you so much" and also "you are driving me crazy." God is grieved, sad and disappointed. In effect: "I thought you would listen to me, and you didn't."

#### 8. Second Isaiah: Pathos in a New Setting

- Now the people are in **Babylonian exile** and have already suffered. The pathos takes a different form.
- The prophet is confused that God hasn't acted: "We've experienced your discipline. Where is your rescue?"
- God reminds the prophet of his place. Heschel's subheading "Who taught Him the path of justice?" sounds like Job.
- **The Suffering Servant:** Heschel, as a Jewish scholar, doesn't read the passage as being about Jesus. He still offers real insight. Is the servant Isaiah himself or the people of God? The answer is **both**. The Ethiopian eunuch in Acts 8 asks the same question: "Is the prophet speaking about himself or someone else?"

#### 9. History, Chastisement and Justice

- **History:** As Radner puts it, the prophets define history. They interpret what is happening through the lens of prophetic pathos.
- **Chastisement in three movements:**
  1. **Failure of freedom:** God gave freedom, as in the vineyard of Isaiah 5 that God planted, cleared and walled, but the people misused it.
  2. **Suspension of freedom:** God takes that freedom away for a time.
  3. **A future:** "No word is God's last word." Hope always follows.
- **Justice:** *Mishpat* (justice) and *tzedakah* (righteousness) belong together.

#### 10. Two Pairs That Are Not Really Opposites

- **Personal moral responsibility vs. social responsibility.** Today these are usually split between conservative and progressive politics. The prophets join them in a vertical and horizontal whole. Marty asks how you could pursue social responsibility without personal morality, and how personal morality could be real if it produced no social good.
- **Idolatry vs. injustice.** Some say the world's biggest problem is idolatry, and others say it is injustice. The prophets treat them as cause and effect: what you worship shapes how you treat others. Reed says Walter Brueggemann's *The Prophetic Imagination* will be covered next episode. Marty recalls that in Session 2 he set up an "A or B" frame on purpose, only to show later that "it's both."

#### 11. Pathos and Anthropopathy

- **Pathos:** Appealing to or arising from emotion. It is not irrational, but it is "other than rational."
- **Anthropopathy:** Attributing human feelings to a non-human being, here God. It is related to anthropomorphism, which attributes human *physical* traits. This is what is going on when God is pictured as a disappointed father or a spurned lover.
- **Wrath** (the meaning and mystery of wrath): God's anger is occasional, not part of God's core nature. It drives God to set things right. A God who never got angry at injustice is "not a God I would want," Reed says. God's anger does not last forever. God's love and mercy do.

#### 12. What Prophecy Is Not

Heschel rules out several common explanations:

- Ecstasy or a trance state
- Simple poetic inspiration (only human art coming to life)
- Psychosis

He then gives his own account of **inspiration** rooted in "event and experience," meaning specific times and places, not abstract messages from the heavens.

#### 13. Marty's Summary Definition of a Prophet

A prophet is someone who **experiences God's own reality**: grief, lament, anger, frustration, and seeing injustice clearly. The prophet stands in solidarity with both God and the people at a particular moment in history. Prophecy is not learned or watched from a distance. It is felt from the inside.

### Examples & Applications

- **Reed's multi-title sermon:** Reed's CCF sermon tracing the history behind Isaiah had a first title that began "You were afraid. You made the moment everything…," a second title "Prophecy Needs Place," and a third "Dear Judah, Dear Truman." It shows a pastoral way to give college students something in Isaiah's history they can relate to.
- **Amos and Isaiah back to back:** At CCF, a sermon on Amos (a time of prosperity) followed one on Isaiah (a time of threat). Emily, who preached one of them, pointed out that scarcity and abundance both lead people to set God aside. In abundance we forget who provided. In scarcity we reach for "Assyria, please help us."
- **Parenting:** Every honest parent knows the difference between a parent who *gets* angry and an *angry parent*. That difference helps explain God's wrath as something meant to correct.
- **Martin Luther King Jr.:** Reed calls King a modern prophet. Speeches like "I Have a Dream" and "I've Been to the Mountaintop" moved a nation through pathos, not logical proof. Marty adds that the famous lines can't be separated from the sharp, practical social critique around them. Prophecy is neither raw data nor just poetry.
- **Brent's Session 2 notebook:** Brent had a spot in his notes for every prophet to record "Story A or Story B." It became a funny example of how the prophets refuse to fit either-or categories.
- **Political idolatry today:** Readers are encouraged to notice when their own social or political loyalties lead them to stress only personal morality or only social justice. Isaiah calls for both.

### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

- **Heschel's *The Prophets*:** Read especially "What Manner of Man Is the Prophet?" and the chapters on pathos and wrath.
- **Walter Brueggemann's *The Prophetic Imagination*:** The subject of the next episode, including the link between idolatry and injustice.
- **The authorship of Isaiah:** Compare the First/Second Isaiah view, the Third Isaiah (56–66) proposal, and Marty's "four voices" from Session 2.
- **The Syro-Ephraimite crisis (Isaiah 7–8; 2 Kings 16):** Study Ahaz, Pekah, Rezin and Tiglath-Pileser III.
- **The Suffering Servant songs (Isaiah 42, 49, 50, 52–53) and Acts 8:** Compare Jewish and Christian readings of the servant as an individual, as the people, or both.
- **Anthropopathy and divine impassibility:** How do Heschel's ideas fit with classic Christian teaching that God does not suffer?
- **Job and Second Isaiah:** Compare the "Who taught Him the path of justice?" theme with God's speeches in Job 38–41.
- **Mishpat and tzedakah:** Look at how the two words appear together across the prophets.
- **Hosea:** Marty mentions a possible future series and the strong marriage imagery in that book.
- **Ephraim Radner's work:** How the prophets shape a theological understanding of history.

### Comprehension Questions

1. What two opposite mistakes does Marty find in most writing on the prophets, and why is each one inadequate?
2. Explain Heschel's phrase "few are guilty, all are responsible." How does it change the way a modern reader should approach a prophetic book like Isaiah?
3. According to Heschel, what is **pathos**, and how does it shape the prophet's relationship with both God and the people?
4. How did Isaiah challenge King Ahaz's view of history during the threat from Pekah, Rezin and Assyria? How does the prophetic message differ in times of despair and times of celebration?
5. Describe Heschel's three movements of chastisement (failure of freedom, suspension of freedom and a future). How does the phrase "no word is God's last word" connect to them?

### Personalized Summary

This episode explains why Isaiah is worth slowing down for. The prophets were not mainly fortune-tellers, and they were not just literary artists. They were people who **felt what God feels** and spoke from inside that experience, standing with God and with the people. Heschel's idea of pathos changes the picture of God from a cold judge with a hammer to a grieving parent or wounded spouse. On that picture, wrath is real but meant to correct, and judgment is never the final word. The hosts also push against two easy divisions in our own culture: personal morality vs. social justice, and idolatry vs. injustice. The prophets insist on both, because what we worship shapes how we live together. Whether we are in fear or in comfort, Isaiah asks the same question it asked Ahaz: will we trust that history is in God's hands, even when what we see suggests otherwise?
