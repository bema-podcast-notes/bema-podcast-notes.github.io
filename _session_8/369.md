### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** 369: The Choice Is Yours  
**Hosts:** Marty Solomon and Brent Billings  
**Focus:** Isaiah 24–27 (the so-called "Isaiah Apocalypse"), with connections to Genesis 9 (the Noahic covenant) and Isaiah 5 (the Song of the Vineyard)

In this episode, Marty and Brent read all four chapters of Isaiah 24–27, verse by verse, with only light commentary. The previous episode covered the "burdens," the oracles against the nations in Isaiah 13–23. These chapters work as a capstone to that section. Isaiah 24 describes the whole earth laid waste because humanity has broken the "everlasting covenant." Right after that comes an outburst of praise, a promised banquet for all peoples, a song of trust, resurrection imagery, the defeat of Leviathan, and a renewed vineyard. Marty argues that these passages of hope are not random sprinkles of optimism. They belong to the same "day" as the desolation. Isaiah is asking his listeners how they will respond to destruction: with despair, or by planting their feet in hope. As the title says, the choice is yours.

### Key Takeaways

- **Isaiah 24 is a capstone to the oracles against the nations.** After ten chapters of judgment on Egypt, Babylon, Philistia, Moab, Jerusalem and others, the prophet zooms out: the *whole earth* will be laid waste, and Jerusalem gets no exception.
- **Judgment erases every social category.** Priest and people, master and servant, buyer and seller, lender and borrower all face the same fate. Status pyramids collapse.
- **The "everlasting covenant" probably points back to Noah.** Genesis 9:16 calls God's covenant with all living creatures an "everlasting covenant." That makes the indictment universal, not just about Israel breaking the Sinai covenant.
- **For the oppressed, judgment is good news.** Isaiah 25 praises God *because* the ruthless city has fallen. God has been "a refuge for the poor" and "a shelter from the storm."
- **The hope passages belong to the same "day" as the desolation.** "In that day" comes up again and again. Isaiah is speaking from the smoldering rubble, not years later.
- **Something has to die for something new to live.** Isaiah shows a death-and-resurrection pattern well before the New Testament: "Your dead will live… their bodies will rise."
- **Don't rush to make the passage only about the far future.** Reading these verses only as distant prophecy can drown out what Isaiah was saying to his own listeners.
- **We read the Bible as if it is always about some other time.** Brent suggests this habit is part of why Scripture can feel hard to apply.
- **God hasn't given up on the vineyard.** Isaiah 27 reverses the ruined vineyard of Isaiah 5. God guards it day and night, and Israel will "fill all the world with fruit."
- **Hope takes courage.** Paraphrasing Rabbi Jonathan Sacks, Marty describes courage as looking despair in the face and saying, "Hand me a shovel."
- **God gathers people one by one.** God's care reaches whole nations and also each individual. No one is outside it.

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. Isaiah 24: The Earth Laid Waste

- **The universal scope:** "See, the Lord is going to lay waste the earth and devastate it." This sums up everything said against individual nations in chapters 13–23.
- **Social levelling (24:2):** The prophet lists pairs of people who will share the same fate:
  1. Priest and people
  2. Master and servant
  3. Mistress and maidservant
  4. Seller and buyer
  5. Borrower and lender
  6. Debtor and creditor
- **An unusual phrase:** The section ends with "The Lord has spoken this word" instead of the usual "says the Lord." Marty notes that scholars who hold the multiple-author view might point to phrasing like this. Brent mentions Greg Boyd's idea of "cracks" in the text where God's true character shows through.
- **The reason for the judgment (24:5):** "They have disobeyed the laws, violated the statutes and broken the everlasting covenant."
- **The joy goes silent:** The wine dries up, the timbrels and harps stop, the beer turns bitter, and the city lies desolate.

#### 2. The Everlasting Covenant and Genesis 9

- Brent raises the obvious question: didn't God promise in Genesis 9 never to destroy the earth again?
- Marty's quip: "The prophets would be real big on the fine print of Genesis." The promise was about never again destroying the earth *by flood*.
- **Why it matters:** The first "everlasting covenant" in Scripture is the one with Noah and *all creation* (Genesis 9:16). So the indictment covers every nation, not only Israel under Torah. All of humanity has broken a covenant God made with the whole created order.

#### 3. A Strange Interruption of Joy (24:14–16)

- Voices from the west, the east and the islands "shout for joy" and give "glory to the Righteous One."
- The text doesn't say who is singing. Marty suggests it could be:
  - Creation itself, groaning under injustice and the absence of *shalom*
  - The oppressed and marginalized, who have suffered constant exploitation
- **The prophet's own reaction is different:** "I waste away, I waste away! Woe to me!" Isaiah stands in solidarity with the guilty and sees himself as part of the problem.
- The chapter ends with God punishing "the powers in the heavens above and the kings on the earth below" and reigning on Mount Zion.

#### 4. Isaiah 25: Praise in the Rubble

- **The surprise:** After the despair of chapter 24, chapter 25 opens with "Lord, you are my God; I will exalt you."
- **The reason for the praise:** "You have made the city a heap of rubble." To people crushed by a ruthless city, its fall means freedom.
- **Marty's reading:** This is not a burst of hope out of nowhere, written years later. Isaiah is speaking *in the moment* of chapter 24's desolation. God's judgment is exactly what the people at the bottom of the "power pyramid" need.
- **The banquet on the mountain (25:6–8):**
  - A feast of rich food and aged wine for *all peoples*
  - The "shroud that enfolds all peoples" is destroyed
  - "He will swallow up death forever"
  - "The Sovereign Lord will wipe away the tears from all faces"
- **Theological point:** In Isaiah's view, wrath is part of God's *redemptive* work. To make the world right, the unjust order has to end.

#### 5. "In That Day": Hope and Desolation Tied Together

- The phrase "in that day" repeats through chapters 25–27 (roughly seven times by Brent's count).
- **Which day?** The same day of judgment, "as the smoke is still rising" over the ruins.
- "Surely this is our God; we trusted in him, and he saved us" (25:9). Brent points out that Judah's leaders ran before the sword arrived. Marty concludes that the speakers here are the oppressed, not the elites.
- Moab, the symbol of pride, gets trampled "as straw is trampled down in the manure."

#### 6. Isaiah 26: A Pastoral Invitation

- **The tone shifts:** Chapter 25 sounds like an observer breaking into song. Chapter 26 is *instructive and pastoral*. It speaks directly to the reader.
- **Key lines:**
  - "You will keep in perfect peace those whose minds are steadfast, because they trust in you" (26:3)
  - "He lays the lofty city low… Feet trample it down—the feet of the oppressed, the footsteps of the poor" (26:5–6)
  - "When your judgments come upon the earth, the people of the world learn righteousness" (26:9)
- **The choice:** Faced with desolation, a person can:
  1. Despair, and stop investing in a world that is falling apart, or
  2. See the destruction as God clearing the ground to rebuild the *shalom*-filled world humanity failed to build, and plant their feet in hope.
- **The evidence (26:13–15):** "Other lords besides you have ruled over us… They are now dead." Marty borrows Dr. Phil's line, "How's that working out for you?" Empire-building doesn't last, and history shows it.

#### 7. Resurrection Language in Its Own Setting (26:19)

- "But your dead will live, Lord; their bodies will rise… the earth will give birth to her dead."
- The labor image: "We writhed in labor, but we gave birth to wind." Human effort produced nothing. Only God brings life.
- **Marty's caution:** Don't jump straight to the final resurrection and miss the "big thunderous statement" Isaiah is making to *his own* people. God's covenant story will go on. There will be a remnant. God "loves to make beautiful things out of dead things."
- "Enter your rooms and shut the doors… until his wrath has passed by." This echoes Passover and Noah's ark, a sheltering in place while judgment passes.

#### 8. Isaiah 27: Leviathan, the Vineyard, and the Harvest

- **Leviathan slain (27:1):** God kills "the gliding serpent… the monster of the sea." This is chaos-monster imagery like that in Job and elsewhere in the ancient Near East. Read it as poetry: God puts to death the evils that seem impossible to defeat.
- **The vineyard reversed (27:2–6):**

| Isaiah 5 (Song of the Vineyard) | Isaiah 27 (Song of the Fruitful Vineyard) |
|---|---|
| God tears down the hedge and wall | God watches over it and guards it day and night |
| Briars and thorns take over | "If only there were briars and thorns… I would march against them" |
| The vineyard yields bad fruit | "Israel will bud and blossom and fill all the world with fruit" |
| God withholds rain | "I water it continually" |

- **"I am not angry… let them make peace with me" (27:4–5):** Brent sees this as another moment where God's heart breaks through. Even the "briars and thorns" are invited to make peace.
- **No more idolatry (27:9):** Asherah poles and incense altars will be crushed like limestone. Everything is back in its proper place.
- **The harvest (27:12–13):** God threshes "from the flowing Euphrates to the Wadi of Egypt," and Israel is "gathered up one by one." A great trumpet calls the exiles in Assyria and Egypt home to worship in Jerusalem.

#### 9. The Prophetic Picture of Shalom

Marty says the prophets keep coming back to one picture of the world to come, which is more about everything being in its proper place than about "heaven and hell." Examples:

- Everyone under their own vine and fig tree (Micah 4:4)
- Swords beaten into plowshares (Isaiah 2:4)
- The wolf lying down with the lamb (Isaiah 11:6)
- A banquet for all peoples (Isaiah 25:6)

These images are less literal forecasts and more poetic pictures of what happens when people say yes to God's invitation.

### Examples & Applications

- **Reading from inside the devastation:** Marty suggests that someone in war-torn Ukraine would grasp Isaiah 24–27 right away. To a person whose world has collapsed, these chapters are an invitation, not a puzzle about the future.
- **"Hand me a shovel":** Paraphrasing Rabbi Jonathan Sacks, Marty says it takes courage to build a new world in the middle of destruction. Think of people rebuilding after a hurricane or a war. They aren't naive about the damage, but they choose to start digging.
- **Personal applications:**
  - When an institution, career or relationship falls apart, ask: "Is something dying so that something truer can live?"
  - Notice who hears "judgment" as good news. People suffering under injustice often long for God to set things right.
  - Practice reading Scripture as speaking to its *own* moment first, then let that shape how it speaks to ours.
- **Brent's hermeneutical insight:** Many Christians read most of the Bible as "setting the table" for something later. That can make Scripture feel distant and irrelevant to everyday life. Reading prophets in their own setting brings them back to life.
- **Agricultural images:** Harvest and threshing were part of daily life in the ancient world. Jesus picks up the same images in his parables of wheat and weeds (Matthew 13), and John the Baptist uses them for the threshing floor (Matthew 3:12). Both draw directly on prophets like Isaiah.
- **Individual dignity:** "You, Israel, will be gathered up one by one" (27:12). God's attention to nations doesn't swallow up the individual. Every person matters.

### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

1. **The "Isaiah Apocalypse":** Scholars often call chapters 24–27 by this name. Look into how they compare with later Jewish apocalyptic literature such as Daniel and 1 Enoch.
2. **The Noahic covenant in the Prophets:** Trace how Genesis 9 shapes prophetic ideas of universal accountability, for example in Amos 1–2 and Jeremiah 33:20–25.
3. **Leviathan and chaos-monster imagery:** Compare Isaiah 27:1 with Job 41, Psalm 74:13–14, Psalm 104:26 and the Ugaritic Baal texts that mention Lotan.
4. **Isaiah 25:8 in the New Testament:** Study how Paul (1 Corinthians 15:54) and John (Revelation 7:17; 21:4) reuse "He will swallow up death" and "wipe away every tear."
5. **Isaiah 26:19 and resurrection hope:** Look at how the idea of bodily resurrection developed in the Hebrew Bible, comparing Ezekiel 37 and Daniel 12:2.
6. **The vineyard motif:** Follow it from Isaiah 5 and 27 to Psalm 80, Jeremiah 2:21, Jesus' parable of the tenants (Mark 12) and "I am the vine" (John 15).
7. **Authorship debates:** Explore why some scholars think Isaiah had several authors, and how unusual phrases like "The Lord has spoken this word" feed into that discussion.
8. **Greg Boyd's "cruciform hermeneutic":** Brent alludes to Boyd's approach to violent portrayals of God. *The Crucifixion of the Warrior God* is the place to start.
9. **Rabbi Jonathan Sacks on hope and courage:** Read Sacks' writings on hope versus optimism, for example *Celebrating Life* and his essays on rebuilding after catastrophe.

### Comprehension Questions

1. Why does Marty see Isaiah 24 as a "capstone" to the previous ten chapters, and what does the list of paired social roles in 24:2 tell us about God's judgment?
2. What is the "everlasting covenant" in Isaiah 24:5 most likely referring to, and why does the Noahic covenant make the indictment universal?
3. According to Marty, why does Isaiah break into praise in chapter 25 right after describing a ruined city? Who might be speaking in "we trusted in him, and he saved us"?
4. How does the repeated phrase "in that day" change the way we read the hope passages in Isaiah 25–27?
5. How does Isaiah 27:2–6 reverse the Song of the Vineyard in Isaiah 5, and what does this say about God's commitment to God's people?

### Personalized Summary

This episode changes how you read the bright spots in the prophets. It's easy to treat Isaiah's hopeful passages as small comforts dropped between pages of doom, or as predictions about some distant future. Marty and Brent show that Isaiah 24–27 holds together. The same "day" that brings the devastation of the earth also brings a banquet for all peoples, the defeat of death, the slaying of Leviathan and a vineyard that fills the world with fruit. For the poor and oppressed, the collapse of ruthless powers is good news. For everyone, the destruction is an invitation to choose. You can give in to despair, or you can trust that God makes beautiful things out of dead things and plant your feet in hope. Brent's observation is worth keeping too: the Bible gets more alive when we let it speak to its own moment, and to ours, instead of always pushing its meaning into the future. Hope like this takes courage, the kind that looks at the rubble and says, "Hand me a shovel."
