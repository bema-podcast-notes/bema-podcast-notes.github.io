### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** 370: Isaiah 28–33, God's Message to His Own People  
**Hosts:** Marty Solomon and Brent Billings  
**Focus:** Isaiah 28–33, with connections to Micah 5:2–5 and Revelation's letter to Smyrna

In this Session Eight episode, Marty and Brent go back to Isaiah. They read selected passages from chapters 28 through 33 rather than going verse by verse. In these chapters God turns from the nations to speak directly to his own covenant people. Marty starts by naming three pitfalls in reading the prophets. Then the hosts follow a thread through the five chapters: pride that blinds, stubbornness that runs to "Egypt" for safety, God's steady commitment to mercy, and a call to choose where our allegiance lies. The episode keeps setting worldly power (horses, chariots, alliances, winning) against God's upside-down kingdom, which grows through humility, self-giving, and becoming "a shelter from the wind" for others.

### Key Takeaways

- **There are three pitfalls in reading the prophets.** We can study them only from a cold academic distance. We can use them as weapons against other people. Or we can take every word personally and forget the original author and audience. Good reading keeps the historical context and still lets the text change us.
- **Pride blinds us (Isaiah 28).** The "drunkards of Ephraim" stand for people who should know better. They are overconfident and indulgent, and no longer watchful about what God is doing.
- **God's people need God to be their crown.** Their own ideology, power, or strategy cannot be. "In that day the Lord Almighty will be a glorious crown" (28:5).
- **There are two "cities of David."** Bethlehem, where David came from, is small and humble (Micah 5). Jerusalem ("Ariel"), where David settled, is powerful and proud (Isaiah 29). God works through the first kind of story.
- **Running to "Egypt" never really works.** Conventional wisdom says Egypt never fails, but it fails again and again. We keep going back to the things that have enslaved us.
- **Communities share responsibility for silencing truth.** People who tell the seers "see no more visions" are as guilty as leaders who stay quiet (30:10).
- **God's discipline serves his mercy.** "The Lord longs to be gracious to you" (30:18). His judgment is part of his restorative justice (*mishpat*). It is not wrath for its own sake.
- **Westerners read the Bible from the side that holds the chariots.** As citizens of a superpower, American readers have to remember that the prophets spoke to people living under the power structures.
- **The kingdom comes through participation.** "A king will reign in righteousness and rulers will rule with justice" (32:1). God's people are the "rulers" who become shelter, refuge, and streams in the desert.
- **The final vision is a tent.** Zion is pictured as "a tent that will not be moved" (33:20). The image looks back to humbler days before the temple and empire.

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. A Healthy Way to Read the Prophets

Marty describes three common errors:

1. **Academic distance.** We look only at the historical Isaiah, his context, and his audience, and never ask how the text should change us.
2. **Weaponizing.** People with "prophetic personalities" spot modern empires and ideologies and aim the prophets' curses at others.
3. **Total internalization.** We forget the original setting and hear every word of condemnation as God speaking directly to us.

The goal is to keep the contextual discipline of the first approach and the awareness of the second, and to practice the third in a *healthy* way. Brent adds that confirmation bias and our own emotional state make this hard. He also notes that Session Eight reads Isaiah differently than Session Two did. By now the series has been through Jesus and church history, so it can ask practical questions.

#### 2. Isaiah 28: The Danger of Pride

- **Ephraim** is a poetic name for the northern kingdom of Israel, the seat of northern power. Robert Alter notes that it seems strange here, because the northern kingdom had already fallen to Assyria. Alter suggests that Ephraim's complete desolation is held up as a warning to Judah, which is now making the same mistakes.
- **The "wreath" or "crown"** of Ephraim's drunkards most likely refers to **Samaria**, the northern capital (NET footnote). Marty points out a possible echo in Revelation's letter to Smyrna, a city famous for its "crown" layout and imagery.
- **Drunkenness as a metaphor.** Priests and prophets stagger from wine and beer. When we indulge ourselves we stop being watchful. We assume we already know God's ways, theology, and answers.
- **Verse 10's wordplay.** In Hebrew the verse reads roughly *tsav latsav, tsav latsav, qav laqav, qav laqav*. Many scholars think these are nonsense sounds that mock the prophet's teaching as baby talk. The NET renders it "meaningless gibberish… a syllable here, a syllable there." Alter goes for the spirit of the passage with something closer to "filth, filth, vomit, vomit."
- **The "covenant with death."** Jerusalem's rulers believe their alliances make them safe from the "overwhelming scourge." God says these are lies that make a poor shield, and he will break through their pride to get their attention.

#### 3. Isaiah 29: Ariel and the Two Cities of David

- **Ariel** is a name for Jerusalem that may mean "lion of God." It also plays on "altar hearth" (29:2).
- **"The city where David camped."** The Hebrew root *chanah* means "to camp" (compare *machaneh*, "encampment"). Translations differ: NIV has "settled," and NET has "besieged." That makes for irony, since God says in the next verse that *he* will besiege the city.
- **Juxtaposition with Micah 5.** Bethlehem Ephrathah is "small among the clans of Judah," yet the coming ruler will come from there. David himself was the overlooked youngest brother. Jerusalem is the impressive city where David ended up, and it has grown proud.

| Bethlehem (Micah 5) | Jerusalem/Ariel (Isaiah 29) |
|---|---|
| Where David came from | Where David settled or camped |
| Small, overlooked | Powerful, impressive |
| Humility | Pride |
| Source of the shepherd-ruler | Under siege, speaking "from the dust" |

- Brent connects this to **Deuteronomy's call to remember where you came from**. In Micah's day the people heeded that call and were spared from Assyria. Within a generation they had lost the plot again.
- **Prophecy as diagnosis, not a crystal ball.** The prophet is saying that this path cannot last and showing where it leads.
- **Spiritual blindness.** "Be drunk, but not from wine" (29:9). The systems and roles people trust, including prophets and seers, will not save them.

#### 4. Isaiah 30: An Obstinate Nation

- **"Woe to the obstinate children"** who make alliances "not by my Spirit" and go down to Egypt for "shade." The desert image of shade comes from Session One.
- Going to Egypt made perfect sense politically. It was exactly what God had told his people not to do.
- **Silencing the prophets.** "Tell us pleasant things, prophesy illusions" (30:10). Marty cites a post by Beth Moore: history will judge preachers who failed to speak the truth, and also the congregations and institutions that would not let them. He links this to Abraham Joshua Heschel's line: *"Few are guilty, but all are responsible."*
- **The cracked wall.** The sin will collapse suddenly "like a high wall, cracked and bulging" and shatter like pottery (30:13–14).
- **The turn to mercy (30:18–26).** "Yet the Lord longs to be gracious to you." God's discipline is part of his *mishpat*, his restorative justice. There is always a chance to return (*shuv*). The passage ends with healing: "the Lord binds up the bruises of his people."

#### 5. Isaiah 31: Horses, Chariots, and Allegiance

- "Woe to those who go down to Egypt… who trust in the multitude of their chariots." In modern terms, horses and chariots are **tanks and Humvees**, real social, political, and military power.
- **The Western reading problem.** Isaiah's audience did *not* have chariots. They were tempted to trust in someone else's. American readers belong to the world's largest superpower, so they can put their allegiance in both God and military power at once. Marty calls this a "luxury," and a trap.
- Brent notes that this is not ancient history: tanks are in use right now in Ukraine and Israel.
- Marty is careful not to pass judgment on particular conflicts. Still, he notes that peace kept by alliances and arms sales "doesn't put the world back together." Being the largest arms dealer on the planet is not the way of *shalom*.
- **God as a lion (31:4–5).** A lion over its prey is not frightened by shepherds shouting. God is not intimidated by our superpowers.

#### 6. Isaiah 32: What the Kingdom Looks Like

- "A king will reign in righteousness and rulers will rule with justice" (32:1). This was covered in depth in **Episode 136**.
- **King and rulers.** For Christians the King is Jesus. The "rulers" (plural) are God's people, who take part in the kingdom.
- Each ruler will be "like a shelter from the wind… streams of water in the desert… the shadow of a great rock in a thirsty land" (32:2).
- **What causes healing?** Not the right superpower winning the right war. The eyes of the blind open when God's people actually become refuge, shade, and water for others. In New Testament language, it all hangs on **love**.
- **Two groups.** The righteous (*tsedakah* and *mishpat*) stand against the fools, who "leave the hungry empty" and "destroy the poor with lies." "But the noble make noble plans, and by noble deeds they stand" (32:8).

#### 7. Isaiah 33: The Choice and the Tent

- "Woe to you, destroyer… When you stop destroying, you will be destroyed." Violence comes back on those who use it.
- "The fear of the Lord is the key to this treasure" (33:6).
- **Zion as a tent.** Jerusalem is described as "a peaceful abode, a tent that will not be moved." The people already had a permanent temple, yet the future vision goes back to a tent. That fits the backwards, humble character of God's kingdom: the house David knew, not the house David built.
- **The core question.** Do we believe God's kingdom comes because *we win*: the war, the battle, the power struggle? Or because God builds it through generosity, hospitality, self-sacrifice, mercy, and forgiveness? Marty admits that many of us give God's way lip service while in practice trusting what "works."

### Examples & Applications

- **Social media and memes:** Before posting that punchy meme or hot take, ask whether pride and stubbornness are steering you.
- **Going back to "Egypt" personally:** Brent points out how often we return to the same habits, coping mechanisms, or securities that have hurt or enslaved us, because they promise to deliver.
- **Church culture:** Congregations that reward pleasant preaching and punish prophetic challenge share in the blame. "Few are guilty, all are responsible."
- **Modern geopolitics:** Horses and chariots become tanks, military aid, and strategic alliances. The text asks Western believers where their trust really lies, without making simple judgments about specific conflicts.
- **Practicing "ruler" work:** Each of us can be a "shelter from the wind" in small ways: welcoming a newcomer, giving generously, sticking with someone through hardship, advocating for the poor when their "plea is just."
- **Remembering where you came from:** Like Israel in Deuteronomy, individuals and churches can lose humility within one season of success. Regular habits of remembering, like testimony, Sabbath, and confession, help us stay watchful.

### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

1. **Isaiah 28 and Revelation's letter to Smyrna.** What connections exist between Ephraim's fading crown or wreath and Smyrna's "crown of life" imagery?
2. **The Hebrew of Isaiah 28:10.** Study *tsav latsav, qav laqav* in different translations and commentaries, including Alter, the NET notes, and the NIV notes.
3. **The meaning of "Ariel."** Look at the possible meanings, "lion of God" and "altar hearth," and how Isaiah plays on both.
4. **The authorship of Isaiah.** How do theories of multiple authors (First, Second, and Third Isaiah) affect references like "Ephraim" versus "Israel"?
5. **Micah 5 and the Bethlehem tradition.** Trace the "small city" theme through David's anointing (1 Samuel 16) and the birth stories in Matthew and Luke.
6. **The "Egypt" motif across Scripture.** Follow how going down to Egypt works as a symbol of misplaced trust from Genesis through the prophets.
7. **Episode 136 on Isaiah 32.** Revisit the earlier teaching on "a king and rulers" and the partnership model of the kingdom.
8. **Tent versus temple theology.** Compare the tabernacle, Solomon's temple, Isaiah 33:20, John 1:14 ("tabernacled among us"), and Revelation 21:3.
9. **Abraham Joshua Heschel's *The Prophets*.** Explore his ideas of divine pathos and shared moral responsibility.

### Comprehension Questions

1. What are the three pitfalls Marty describes in studying the prophets, and how does he suggest we balance them?
2. Why does Isaiah 28 address "Ephraim" when the northern kingdom had already fallen, and what warning does this carry for Judah?
3. How does setting Isaiah 29 (Ariel, "the city where David settled") beside Micah 5 (Bethlehem) bring out the difference between pride and humility in God's story?
4. According to Isaiah 30–31, why was going down to Egypt both logical and wrong? Why does Marty say Western readers find this message especially hard to hear?
5. In Isaiah 32:1–2, who are the "rulers," and what does the passage say actually brings healing and restoration to the world?

### Summary

This episode is a hard look at where we place our trust. Marty and Brent read Isaiah 28–33 as God speaking directly to his own people. These chapters warn against pride that blinds, stubbornness that silences the prophets, and alliances that rely on Egypt's horses and chariots instead of on God. The two cities of David frame the contrast. Bethlehem is the humble beginning God loves to use, and Jerusalem is the proud capital that forgot where it came from. Even in the woes, God "longs to be gracious," and his discipline aims at healing. The main invitation comes in Isaiah 32. The King reigns, and his people are called to be shelter, shade, and streams of water for others. The closing image of Zion as an unmovable *tent* sums up the upside-down kingdom. For you as a reader, the challenge is honest self-examination. Do you really believe God's world comes through winning, or through love, mercy, and self-giving? The choice is still yours.
