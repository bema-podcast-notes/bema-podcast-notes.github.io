### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** 374: Isaiah and Tikkun Olam (Isaiah 40–48)  
**Hosts:** Marty Solomon and Brent Billings  
**Focus:** Isaiah 40:1–11; 41:8–20; 42:1–9; 43:1–13; 44:1–5, 21–23; 45:14–17; 46:1–4; 47; 48 (with connections to Matthew 12, John 13–16, Luke 4, and Revelation 18)

This episode starts the second major part of Isaiah (chapters 40–66). Marty calls the first part (chapters 1–39) "a father disciplines his sons": warning, judgment, and woes aimed at a people who would not listen. He titles the second part "Isaiah and Tikkun Olam," using a later Hebrew phrase that means "the repairing of the world." The setting has changed. God's people are no longer being warned about Babylon. They are living in exile and feeling God's discipline, and Isaiah's voice turns to comfort, restoration, and hope. The hosts move quickly through chapters 40–48 and point out a repeated theme: God partners with his servant, who is named again and again as Jacob/Israel. They also trace a set of images of God as Helper, Advocate, Savior, and Counselor, which Marty links to Jesus' teaching on the Holy Spirit as the *paraklētos* in John's Gospel. The episode closes with God's judgment on Babylon, the oppressor (chapter 47), and a warning to "stubborn Israel," the people who had gotten comfortable inside the Babylonian system (chapter 48).

### Key Takeaways

- **Isaiah 40 marks a big shift in tone.** Chapters 1–39 warn of coming discipline. Chapters 40–66 speak to people already in exile: "Comfort, comfort my people… her sin has been paid for."
- **Tikkun Olam ("repairing the world") frames the second half of Isaiah.** God is putting the world back together, and his people are called to take part in that repair.
- **Isaiah keeps naming the servant.** Over and over the text calls the servant "Jacob," "Israel," "my people." The Servant Songs are first a calling for God's people, not only a prediction about one future person.
- **Judgment opens the way to hope.** Because the discipline has happened, restoration is now close.
- **God's word lasts when everything else fades.** People, empires, theologies, and strategies wither like grass. God's word, plan, and love endure (Isaiah 40:8; 55:10–11).
- **Marty uses four images for God in these chapters:** Helper (ch. 41), Advocate (ch. 42), Savior (ch. 43), and Counselor. These match John's description of the Holy Spirit as the *paraklētos*, "the one who comes alongside."
- **"A bruised reed he will not break" describes ministry with no collateral damage.** Matthew 12 says Jesus fulfilled Isaiah 42 by the way he restored hurting people.
- **How God's people suffer makes God visible to the nations.** Faithful endurance under imperial oppression, rather than fighting fire with fire, lets the nations see God (Isaiah 45:14).
- **God speaks to the oppressor too.** Babylon, the proud superpower, is brought down for its arrogance and its cruelty to those placed in its hands (ch. 47).
- **Some Israelites settled comfortably into Babylon.** Chapter 48 confronts those who joined themselves to the empire and calls them to "leave Babylon," a call that Revelation 18:4 picks up later.

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. The Two-Part Structure of Isaiah

- **Isaiah 1–39, "A Father Disciplines His Sons":** warning, woes, and judgment while the people refuse to listen. It ends with the history section about Hezekiah and Sennacherib (chapters 36–39).
- **Isaiah 40–66, "Isaiah and Tikkun Olam":** written to people already "sitting in time-out" in Babylon. The message moves from "destruction is coming" to "your hard service has been completed."
- Marty recalls a guest's point from a couple of episodes earlier: darkness and suffering are real, but God's restoring work is an even greater reality.

#### 2. Tikkun Olam: Repairing the World

- **Meaning:** "Repairing of the world" (Hebrew *tikkun olam*).
- **Origin:** The phrase is not biblical Hebrew. It comes from after the biblical period, from the Talmudic era or perhaps medieval times, and became a widely used idea in Jewish thought.
- **Use in this study:** Marty uses it to sum up Isaiah 40–66. God repairs the world, and his people take part in that repair.
- Brent notes that the show first discussed Tikkun Olam in Episode 192, the introduction to the church history series.

#### 3. Comfort and the Endurance of God's Word (Isaiah 40)

- "Comfort, comfort my people… speak tenderly to Jerusalem." The sin has been paid for, and the glory of the Lord will be revealed.
- "A voice of one calling in the wilderness": prepare a highway, raise up valleys, make mountains low.
- **Grass vs. Word:** "The grass withers and the flowers fall, but the word of our God endures forever."
  - What is temporary: people, kingdoms, empires, ideologies, and our own clever theologies and strategies.
  - What is permanent: God's word, goodness, redemptive plan, and love for creation.
- This theme comes back in Isaiah 55:10–11. Just as rain does not return without watering the earth, God's word "will not return to me empty."
- **The Shepherd image:** God "tends his flock like a shepherd… carries [the lambs] close to his heart."
- **Brent's question about the "high mountain" (40:9):** Marty suggests it refers to Zion. Physically Zion is barely a hill, but in theological thinking it is the highest of all mountains in importance. There may also be echoes of Sinai.

#### 4. The Servant Is Jacob/Israel

Marty highlights the servant passages on purpose, continuing the "servant discourse" covered earlier in the Isaiah series (Episode 64):

| Passage | How the servant is identified |
|---|---|
| Isaiah 41:8 | "But you, Israel, my servant, Jacob, whom I have chosen" |
| Isaiah 43:1 | "He who created you, Jacob, he who formed you, Israel" |
| Isaiah 44:1–2 | "Jacob, my servant, Israel… Jeshurun, whom I have chosen" |
| Isaiah 44:21 | "Remember these things, Jacob, for you, Israel, are my servant" |

- The servant is a **partner**. God does not repair the world from a distance. He works through a people he formed "in the womb" for this purpose.
- The servant's job: "a covenant for the people and a light for the Gentiles" (42:6).
- **Jeshurun ("upright one"):** a poetic name for Israel. Outside Isaiah 44:2 it appears only in Deuteronomy 32:15 ("Jeshurun grew fat and kicked") and 33:5, 26. Marty has heard it used for God's people when they are as they should be, living in God's abundance (*shalom*). He admits there are other explanations.

#### 5. Marty's Four Images: Helper, Advocate, Savior, Counselor

1. **Helper (Isaiah 41):** "Do not fear, for I myself will help you… I will hold you with my righteous right hand." God promises full restoration with creation imagery: rivers on barren heights, springs in valleys, and cedar, acacia, myrtle, and olive trees planted in the desert. Brent stresses that this restoration is complete, not partial.
2. **Advocate (Isaiah 42):** In the ancient world an advocate came alongside someone to plead their case, often in the court of public opinion and community. God says, in effect, "I've got you. I know you've made mistakes, but I'll take your hand and walk beside you."
3. **Savior (Isaiah 43):** "I, even I, am the Lord, and apart from me there is no savior."
4. **Counselor:** Marty includes this in the list when he makes the connection to John.

#### 6. Isaiah 42 and Jesus (Matthew 12:15–21)

- Isaiah 42:1–4: "Here is my servant… A bruised reed he will not break, and a smoldering wick he will not snuff out."
- Marty first guessed out loud that Jesus applied this to John the Baptist. When they looked it up, the passage was Matthew 12, where *Matthew* says Jesus' healing ministry fulfills Isaiah 42. Marty's own lesson: "stick to the notes."
- **Context in Matthew 12:** It comes right after the disciples pick grain on the Sabbath and Jesus heals the man with the shriveled hand while confronting the religious leaders.
- **Meaning:** Jesus brings the kingdom without harming people who are already hurting.
- **Application:** Marty asks whether our churches and ministries are built to live out this same calling.

#### 7. Messianic Prophecy and the Call to God's People

- Marty pushes back on reading these passages *only* as predictions about Jesus. Doing that misses the practical, immediate call to God's people.
- He affirms that the passages carry messianic overtones and that Jesus fulfilled them perfectly. But the call Jesus gave his own disciples is also a call to us.
- A favorite saying Marty quotes (author unknown to him): **"Jesus didn't go to the cross so that I don't have to. Jesus went to the cross to show me how to."**
- **Connection to John 14:** "I am the way" means more than "I am that thing." It also means "I'm showing you how to do it," and the way Jesus was about to show them was the way of faithful suffering.

#### 8. The Isaiah–John Parallel

| Isaiah | John |
|---|---|
| Two major parts (1–39, 40–66) | Two major parts: Book of Signs (ch. 1–12) and Book of Glory (ch. 13–21) |
| A large discourse about God's servant | A large discourse about the Holy Spirit (ch. 13–16) |
| God as Helper, Advocate, Savior, Counselor to his suffering servant | The Spirit as *paraklētos*: Helper, Counselor, Advocate, "one who comes alongside" |

- Relevant BEMA episodes on John: 289, 290, and 291.
- Marty's conclusion: sometimes God calls us to suffer well. In doing so we become the servant God formed, and outsiders can see God in *how* we suffer.

#### 9. Reversal and Faithful Suffering (Isaiah 45:14–17)

- Egypt, Cush, and the Sabeans come to Israel saying, "Surely God is with you… Truly you are a God who has been hiding himself."
- **Role reversal:** The people who were in chains will be approached by the nations.
- Marty notes an echo of Isaac in Genesis, where outsiders recognize that God is with him.
- **Key point:** The nations see God because of *how* Israel endured exile, not by trading sword for sword.
- God had stripped Israel of its own power. They had been trying to go toe-to-toe with Assyria and Babylon and making alliances with Egypt. Taking that away let them show the way of the kingdom.
- **Marty's caveat:** This is not a teaching about individual trauma or a reason to stay in abuse. It is about a nation under imperial oppression.

#### 10. Idols That Must Be Carried vs. a God Who Carries (Isaiah 46)

- "Bel bows down, Nebo stoops low." Babylon's gods have to be loaded onto beasts of burden and go into captivity themselves.
- God, by contrast, says: "I have made you and I will carry you… even to your old age and gray hairs."

#### 11. God's Message to the Oppressor (Isaiah 47)

- Marty notes that by now the exiles would be asking, "When will this happen? Babylon is still here." So the message becomes more practical.
- "Go down, sit in the dust, Virgin Daughter Babylon." The "queen of kingdoms" is humbled.
- **The charge against Babylon:** "I gave them into your hand, and you showed them no mercy." Babylon went beyond being God's instrument of discipline and abused the people placed in its hands.
- **Babylon's arrogance:** "I am, and there is none besides me." The empire claims a phrase that belongs to God.
- Sorceries, astrologers, and stargazers cannot save it. "These are not coals for warmth."
- **Context matters:** This message is aimed at the proud superpower at the top of the power pyramid, the one exploiting others and selling captives into slavery. It is not an abstract statement.

#### 12. Stubborn Israel (Isaiah 48)

- Addressed to those who "take oaths in the name of the Lord… but not in truth or righteousness."
- **Historical note:** Not every exile was at the bottom of the power pyramid. Babylon drew some upward to pull them into its way of life.
- "Your neck muscles were iron; your forehead was bronze." God foretold events so Israel could not credit idols.
- "I have refined you, though not as silver; I have tested you in the furnace of affliction."
- "The Lord's chosen ally will carry out his purpose against Babylon." Commentators widely identify this ally as Cyrus of Persia, who is named in Isaiah 44:28–45:1.
- "If only you had paid attention… your peace would have been like a river."
- **"Leave Babylon, flee from the Babylonians!"** Revelation 18:4 echoes this: "Come out of her, my people." Marty remarks that John clearly loved Isaiah.

### Examples & Applications

- **Familiar passages trigger an automatic response:** Brent describes an almost physical reaction to Isaiah 9:6 ("For to us a child is born"). Many people could finish that line, yet hardly anyone knows the verses around it. Isaiah 40–66 has many such passages, including "Comfort, comfort my people," "a voice calling in the wilderness," and "a bruised reed." The challenge is to hear them in context.
- **Hostage exchanges and Isaiah 43:3–4:** Brent recalls a modern prisoner exchange in which one soldier was traded for about a thousand prisoners. Marty is careful to say this is not political commentary. He finds the uneven exchange a helpful picture of "I will give people in exchange for you, nations in exchange for your life," meaning God is willing to give anything for his people.
- **The diaspora as a recurring reality:** Babylon did not send every captive to Babylon. It sold many as slaves on international markets, scattering them north, south, east, and west. Obadiah mentions exiles in Sepharad, which Marty identifies with later Sardis. Isaiah 43:5–6 promises to gather them from every direction. Scattering happened again in the Greco-Roman period and later, and many Jews stayed in Babylon even after the exile ended.
- **"The Holy One of Israel":** This title is characteristic of Isaiah. Brent counts it around a dozen times or more across all of Isaiah, starting in 1:4, compared with only a few uses in 2 Kings, Psalms, Jeremiah, and Ezekiel. Marty notices a possible echo in Luke 4:34, where a demon calls Jesus "the Holy One of God."
- **Ministry without collateral damage:** Churches can ask whether their structures and practices crush "bruised reeds" and snuff out "smoldering wicks," or whether they restore people the way Jesus did.
- **Choosing alliances:** Isaiah 48 invites modern readers to ask what systems ("Babylons") they have joined themselves to and whether they really trust God to be in charge.
- **Suffering well:** Followers of Jesus are invited to see that the *manner* of their faithfulness under hardship can show others who God is. This is not a call to tolerate abuse.

### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

1. **Tikkun Olam in Jewish thought:** How the concept developed from rabbinic literature to medieval Kabbalah to modern Judaism (see BEMA Episode 192).
2. **The Servant Songs:** A full study of Isaiah 42:1–9; 49:1–13; 50:4–11; and 52:13–53:12, looking at how the servant moves between collective Israel and an individual figure.
3. **Paraklētos in John 14–16:** The Greek term and its legal, advocacy, and companionship senses, compared with God's role in Isaiah 40–48 (BEMA Episodes 289–291).
4. **Jeshurun:** Its meaning and use in Deuteronomy 32–33 and Isaiah 44.
5. **"The Holy One of Israel" vs. "The Holy One of God":** Whether Gospel writers deliberately echo Isaiah's distinctive title.
6. **Zion as the "highest mountain":** Mountain theology across Scripture, including Sinai, Zion, and the Mount of Transfiguration.
7. **Cyrus as God's "chosen ally":** The surprising picture of a foreign king as God's anointed instrument (Isaiah 44:28–45:7).
8. **Revelation's use of Isaiah:** How John's Apocalypse draws on Isaiah's portrait of Babylon (compare Isaiah 47–48 with Revelation 17–18).
9. **The Jewish diaspora:** The economics of ancient slave trading and the spread of exiled communities, such as Sepharad in Obadiah 20.
10. **Rethinking messianic prophecy:** How Jewish readers understood prophetic fulfillment, and how New Testament writers use "this was to fulfill…"

### Summary

In this episode Marty and Brent begin the second half of Isaiah, where the message shifts from warning to comfort. Chapters 1–39 told a rebellious people that discipline was coming. Chapters 40–48 speak to exiles who have lived through it and now hear, "Comfort, comfort my people… her sin has been paid for." Using the Jewish idea of *Tikkun Olam*, Marty presents this section as God's plan to repair the world with his servant as a partner, and Isaiah repeatedly names that servant as Jacob/Israel. Along the way God appears as Helper, Advocate, Savior, and Counselor, which Marty connects to the Spirit as *paraklētos* in John's Gospel. The bruised-reed servant of Isaiah 42, whom Matthew saw in Jesus' gentle and restoring ministry, is also a model for how God's people should live and serve today. Chapter 47 brings down proud Babylon for abusing those placed in its hands, and chapter 48 warns Israelites who had grown comfortable in the empire to get out. The main lesson for followers of Jesus is that "Jesus didn't go to the cross so that we don't have to; he went to show us how to." When we stay faithful through hardship, and pay attention to *how* we suffer, others can see what God is like.
