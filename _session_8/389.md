### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** BEMA 389: Psalm 23, A Second Stroll  
**Hosts:** Brent Billings, with guests Elle Grover-Fricks and Josh Bosse  
**Focus:** Psalm 23, with connections to Genesis 1–4, Genesis 31 (Laban and Jacob), Exodus, Numbers 17 (Aaron's staff), and the wider biblical theme of the shepherd

This is the second episode on Psalm 23, after Josh's earlier teaching with Reed and Brent. Brent calls it a "second course" in the feast, in the spirit of the rabbinic idea that every text has "seventy facets." Elle offers her own translation, which aims to bring out the context more than the poetry. The group walks slowly through the psalm, stopping at Hebrew words that change the familiar images. They talk about lacking versus wanting, the "crouching" that echoes Genesis 4, the "calf tracks of justice," the "ravine of the shadow of death" (and a wordplay on *tselem*, "image"), God "standing with" us, a table set facing our oppressors, and goodness and compassion that chase us down. The common thread is that God's remedy for sin, fear, and scarcity is rest, abundance, and closeness to God, not a lonely struggle.

### Key Takeaways

- **Shepherd imagery shaped early Christian identity.** Before the cross became the main Christian symbol, the Good Shepherd carrying a sheep on his shoulders was the main picture of Jesus. It appears in catacombs, on early Christian graves, and in Byzantine art.
- **"I will not lack" is different from "I shall not want."** The psalm promises that we will have enough. It does not condemn desire. Scripture often invites God's people to desire good things and uses the language of desire for God too.
- **"Crouch down" echoes Genesis 4.** The same Hebrew word describes sin crouching at Cain's door. Elle suggests that resting with God is the remedy for the sin that crouches in wait for us.
- **"He returns my soul" uses *shuv*.** Restoration is a journey back home, the same root used for repentance. It is not a snap-of-the-fingers fix.
- **The paths are "calf tracks."** The word means well-worn animal tracks, and it suggests circularity. Life in God's justice is a rhythm that keeps returning: festivals, Sabbath, Jubilee.
- **The dark valley is on the route, not an accident.** The future tense "I will walk" suggests the gorge is part of the path God leads us along toward the green meadows.
- **"Shadow of death" may hide the word "image."** Squeezing *tsalmavet* together suggests *tselem* (image or idol). Many of our fears are imagined threats rather than real ones.
- **God stands with us as family.** The "with" here echoes Laban asking Jacob whether he is "with" him in building a household. It points to belonging, alliance, and God humbling himself (Immanuel).
- **The table is set in front of the enemies.** God does not wait until the threats are gone before welcoming us. Rest and feasting come first, and the threats lose their power.
- **Some of us need to be caught.** This is the only place in the Hebrew Bible where goodness or compassion is the subject of the verb "chase." People who dodge rest need to let God's goodness catch them.

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. Reading Psalm 23 as a "Second Feast"

- The hosts present multiple readings as a normal rabbinic practice, not a contradiction. In Jewish tradition a text has "seventy faces," many valid ways to read it.
- Brent's Thanksgiving analogy: a dinner with a different family may feel unfamiliar, but that doesn't make the meal worse. It would be silly to ask which one is the "real" Thanksgiving.
- Elle's translation is deliberately less polished. She wants to show the concrete, earthy images under the familiar English.

#### 2. "Yahweh Is My Shepherd": An Ancient Christian Symbol

- Early Christians marked graves and catacombs with a young shepherd carrying a sheep on his shoulders.
- Elle notes that the cross became the main emblem later, as theology came to focus more on the crucifixion than on the resurrection and other themes.
- Psalm 23 is one of the main biblical sources for this image of Jesus as shepherd.

#### 3. Lacking vs. Wanting: Rethinking Desire

| Translation | What it implies |
|---|---|
| "I shall not want" | Can suggest that desire itself is bad, or that we need no one and nothing |
| "I will not lack" | I will have enough, because the shepherd provides |

- **Josh's point:** In modern English, "I shall not want" can sound like a life of luxury. "I will not lack" sounds like a life of *enough*.
- **Elle's point:** Some church cultures treat every desire as evidence of a deceitful heart. Scripture has many words for desire, though, and uses them for God (1 Timothy 2:4: God "desires all people to be saved").
- **Individualism vs. community:** In a communal, patronage-based society, refusing to admit any need cuts you off from the community. Saying what you need lets others care for you.
- **Contrast with Stoicism:** The Stoic ideal of needing nothing (Elle's "go live in a box in the street") differs from the biblical pattern of being invited to enjoy good things.
- **Josh's proof text (tongue in cheek):** In Genesis 2, God made trees "pleasing to the eye," so God made desirable things on purpose.
- **Nuance:** Not every desire is good. Abandoning your family because you want to is still wrong. Desire, though, is not evil in itself.

#### 4. "Sprouting Green, Lovely Meadows"

- The Hebrew word for pastures/meadows is related to words for *beauty*, *rest*, and *home or dwelling*.
- Elle thinks the core sense is a place that **lets you breathe**, much as beautiful art pulls you out of chaos and into the present.
- The phrase points back to creation: sprouting vegetation (Genesis 1) and the breath of life (Genesis 2).
- **"Sprouting green"** (Brent's favorite phrase) suggests new spring growth, which is fresh, vibrant, and plentiful. It is not the last hay of the season.

#### 5. "He Makes Me Crouch Down": The Genesis 4 Connection

- The verb (*ravats*) is the same one used of sin "crouching at the door" before Cain (Genesis 4:7).
- The posture is animal-like: lying on the forearms, alert and engaged, but not asleep or curled up. Think of a dog lying on its front paws, still watching.
- **Elle's parallel:**
  - Sin crouches, waiting to overpower us.
  - God invites us to crouch down too, in rest, beside him.
  - Being led by God and resting in his goodness may be how sin is defeated, rather than trying harder on our own.
- **Josh's addition (the "rabbit trail"):** Jewish thought speaks of the *yetzer hara*, the inclination toward evil. It is always present but not irresistible. It feels "natural," the way tiredness makes us snap at someone. Psalm 23 suggests that when God leads, **rest can become as natural as sin**, and repentance becomes a return to safety.

#### 6. "He Guides Me" and "He Returns My Soul"

- **Guide:** Elle says the verb for leading or teaching here is related to a word for a gentle, flowing stream. Good teaching carries people along and shows them what was there all along.
- **Returns (*shuv*):** The verb is not "revive" (from *chayah*, life) but "return," the same root behind repentance.
  - Josh points out that *shuv* is not just turning around. It includes the whole journey back home.
  - Josh, speaking from his own recent brush with death, says that being fully "returned" to life means living without fear hanging over you, not just narrowly escaping death.
  - Brent hears it as restoration as a process. Elle notes that restoration often wrongly brings to mind an instant time-lapse of a wilting flower springing back up.
- **Elle's picture:** When we fight our sin alone, our soul goes out on a failed "hero's journey." God brings it home to the body. Repentance itself is a gift that the Spirit makes possible.

#### 7. "Calf Tracks of Justice"

- Hebrew has many words for road or path (*derekh*, highways, and others). This one (*ma'gal*) means **a worn animal track**, the kind used to lead livestock to pasture. Elle connects it to the word for *calf* (*egel*), the same word as in the golden calf story in Exodus.
- **Brent:** Calf tracks are for *young* animals, so the shepherd is showing them the way to food for the first time.
- **Josh:** Lexicons link the root to *roundness* or a circuit. A track goes out to pasture and comes back home.
  - In Western culture "going in circles" means stagnation. In biblical life, cycles are how life becomes whole: the yearly festivals, Sabbath, letting the land rest, freeing slaves, Jubilee.
  - Obedience becomes comforting: "We go out, the good shepherd feeds us, he brings us back in. That's what justice is like."
- **Justice or righteousness?** The word is *tsedeq*. Elle prefers "justice" partly because Tsedeq was the name of a deity of justice in the surrounding Amorite world. Her real disagreement with Marty is about *mishpat*. Her conclusion: "Righteousness is just justice embodied."

#### 8. "I Will Walk in the Ravine of the Shadow of Death"

- **Future tense:** "I *will* walk," not "though I *may* walk." Hard times are not hypothetical. Everyone will meet them.
- **A possible chiasm:** If the "calf tracks of justice" run *through* this gorge, then the dark valley is part of the route to the green meadows. It is not a wrong turn, and we can prepare for it.
- **Josh's picture:** This could be where a predator once attacked the sheep. That would explain why the shepherd walks right *beside* this sheep, close enough for the rod and staff to comfort it.
- **Valley vs. gorge vs. ravine:**
  - "Valley" sounds sheltered and romantic.
  - "Gorge" or "ravine" sounds confined, hot, rocky, shadowy, and exposed to predators above. Elle and Brent compare it to Hell's Canyon near where they live.
  - Josh suggests "ravine," and Elle adopts it.

#### 9. *Tsalmavet* and *Tselem*: Shadows and Images

- The Hebrew *tsalmavet* is usually read as *tsel* (shadow) + *mavet* (death).
- Ancient scrolls packed letters closely together. Read as one run of letters, the word suggests ***tselem*, "image,"** the same word as in "image of God" (Genesis 1:26–27), and elsewhere used for idols.
- **What this suggests:**
  - Death isn't present; only its *shadow* or *image* is. The threat might be around the corner.
  - Many fears are **imagined** threats: that God is no longer good, or worries about our children.
  - Such fears harm us mostly through the fear itself.
- **Josh:** Sometimes we only become afraid once an image of the danger is "painted" for us. The way we picture human nature ("kill or be killed," "dog eat dog") can produce a fear-based world.
- **Elle:** These images are like illusions, and in light of the resurrection, death itself is an illusion too.

#### 10. "I Will Not Fear Evil": A Proposed Structure

- Elle suggests a pattern in which "I will not lack" pairs with "I will not fear evil." Both describe how I refuse to behave, and many fears are fears of lacking something.
- Likewise, "Yahweh is my shepherd" pairs with "I will walk in the ravine." Both are basic statements about reality.
- "Do not fear" is how angels and appearances of God almost always open a conversation. It has to come first before we can partner with God.
- The ravine verse falls at the **midpoint** of the psalm, and it is the line people remember most.

#### 11. "For You Are Standing With Me"

- The usual Hebrew word for "with" is *im*. Here it is the less common form *immad* (with a *dalet*).
- That form stands out in the Laban and Jacob story (Genesis 31). Laban keeps asking whether Jacob is "with" him, meaning building the household together. In the end Jacob leaves to build his own house.
- **What this suggests:** belonging to God's household, alliance, and team. It also shows God's humility. We might expect "we are with you," but God comes down to be with us (Immanuel).

#### 12. "Your Staff and Your Supporting Branch"

- **Staff (*shevet*):** The same word as *tribe*. A tribal leader carried a staff as a sign of authority. Examples include Moses' staff in Exodus and the twelve tribal staffs, with Aaron's budding for the Levites (Numbers 17).
- **Supporting branch (*mish'enet*):** From the verb "to lean." It means a support or walking stick, something you can lean your weight on.
- Elle pushes back on readings where God's rod is mainly for hitting. Psalm 23 calls these tools *comforting*. (She jokes that the "smacking stick" view makes God sound like Vernon Dursley.)

#### 13. A Table in the Presence of Enemies

- Elle reads it as **"a table set parallel to those who oppress me."**
- **The main point:** God doesn't make us solve every problem first: deal with our sin, settle our doubts, finish a checklist of repentance steps. He invites us to the table *while the enemies are still there*.
- **Josh:** What stands between the sheep and the wolves isn't a wall or bodyguards. It's a table, and the sheep is getting spa treatment. That is radical confidence. Jesus works the same way: put the kingdom first rather than playing "sin whack-a-mole."
- **Brent's picture:** Two armies, after a day of fighting, set up their tables back to back along the front line instead of retreating. Perhaps peace could come over a meal.
- **Elle:** Perhaps the wolves have places set for them too, since Jesus came for our oppressors as well.
- **Reed's teaching:** A banquet is a **"forward operating base of the kingdom."** It is a strategic outpost near enemy territory where the community lays down its weapons, feasts, and recovers. Communion is a foretaste of this coming banquet.

#### 14. Dampened Head and a Satisfied Cup

- **"Dampen"** rather than "anoint" points to luxury and cooling refreshment more than a formal religious rite. Alter uses "moisten."
- **"Abundantly satisfied"** (*revayah*): more than full. Elle hears in the word the sound of a contented sigh. Leaving out the usual "overflowing" image lets the reader be the vessel that is satisfied too.

#### 15. Goodness and Compassion Chasing Us and "Many Long Days"

- **"Chase" (*radaf*)** is a hunting or pursuit word. A footnote Brent reads says this is the **only place in the Hebrew Bible where goodness or compassion is the subject** of this verb.
- **Elle's challenge:** Some people dodge goodness and treat rest as something for other people. The psalm invites them to stop running and let themselves be caught.
- **"For many long days" (Alter's phrasing too):**
  - Elle misses "forever," but she enjoys the legendary feel of a hero finally coming home to stay.
  - Josh: In a culture short on time, "many long days" means unhurried, rich time, the kind of feast where you are so present that time stretches.

### Examples & Applications

1. **Thanksgiving at someone else's house:** Different readings of a text are like different family dinners. They're unfamiliar but no less nourishing, and neither one is the only real dinner.
2. **The communal "curtains" exchange:** In a patronage society, a newcomer who finally admits needing curtains receives more than enough the next day. Admitting need builds community, and self-sufficiency can isolate.
3. **The alert, resting dog:** Lying on its forearms, relaxed but watchful, is the posture of *ravats*. It is ready, not asleep, and it is also how sin crouches.
4. **Recovering from a health crisis:** Josh describes the anxiety that remains after nearly dying. Being fully "returned" to life means being free of that cloud, not just surviving.
5. **The calendar as a calf track:** Weekly Sabbath, yearly festivals, and Jubilee show justice as a repeating path home. A modern parallel is a household or church with steady rhythms of rest, generosity, and celebration.
6. **Hell's Canyon:** A hot, rocky gorge with lookouts for predators above. It is a far more threatening picture than a pastoral valley.
7. **A waiter who keeps filling your glass:** Josh's honeymoon dinner, where his glass was refilled before he noticed, illustrates "my cup is abundantly satisfied."
8. **Bottomless fries:** "Many long days" is like a never-ending meal. It isn't literally infinite, but it's abundant enough that you stop counting.
9. **The forward operating base:** A church potluck, communion, or shared meal works as a strategic outpost of the kingdom, a place of rest in the middle of the struggle.
10. **Generosity that feels like a loss:** Following a prompting to help someone with their mortgage instead of taking a vacation can feel like a letdown at first, yet it becomes part of the beautiful life of goodness pursuing us.
11. **The overworked believer:** For people who believe rest is "for other people," the application is to deliberately let God make them lie down and let goodness catch them.

### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

- **Genesis 4 in depth:** The hosts joke about doing a whole series on it "two words at a time." How does *ravats* relate to *yetzer hara* and the Jewish idea of sin?
- **Early Christian art and symbols:** How and why the Good Shepherd gave way to the cross. Recommended book: *Saving Paradise* by Rita Nakashima Brock and Rebecca Ann Parker.
- ***Tsedeq* vs. *mishpat*:** How the words for justice and righteousness are used across the Hebrew Bible, including the possible link to a Canaanite/Amorite deity named Tsedeq.
- ***Tselem* (image):** Follow the word from Genesis 1 through idolatry passages. How does "image" relate to fear and to being made in God's image?
- **Chiastic structure in the Psalms:** Test Elle's proposed pairings (lack/fear, shepherd/ravine) and decide which line sits at the center.
- ***Shuv* and repentance:** Compare "return" in the Prophets (for example Hosea 14, Jeremiah 3) with Psalm 23:3.
- **The Laban and Jacob narrative (Genesis 29–31):** What does "standing with" someone mean in the context of building a household?
- **Staff and tribe (*shevet*):** Follow the staff as a symbol of authority from Moses to Aaron's budding staff to the Messianic scepter (Genesis 49:10).
- **Banquet theology:** Isaiah 25:6–8, Jesus' meal parables, and the Lord's Supper as a foretaste of the messianic feast. What would Reed's "forward operating base" idea look like in practice?
- **Desire in Scripture:** A word study of Hebrew terms for desire and longing, including those used of God, compared with Stoic ideals of contentment.
- **Josh's other question:** Genesis 14 and the righteousness/justice discussion (Melchizedek, whose name means "king of righteousness").

### Comprehension Questions

1. How does translating Psalm 23:1 as "I will not lack" instead of "I shall not want" change the way we understand desire? What cultural assumptions about desire and self-sufficiency did the hosts question?
2. The verb "crouch down" in Psalm 23:2 is the same word used of sin "crouching at the door" in Genesis 4:7. What does Elle suggest this parallel teaches about how to respond to sin?
3. What does it add to picture the "paths of righteousness" as "calf tracks of justice," worn circular animal tracks? How does Josh connect this circularity to life under Torah?
4. Explain the wordplay between *tsalmavet* ("shadow of death") and *tselem* ("image"). How does this reading speak to fear and anxiety?
5. Why does it matter that the table is set "in the presence of" or "parallel to" our oppressors, rather than after they are gone? How does Reed's idea of a banquet as a "forward operating base of the kingdom" deepen this image?

### Personalized Summary

This second walk through Psalm 23 turns a very familiar passage into something new by staying with its earthy Hebrew images. Elle, Josh, and Brent show that the psalm is about *enough* rather than having no desires. In it, the same posture that sin takes outside Cain's door becomes the posture of rest beside our Shepherd. The paths of justice are well-worn calf tracks that take us out and bring us home. Those tracks run straight through a dark ravine full of shadows and imagined threats, and the Shepherd walks close enough there to lean on. God stands with us as family and sets a feast in full view of our enemies without waiting for us to fix everything first. He sends goodness and compassion to chase us down. The invitation is easy to say and hard to accept: stop fighting alone, lie down when the Shepherd says to, and let yourself be caught by his goodness for many long days.
