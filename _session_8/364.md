### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** BEMA 364: Isaiah — A Father Disciplines His Sons  
**Hosts:** Marty Solomon and Brent Billings  
**Focus:** Isaiah 1–6 (with emphasis on Isaiah 1, 2:1–8, 4, and 6)

After a sabbatical season, Marty and Brent go back to reading the text itself and start a series on the book of Isaiah. They frame Isaiah 1–39 as a distinct section tied to the reigns of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah. They then read the opening six chapters through the image of a frustrated father pleading with stubborn children. Several themes come up along the way. One is a comfortable, prosperous Judah that keeps up its worship while ignoring justice. Another is God's sincere invitation to "reason together." The hosts also discuss the utopian vision of Zion, the rabbinic idea of "backing into the future," the *netzer* (shoot) that grows from a pruned olive stump, and Isaiah's throne-room call, which they treat as a picture of prophetic pathos.

### Key Takeaways

- **Isaiah 1–39 is its own section.** The opening verse lists the kings from Uzziah to Hezekiah, and Hezekiah's reign is where the historical interlude of Isaiah 36–39 sits. So the text itself marks off the first half of the book.
- **God speaks as a grieving father, not a distant judge.** Isaiah 1 opens with "I reared children... but they have rebelled," which presents a relationship that is hurt, not just broken rules.
- **A transactional faith can make God hard to recognize.** In a midrash about a child riding on his father's shoulders, the child asks a stranger where his father is. The point is that when God becomes the supplier of what we want, we stop recognizing Him as Father.
- **Responsibility is shared.** Drawing on Heschel ("few are guilty, but all are responsible"), the hosts note that Isaiah 1 is addressed to the whole nation, not only to corrupt leaders.
- **Worship that ignores justice is rejected.** Judah is keeping the festivals, the sacrifices, and the Sabbaths, yet God says, "I cannot bear your worthless assemblies," because their "hands are full of blood."
- **Critique should lead somewhere.** Isaiah 1:18–20 ("Come now, let us settle the matter") is a sincere invitation to repent and restore. It goes beyond both denial and critique for its own sake.
- **Hope is "Plan A" restored, not a "Plan B."** The vision of the mountain of the Lord in Isaiah 2 imagines God's original purpose renewed, with swords beaten into plowshares.
- **"You have abandoned your people."** Following Robert Alter, Marty suggests that Judah, not God, is the one doing the abandoning in Isaiah 2:6. They have walked away from their calling, their identity, and their story.
- **Backing into the future.** In rabbinic thought, God's people keep their eyes on what God has already done and walk backward into a future only God can see.
- **Pruning produces a remnant.** The *netzer* is the shoot that grows from a pruned olive stump. Judgment is real, but it serves renewal.
- **Isaiah's call shows prophetic pathos.** "Here am I, send me" may come from excitement or from terror. Either way, it is the answer of someone who cannot unsee what he has seen.

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. Authorship and the Structure of Isaiah

- **Competing theories:** Scholars propose anything from a single author, to two, three, or four "voices," to a patchwork of sources assembled by an editor (redactor).
- **Marty's view:** He used to teach three sections and now sees four sections and possibly four voices. He also hears a change in style around Isaiah 12.
- **Broad agreement:** Even conservative scholars generally see at least **two parts**, Isaiah 1–39 and Isaiah 40–66. The historical narrative of chapters 36–39 sits between them.
- **Evidence from the text:** Isaiah 1:1 names the kings Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah. The narrative ends with Hezekiah right before the break at chapter 40, so the first section describes its own time frame.
- **Dating:** A study Bible footnote places Isaiah's ministry from about 740 BC (the year Uzziah died, Isaiah 6:1) into Hezekiah's later years (his reign ended in 686 BC). That would be a career of roughly 55–60 years.

#### 2. God as Father: The Relational Frame

- Isaiah 1:2–3 contrasts animals that know their master ("The ox knows its master, the donkey its owner's manger") with Israel, which "does not know."
- The relationship is complicated. It looks less like a cold legal arrangement and more like a scorned spouse or a frustrated parent.
- God is not an angry deity "playing whack-a-mole" from behind a curtain. He is a parent trying to get His children's attention.
- **The marketplace midrash** (shared from Tommy Brown's *The Ache for Meaning*): A father carries his child through the market on his shoulders and buys everything the child asks for. At the end of the day, the child asks a stranger, "Have you seen my daddy?" The father says, "You foolish child, you are riding on my shoulders." The lesson is that a transactional relationship with God blinds us to who He is.

#### 3. Corporate Responsibility and the Comfortable Nation

- Isaiah 1:4 ("Woe to the sinful nation") addresses the whole people.
- Unlike the northern kingdom, where Heschel saw a gap between corrupt leaders and oppressed people, Judah as a whole is **benefiting** from an economic and commercial system.
- By their own measures, life is going well: good harvests, growing wealth, comfort, and status.
- Isaiah's comparison to **Sodom and Gomorrah** (1:9–10) would have hit hard. It tells them they have lost the plot of the story.
- **Brent's observation:** Isaiah says "we would have become like Sodom," which pulls back slightly, and then immediately addresses "you rulers of Sodom." The nation is at a tipping point, and its leaders have already crossed the line.

#### 4. Worship Without Justice (Isaiah 1:10–17)

- **Brueggemann's prophetic imagination:** The prophet uses provocative language to show that a system people think is secure is actually bankrupt and mortal.
- Judah is not irreligious. They keep the new moons, Sabbaths, festivals, and offerings.
- God's response is sharp: "I have no pleasure... Your incense is detestable... I hate with all my being."
- **The dissonance:** Their worship may even feel sincere to them, but how they live and how they shape their world contradicts it.
- **The remedy is *mishpat* (justice):** "Seek justice, defend the oppressed, take up the cause of the fatherless, plead the case of the widow."
- **A warning about reading in context:** Focusing on historical context can tempt readers to say "those foolish Israelites," which can slide toward antisemitism. Instead, readers should ask how the text speaks to their own comfortable, church-going cultures.

#### 5. "Come Now, Let Us Reason Together" (Isaiah 1:18–20)

- This is a sincere invitation, not sarcasm. "Let us" means God and His people sitting down together.
- "Though your sins are like scarlet, they shall be as white as snow."
- **A contrast with today's polarization:**
  - One side denies that anything is wrong and ignores verses 5–14.
  - The other side lives only in verses 5–14, offering critique without hope.
- Isaiah keeps both. The critique is legitimate, and it is followed by an invitation to *teshuvah* (return or repentance) and to repair what is broken.
- Marty affirms that people's faith journeys and deconstruction are valid. He asks whether there is room within them for repair, beyond judging things as simply right or wrong, in or out.

#### 6. The Mountain of the Lord (Isaiah 2:1–5)

- A short utopian vision placed among chapters of judgment.
- All nations stream to Zion to learn God's ways. "They will beat their swords into plowshares."
- **Key point:** Isaiah does not imagine God giving up on His plan. The hope is renewed faithfulness to **Plan A**. God will deal with the corruption in His own time.

#### 7. "You Have Abandoned Your People" (Isaiah 2:6)

- **Translation issue:** The NIV and NET add "Lord" ("You, Lord, have abandoned your people"), and the NET admits in a footnote that the word was supplied. The ESV reads "you have rejected your people."
- **Robert Alter's reading:** The "you" more likely refers to the nation itself. The people have abandoned their calling and identity, along with the patriarchs and the generations of faith they stand on.
- In BEMA language, they have **stopped trusting the story** and stepped out of the plot God is writing.
- **Geography:** The people look east for superstitions and west to the coastal Philistines for divination. They look in every direction except to God and their own past.

#### 8. Backing Into the Future

- "In the last days" (Isaiah 2:2) can be read more literally as **"in the behind days."**
- **The rabbinic explanation:** Pagan nations try to predict the future through divination. God's people know that only God sees what is coming.
- So the faithful "back into the future." They walk backward with their eyes on what God has already done and glance over their shoulders as they go.
- **The connection:** A few verses later, Judah is condemned for doing the opposite. They copy pagan practices and turn away from their own story. Brent connects this to 2:5, "let us walk in the light of the Lord."

#### 9. Greed Shared by Leaders and People (Isaiah 3)

- In brief: a people consumed by greed who gladly follow corrupt leaders, because the system those leaders run benefits them.

#### 10. The *Netzer*: Branch, Shoot, and Remnant (Isaiah 4)

- **Netzer** means a shoot or branch. *Nazareth* likely comes from the same root ("shoot town").
- **Olive tree pruning:** Every few centuries (Marty was taught every 300–400 years), the trunk is cut flat just below where the branches start. A new shoot, the *netzer*, grows from the stump.
- Without pruning, the tree grows wild and becomes useless. With pruning, it can produce for centuries.
- **Application:** Exile and discipline are God's pruning. What looks dead is being preserved.
- The "filth" and "bloodstains" of Isaiah 4:4 are concrete. According to Alter, the term for bloodguilt points to murder, meaning real harm to real people.
- **On Christology:** Christians rightly connect the branch with Jesus, but Marty suggests Isaiah's primary concern was the **remnant** of his own people.
- The cloud by day and fire by night (4:5) echo the Exodus wilderness.

#### 11. Isaiah's Throne-Room Call (Isaiah 6)

- Set "in the year that King Uzziah died." The seraphim cry "Holy, holy, holy," the doorposts shake, and smoke fills the temple.
- **Isaiah's response:** "Woe to me! I am ruined... a man of unclean lips." This is an understandable sense of his own frailty.
- **Atonement:** A seraph touches his lips with a live coal from the altar. "Your guilt is taken away."
- **"Here am I, send me"** (an echo of the Hebrew *hineni*). Two readings:
  - **Brent:** He has just been restored and is eager to go.
  - **Marty:** He may be terrified but responds anyway, which illustrates **Heschel's pathos**, an overwhelming encounter that leaves him no real alternative.
- **A hard commission:** "Make the heart of this people calloused." The Septuagint renders it as "this people's heart *has become* calloused," which recalls the question of who hardened Pharaoh's heart.
- **"For how long, Lord?"** Brent hears an echo of Abraham bargaining in Genesis 18, except Isaiah never actually negotiates.
- **The answer:** Until the cities are ruined and the land is forsaken. Even the remaining tenth will be laid waste. Yet "the holy seed will be the stump in the land," which brings back the pruning image.

### Examples & Applications

- **The Frodo parallel:** At the Council of Elrond in Tolkien's *The Fellowship of the Ring*, Frodo agrees to carry the Ring to Mordor because no one else can. Like Isaiah's "send me," it is a reluctant, costly yes to a burden he cannot put down.
- **Modern comfortable Christianity:** A culture can attend church, sing worship songs, and produce Christian media while benefiting from economic systems that harm the vulnerable. Isaiah 1 asks whether that worship has become noise to God.
- **Social media critique culture:** Accounts and creators who only point out what is wrong live in Isaiah 1:5–14. Isaiah models moving on to 1:18–20, toward repair and hope.
- **Family life:** Any parent who has asked a child "Why are you acting like this?" knows the tone of Isaiah 1. Discipline comes from love and frustration, not detached anger.
- **Gardening and orchards:** Hard pruning of fruit trees, roses, or grapevines looks destructive but keeps the plant healthy. It is a useful picture of seasons of loss that lead to new growth.
- **Backing into the future in practice:** In uncertain times, the hosts suggest focusing on God's past faithfulness instead of anxiously trying to predict what comes next.

### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

1. **Authorship of Isaiah:** Compare the two-, three-, and four-voice theories. What stylistic, historical, and theological evidence supports each one?
2. **Archaeology of the kings of Judah:** What evidence outside the Bible exists for Uzziah, Ahaz, and Hezekiah (for example, Hezekiah's tunnel, the Siloam inscription, and royal seal impressions)?
3. **Heschel's *The Prophets*:** Study his ideas of divine pathos and "few are guilty, but all are responsible."
4. **Brueggemann's *The Prophetic Imagination*:** How does prophetic language "criticize and energize" a culture?
5. **Prophetic critiques of worship:** Compare Isaiah 1:10–17 with Amos 5:21–24, Micah 6:6–8, Hosea 6:6, and Jeremiah 7.
6. **The *netzer* and Nazareth:** Trace the branch and shoot imagery through Isaiah 11:1, Jeremiah 23:5, Zechariah 3:8 and 6:12, Matthew 2:23, and Romans 11 (the olive tree).
7. **Hardening of hearts:** Compare Isaiah 6:9–10 with the Pharaoh narratives and with how the New Testament quotes the passage (Matthew 13, John 12, Acts 28).
8. **The "behind days":** Explore the Hebrew *acharit hayamim* and how Jewish thought understands time, memory, and the future.
9. **Translation choices:** Compare how several translations handle Isaiah 2:6 and what interpretive assumptions lie behind them.
10. **Call narratives:** Compare Isaiah 6 with the calls of Moses (Exodus 3), Jeremiah (Jeremiah 1), and Ezekiel (Ezekiel 1–3).

### Comprehension Questions

1. How does Isaiah 1:1 support dividing the book into Isaiah 1–39 and Isaiah 40–66, and which king connects the opening verse to the historical section of chapters 36–39?
2. What is the main lesson of the midrash about the child riding on his father's shoulders, and how does it connect to Isaiah 1:2–3?
3. Why does God reject Judah's sacrifices, festivals, and prayers in Isaiah 1:10–15, and what does He ask of them instead in verses 16–17?
4. How does Robert Alter's reading of "you have abandoned your people" (Isaiah 2:6) differ from translations that add "Lord," and how does it connect to the rabbinic idea of "backing into the future"?
5. Explain the olive tree pruning image behind the *netzer* in Isaiah 4 and the stump in Isaiah 6:13. What does it suggest about the purpose of God's judgment?

### Personal Summary

This episode starts the Isaiah series with a father's grief instead of a judge's verdict. Isaiah's audience was comfortable, prosperous, and religious. They kept the festivals and offered the sacrifices while their hands were "full of blood" from injustice. That makes these chapters uncomfortable to read in any culture where faith and comfort have become intertwined. What stands out most is that Isaiah does not stop at critique. Alongside the sharp indictment, God says, "Come now, let us reason together," and holds out a vision of swords turned into plowshares. That vision is the original plan renewed, not abandoned.

The ideas of "backing into the future" and the *netzer* rising from a pruned stump both point to trust. God's people are meant to stay anchored in what He has already done and to believe that even severe pruning serves new life. Isaiah's "Here am I, send me," whether spoken in eagerness or in fear, models faithfulness that responds to God's presence even when the road ahead looks like ruin. The challenge to take away is to hear Isaiah 1 as addressed to us too, to take the call to justice seriously, and to stay in the story God is still telling.
