### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** BEMA 390: Psalms Conclusion  
**Hosts:** Brent Billings, with Reed Dent and Elle Grover-Fricks (Marty Solomon absent)  
**Focus:** Wrap-up of the Psalms series. It looks back at Psalms 4, 8, 13, 19, 23, 32, 51, 91, 100, 104, 118, 137 and 139, and at what Hebrew poetry is.

This episode closes the ten-episode Psalms series. Reed Dent leads it, and he draws out two main points about the Psalms. First, they free us from "decorum," so we can bring our whole selves to God honestly. Second, that expression is never just for its own sake, because every psalm is aimed at God. Elle Grover-Fricks explains the three main features of Hebrew poetry and why they help us pray honestly without a fixed formula. Reed then reads from three modern poets who write in a psalm-like way: Christian Wiman, Kaveh Akbar and Franz Wright. The hosts share the music and poems that work this way for them. The episode ends with practical encouragement for praying, writing and living with the Psalms. Before all that, the hosts check in on a running joke from the series: did the podcast ever actually explain "life psalms"?

### Key Takeaways

- **The Psalms give us permission to drop the act.** Their guiding rule is roughly "speak what we feel, not what we ought to say." This works in both directions: lament and anger, and also joy and praise.
- **You can fake feelings in either direction.** Gushing, sugary praise can be fake. So can dramatic, edgy complaining. The Psalms ask for sincerity, not provocation.
- **Different people need different psalms.** Some people need Psalm 13 ("How long, O Lord?") to admit they doubt. Others need Psalm 100 ("Shout for joy to the Lord") to let themselves celebrate.
- **Honest prayer is an act of trust.** Holding back joy or pain often comes from wanting to control how we are seen. Bringing everything to God says that God is big enough to handle it.
- **Hebrew poetry has three main features:** (1) structures shared with other ancient Near Eastern poetry, such as parallelism and chiasm, (2) brevity, and (3) special vocabulary.
- **Expression is not the goal in itself.** God is at the center of every psalm. The psalmists celebrate God, wrestle with God, or move toward and away from God.
- **The Psalms are a two-way conversation.** Whoever prays them may be answered, and that answer may comfort or confront.
- **Reorientation matters.** Repeating a complaint with no turn toward truth can strengthen the very story that is hurting us. Many psalms "preach to themselves" by coming back to who God is.
- **Practical paths forward:** let the Psalms be your prayer life for a season, write your own psalms, put psalms up in your home, and let psalms become markers of key moments in your life.

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. Done with Decorum: Honest Expression Before God

Reed's first main point is that the Psalms free us from being overly polite, deferential or self-conscious with God.

- **Sincerity is the goal.** The aim is not to be edgy or dramatic. It is to speak truthfully about what is going on inside us.
- **Discomfort goes both ways:**
  - *Uncomfortable with negative feelings:* People raised to think doubt or anger is off-limits may need Psalm 13, the hard line in Psalm 139 ("I hate them with complete hatred"), or the shocking last verse of Psalm 137.
  - *Uncomfortable with positive feelings:* Some people, including Reed and possibly some "deconstructors," find uninhibited joy and praise harder. Psalm 100 can stretch them.
- **Reed's own example:** He doesn't dance at wedding receptions because unfiltered joy feels exposing to him.

#### 2. Fear, Control and Trust

Elle explains why we hold back.

- Both performing and staying silent come from the same place: **a fear of being seen, or a wish to control how we are seen.**
- There may also be a **fear of losing control of ourselves**. We worry about becoming a "cringy happy-clappy Christian," or about getting lost in pain we "pull out from under the bed."
- Bringing all of this **to God**, rather than just venting alone, is a **statement of trust**. It says God can hear it and also work through it with us.
- **God already knowing doesn't make speaking pointless.** Elle compares it to marriage. A spouse may read your body language, but the relationship is still stronger when you say plainly, "This is how I'm doing, and here's why."

#### 3. What Makes Hebrew Poetry "Poetry"

Elle goes back to the series' opening question, "What is poetry?", and gives the academic answer:

1. **Near Eastern structures.** Chiasms, parallelism and recurring motifs are common, though they also show up in some prose. (She jokes that not every chapter of the Hebrew Bible has a chiasm, much to BEMA listeners' disappointment.)
2. **Brevity.** Grammatical "filler" like articles, prefixes and connecting words is dropped, leaving only the core words.
   - *Example:* Reed's third-grade classmate wrote: "Brian Coble is so noble. He is small, but not like a ball." In Hebrew style it might shrink to something like "Small, not ball."
   - Elle compares it to French art songs, which are lean, as opposed to the denser German Romantic style.
3. **Special vocabulary.** Some words show up almost only in poetic books such as Psalms and Job. This marks genre, not date.

**Why it matters for prayer:**

- The Psalms don't follow a strict prayer formula. Elle mentions being taught an ordered pattern of thanksgiving, adoration, confession and so on. The Psalms aren't bound by anything like that.
- Overly formal, hedged prayer ("if it be within your sovereign will...") can be as performative as anything else.
- Special vocabulary gives us words for deep feeling that everyday speech doesn't, such as "my soul yearns." We rarely let ourselves say things like that, yet they can be true.
- **Orientation, disorientation and new orientation** (Walter Brueggemann's framework, used throughout the series) are helpful categories. But real psalms rarely "play nicely by the formula." Nobody prays, "Lord, here is my prayer of orientation."

#### 4. Expression Is Not for Its Own Sake: God at the Center

Reed's second main point is that the Psalms always tie experience back to God.

- **The challenge for post-Enlightenment readers** may be less about the emotions and more about **the "God-ness"** of the Psalms. They insist on connecting everything to God.
- **Examples of how the Psalms turn experience toward God:**
  - Not "nature is cool," but "He wraps himself in light as with a garment; he stretches out the heavens like a tent" (Psalm 104).
  - Not "I am sad," but "How long, O Lord? Will you forget me forever?" (Psalm 13).
  - Not abstract thinking about human nature, but "When I consider your heavens, the work of your fingers... what is mankind that you are mindful of them?" (Psalm 8).
  - "This is the day the Lord has made" (Psalm 118), whether the day is good or bad.
- **Written in first person, spoken to "you":** The Psalms are full of "I" and "my" and speak to God directly. Praying them as written "forces us to the table" with God, whether to talk, catch up or argue.

#### 5. Reorientation and "Preaching to Yourself"

Elle explains why expression alone can backfire.

- **Brain science:** Every time we retell a story, we strengthen it in memory. "Just getting it out of my system" can actually lock in a harmful story.
- **Psalm 4 as a model:** The psalmist complains for several verses ("How long shall my honor be turned to shame?"). Then he turns: "In peace I will lie down and sleep, for you alone, O Lord, make me dwell in safety."
- **Elle's teenage journals:** She wrote pages of angsty prayers and came away more anxious. Eventually she realized she never got around to reminding herself what was true about God.
- **A practice she suggests:** Write the psalm on one half of the page and your own response on the other. The psalm's structure usually carries you to reorientation, which "resettles your soul back into its refuge."

#### 6. The Psalms Are a Dialogue

- Prayer is both speaking and listening. The Psalms invite God to answer.
- That answer is rarely "a voice from the sky." It may come "a little sideways" or through the text itself.
- Listening only becomes a habit if you deliberately make room for it.

#### 7. Modern Poets Writing in a Psalm-Like Way

Reed shares three contemporary poets who still write "psalmically":

| Poet | Work | Excerpt / Theme |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Christian Wiman** (Yale; a Texas Baptist who became an atheist and later returned to faith) | *My Bright Abyss* (a memoir written after his cancer diagnosis) | "My God, my bright abyss / into which all my longing will not go / once more I come to the edge of all I know / and believing nothing believe in this:" The colon leaves it unfinished, and the memoir works as his answer. |
| **Kaveh Akbar** (born in Iran, raised Muslim) | "Despite My Best Efforts, Even My Prayers Have Turned into Threats" from *Pilgrim Bell* | "...I believe my courage will expand like a sponge cowboy in water." It mixes bravado and sheepishness before God. |
| **Franz Wright** | "The Only Animal" from *Walking to Martha's Vineyard* (Pulitzer Prize) | The poem ends with God answering: "...though your own heart condemn you / I do not condemn you." |

- **Imagining God's reply** is a creative practice anyone can use. Reed's ministry ends each semester by asking, "What did God see?"

### Examples & Applications

- **Music that works like a psalm**
  - *Elle* names worship music "that doesn't suck": Kings Kaleidoscope, Citizens, United Pursuit and Will Reagan. She also values older 90s worship songs that bring back memories of times she felt God's faithfulness, even if the lyrics don't hold up.
  - *Community choir:* Singing choral pieces, which are poems set to music, again and again lets her live inside poetry and connect with things she didn't expect to.
  - *Brent* says the sound often moves him more than the words. A particular church band's version of "Revelation Song" twenty years ago carried him somewhere no recording has matched. For his funeral he wants David Crowder's "I Saw the Light," with a full hoedown.
  - *Context changes everything:* Brent wept, and later felt angry, singing a song in Oklahoma that seemed to clash with a recent tragedy. Elle mentions Maverick City Music leading hundreds of prisoners in "Bless Me" at a Miami-Dade correctional facility.
- **Poetry:** Elle copies out Tennyson's "Ulysses" every birthday ("How dull it is to pause, to make an end, / To rust unburnished, not to shine in use!"). She reads it as a poem about the spiritual life, and says it speaks to her Enneagram 8 personality.
- **Psalms as markers in time:** If you connect a psalm to a season of life, your Bible becomes a "road map" over the years of where God showed up.
- **Psalms in the home:** The Dent household hangs prayers from *Every Moment Holy* on its walls. Elle connects this to ancient amulets inscribed with Psalm 91, which Robert Alter calls an "amulet psalm." At their best these were physical reminders of God's protection, not superstitious charms.
- **Writing your own psalm:** Use Psalm 8 or Psalm 51 as a template. Where David confesses, write your own confession. Where he turns, write your own turn. Making something helps you remember what you learned.
- **When prayer feels impossible:** Reed suggests letting the Psalms be your prayer life for a season. Read and pray one every day for a month and see what happens.
- **The "life psalms" mystery:** Brent searched the transcripts and could only find a passing mention in episode 129, plus a possible link to Jesus reciting Psalm 22 on the cross. Elle first learned the practice in Marty's original classes at WSU in 2013. It remains unresolved.

### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

1. **Hebrew poetic features in practice:** Study parallelism, chiasm and poetic vocabulary using a concordance. Try the hosts' question: is Genesis 1 poetry?
2. **Brueggemann's orientation, disorientation and new orientation:** Explore where the framework helps and where real psalms resist it.
3. **Psalm 100 and other psalms of joyful praise:** Reed mentioned a possible future episode. Ask why exuberant praise is hard for some readers.
4. **Psalm 4 and Psalm 104:** Neither got its own episode, but both are good examples of reorientation and creation praise.
5. **"Life psalms" and Jewish practices of praying the Psalms:** Look at whether Jesus' cry from Psalm 22 reflects a broader practice of recitation.
6. **Ancient amulets and inscribed Scripture:** Look into archaeological finds of protective texts and what they say about popular faith.
7. **Contemporary psalm-like poetry:** Read Christian Wiman's *My Bright Abyss*, Kaveh Akbar's *Pilgrim Bell*, and Franz Wright's *Walking to Martha's Vineyard*.
8. **The psychology of lament:** How does repeating a story affect memory and emotional health, and how does the structure of lament guard against spiraling?

### Personalized Summary

This last Psalms episode gives you two things to carry with you. First, you don't have to perform for God. Whether you are stuck in anger and doubt or can't let yourself feel joy, there is a psalm that can draw out what you have been holding back, and offering it to God is an act of trust. Second, the Psalms never treat feelings as the final word. They keep pointing you back to God, invite God to answer, and often carry you from complaint to a renewed sense of who God is. Hebrew poetry's brevity and lack of a fixed formula free you to pray honestly, and modern poets like Wiman, Akbar and Wright show the practice is still alive. Going forward, you could read and pray a psalm every day for a month, write one of your own, put one up on your wall, and notice which psalms mark the seasons of your life. As Elle put it, you're never just feeling down alone in your room. You're feeling down "with a great cloud of witnesses."
