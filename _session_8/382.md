### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** BEMA 382: Psalm 8  
**Hosts:** Brent Billings, with guests Reed Dent and Elle Grover-Fricks  
**Focus:** Psalm 8; also Genesis 1, Genesis 3:16, 1 Samuel 17 (David and Goliath), Mark 10:42–45, and the Septuagint's reading of Psalm 8

This episode continues the Psalms series and is Elle Grover-Fricks' first time on it. Reed, Brent, and Elle read Psalm 8 slowly: it is a psalm about wonder, about how small humans are, and about the dignity and responsibility God gives them anyway. They start by looking at Walter Brueggemann's orientation / disorientation / new orientation framework. Then they work through Elle's own translation of the psalm, which raises questions about the superscription ("upon the gittith"), the puzzling verse 2, the word usually rendered "angels," and the Hebrew word for "rule." The conversation keeps returning to one tension. People are dust, and yet the universe was made for them. They are not essential to the cosmos, and yet they are crowned with glory and honor. The hosts argue that this tension is what forms humble, attentive, servant-hearted leadership over creation.

### Key Takeaways

- Psalm 8 is usually called a **psalm of orientation**: the world is ordered and humanity is in its place before God. Elle points out that the line "what is man that you remember him?" reads like a moment of sudden **reorientation**, a revelation under the night sky.
- Brueggemann's categories are useful tools, not labels built into the text. Brent calls them "an academic vibe check."
- The superscription *al ha-gittit* is uncertain. It may point to a **winepress** (harvest celebration) or to **Gath**, Goliath's hometown. The second option would place the psalm after David's victory.
- **Verse 2** ("from the mouths of babes…") seems to interrupt the flow from verse 1 to verse 3. Elle suggests David may be describing himself as the "infant" through whom God laid a foundation of strength against Goliath.
- Many translations take the word **"praise"** in verse 2 and **"angels"** in verse 5 from the **Septuagint**, not the Hebrew. The Hebrew of verse 5 reads ***Elohim***: God, or divine beings.
- Being small is not the same as being worthless. Humans are **not essential to the universe, and yet** God is mindful of them and crowns them with glory and honor.
- The verb for "rule" in verse 6, ***mashal***, is not the same as the "tread down" verb ***radah*** in Genesis 1:26. Elle reads Psalm 8's ruling as gathering creation into God's mission, not dominating it.
- Ruling well means **seeing before acting**. The psalm makes the reader look at each group of creatures in turn, the way God "comes down" to see humanity before acting.
- Some of what the psalm offers can't be reproduced on a podcast or in a classroom. **Read it outside, under the stars.**

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. Brueggemann's Threefold Framework for the Psalms

Walter Brueggemann groups the psalms by the stance the psalmist takes toward the world:

1. **Orientation:** Life feels ordered and right, and God is reliably in charge.
2. **Disorientation:** The world has been knocked off its axis. These psalms often carry despair, confusion, or anger.
3. **New orientation:** The psalmist has been in "the pit" and has been rescued, often unexpectedly, and now praises God with new depth.

Reed puts Psalm 8 in the orientation category. Elle pushes back gently: human experience rarely fits neatly into one bucket. Psalm 8 reads like orderly doctrine on the surface, but its central question carries the shock of a fresh revelation. All three hosts agree the categories are made-up conventions, useful only insofar as they help.

#### 2. A Contemplative Psalm

Reed notes that many psalms are first-person reports of distress or deliverance. Psalm 8 steps back and reflects on the **nature, purpose, and destiny of humanity**. That makes it unusual, and it suits a reflective, contemplative personality. Reed jokes that it is a psalm an Enneagram Five could "get down with."

#### 3. The Superscription: "Upon the Gittith"

- Translations often render the heading as "For the director of music, according to *gittith*." Robert Alter wryly notes that this is "another musical instrument that has eluded persuasive identification."
- Elle's translation reads: **"To the glittering one upon the winepress."**
  - *Menatseach* ("director") carries the idea of glittering or preeminence.
  - *Gat* means "winepress." On that reading, the psalm may have been sung at a harvest festival, with the community out under the stars treading the first fruits.
- Another option is that *gitti* means "Gittite," someone from **Gath**, Goliath's city. Read this way, the psalm could be "over the Gittite," written in the afterglow of David's victory in 1 Samuel 17. (For more on David and Goliath, see **BEMA Episode 39**.)

#### 4. The Puzzle of Verse 2

Brent first reads verse 1 and then verses 3–4, skipping verse 2. The psalm flows without it, which raises the question of what verse 2 is doing there.

**Comparing translations:**

| Source | Rendering of verse 2 |
|---|---|
| NIV | "Through the praise of children and infants you have established a stronghold against your enemies…" |
| Robert Alter | "From the mouths of babes and sucklings you founded strength on account of your foes…" |
| BibleProject | "From the mouths of infants and nursing babies you have established a stronghold because of your adversaries…" |
| Elle Grover-Fricks | "From the mouth of babies and nursing infants, you have laid a foundation of fierce strength, that those who bind and restrict, the vengeful, and those who blow and puff with hate will be still." |

**Translation notes:**

- **"Praise"** comes from the **Septuagint** (the Greek Old Testament), not the Hebrew, which reads "strength." Elle criticizes translations that lean on the Greek or paraphrase the KJV instead of working from the Hebrew.
- The verb means to lay down, set up, or found something. Reading it as a military "stronghold" is, in Elle's view, a stretch. Reed suggests the battle imagery might fit if the Goliath reading is right.
- **"Those who bind and restrict":** Elle hears an echo of the word behind *Mitzrayim* (Egypt, the "narrow place"), which recalls Israel's slavery under empire.
- **"Blow and puff with hate":** This draws on the breath imagery under the Hebrew word. It also hints at how empty Goliath's taunting was, as he shouted while Israel's sacrifices went on.

**Elle's proposal:** The "infant" in verse 2 may be **young David**, the smallest person on a battlefield of older, bigger men. Through David's faithful willingness, God laid the foundation of the kingdom that Samuel had foretold. On that reading, verse 3 follows naturally. After the victory, David looks up at the stars and is humbled. As Elle puts it, he isn't strutting around like he "won the Super Bowl." He is asking, *who even am I?*

Reed adds a more general reading that also works: God brings strength out of weakness, much as in 1 Corinthians 1, where God's foolishness is wiser than human wisdom.

#### 5. Smallness Before the Stars

- **Elle:** Praise is not God acting like "a creepy narcissist." People find their deepest **meaning and delight** when they remember how big God is and how small they are, the way they feel standing in front of a huge mountain.
- **Reed:** Stargazing brings up a sense of **longing**. It may be a longing for "out there," or, in C.S. Lewis's phrase, for "the thing behind the thing": the God who made it all. It also brings wonder and stillness.
- **Abraham Joshua Heschel (*The Sabbath*):** "He who wants to enter the holiness of the day must learn to understand that the world has already been created and will survive without the help of man."
- Reed's point: being **non-essential** does not mean being insignificant. Psalm 8 says *you are not essential, and yet here you are, with a purpose.*

#### 6. The Cinematic "Pan Down"

Elle describes the psalm as one long camera shot. It starts in the heavens (moon and stars), pans down to humanity, and then moves to everything under human feet. That raises questions. Is humanity part of the "rolling, blazing stars," set apart from them, or made of the same stuff? She also mentions a neuroscience finding: when you zoom out in your imagination from your room to your street, city, region, and beyond, your **perception of time slows**. In a sense, reading this psalm changes how you experience space and time.

#### 7. "A Little Lower Than the Angels"? Elohim and Septuagint Influence

- The Hebrew of verse 5 says humanity was made "a little lower than ***Elohim***." That word can mean God or supernatural beings, but it is not the usual word for angels.
- The rendering **"angels"** comes from the **Septuagint**. Hebrews 2:7 later follows that Greek wording. Elle suggests translators choose "angels" when they are uneasy about comparing humans too closely to God.
- **"Glory and honor"** are words Scripture usually uses for God himself. Humanity is "crowned" with divine attributes, which fits the idea of bearing God's image.
- Reed notes a parallel. Verse 3 calls the heavens "the work of your fingers," and verse 6 says humans rule over "the works of your hands." Humanity is given authority over the same divine handiwork that made them feel so small.

#### 8. Holding Both Truths: Greatness and Littleness

- Reed cites a reflection (attributed in the episode to Lewis) on "the greatness and littleness, the humble origins and the amazing destiny of humanity." If you are proud, you are less than you think. If you think little of yourself, you are more than you think.
- **The rabbinic "two pockets" teaching** (traditionally attributed to the Hasidic Rabbi Simcha Bunim of Peshischa): Carry a note in each pocket. One says, *"I am but dust and ashes"* (compare Genesis 3:19; 18:27). The other says, *"For my sake the world was created"* (Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5). You have to hold both at once.
- Holding this tension produces both **healthy self-worth** and **healthy humility**, which is the right stance for carrying out the work of image-bearing.

#### 9. Mashal vs. Radah: What Kind of Ruling?

- **Genesis 1:26** uses ***radah*** ("rule over the fish of the sea"), which carries the image of treading down.
- **Psalm 8:6** uses ***mashal***. Elle notes that its first prominent use is in **Genesis 3:16**, where the man will "rule over" the woman. She suggests the sense there is **assimilation**: drawing the woman's will and desires into the man's household and culture.
- In Psalm 8, Elle reads *mashal* more positively. Creation is **brought into humanity's mission**, which is to bless all nations and to love God and neighbor. It is "harnessed under our yoke" for God's purposes, not crushed underfoot.
- Ruling over creation does not justify oppressing other people, who are also "crowned with honor and adornment."

#### 10. Ruling by Seeing

- The psalmist could have said "all living things" and stopped. Instead, like Genesis 1, the psalm **lists each group**: flocks, gentle herds, the "quiet ones of the field" (animals with no voice), birds that "dance circles in the heavens," and whatever crosses the paths of the seas.
- This makes the reader *see* each creature before deciding how to rule it, just as God "comes down" to see humanity (compare Genesis 11:5; 18:21; Exodus 3:7–8).
- **Jesus' model (Mark 10:42–45):** "The Gentiles lord it over them… not so with you. Whoever wants to be great among you must be your servant." Psalm 8's language of "all things under his feet" is applied to Christ in 1 Corinthians 15:27 and Ephesians 1:22. People who follow Christ rule the way he does: with **humility, wisdom, responsibility, and service**.

### Examples & Applications

- **Parenting as *mashal*:** Elle notes that shouting orders from above ("Stop wrestling in the living room!") rarely works. Instead she gets down, holds her three-year-old's hands, looks him in the eye, and asks what is going on. Only after that connection can she do the "assimilation": *"In our house, we use our hands to have fun and to serve others."*
- **Naming individuals:** Reed sees that "ruling over my kids" feels very different when he names each one: Briggs, Jack, and Graham. Naming draws his attention to each person and shapes how he leads. The same principle applies to employees, congregations, students, and land.
- **Scripture under the sky:** Reed's small group took about 20 young people to a dark field lit by tea lights. For 45 minutes they did nothing but sit under the stars while Reed read Psalm 8 every so often, with no commentary. (A stray cat that wandered among them was, according to Elle, "a manifestation of the Holy Spirit.")
- **Stargazing as a discipline:** Elle's childhood pastor, an amateur astronomer, had the youth group lie in a field and think about how God made eyes that can focus on a hand in front of the face and also on stars light-years away. Brent takes late-night walks in Moscow, Idaho, to see Orion. City dwellers are encouraged to get out where the sky is dark.
- **Zoom-out meditation:** Imagine yourself in your room, then your street, town, region, planet, and cosmos, then return to the psalm. It is a practical way to feel how small you are and to slow down.
- **The two-pockets practice:** Literally carry two notes, "dust and ashes" and "for my sake the world was created," as a daily reminder to hold humility and dignity together.
- **Creation care:** Ruling the earth well rules out "pillaging" it. Before deciding how to use land, animals, or resources, *look at them first* and see them as God's handiwork.
- **Daily Psalms and Proverbs:** Elle has read through the Psalms continually for about 20 years and reads a proverb a day, which she says "will eviscerate you right through the middle."

### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

1. **Midrash Tehillim on Psalm 8** (available on Sefaria): rabbinic stories in which angels question God about creating such a flawed humanity, laid out to mirror the psalm's structure.
2. **The Septuagint's influence on English Bibles**, especially "praise" in Psalm 8:2 (which Jesus quotes in Matthew 21:16) and "angels" in 8:5 (quoted in Hebrews 2:6–8).
3. **Psalm 8 in the New Testament:** how Hebrews 2, 1 Corinthians 15:27, and Ephesians 1:22 read the psalm in light of Christ.
4. **The Hebrew vocabulary of dominion:** *radah* (Gen. 1:26–28), *kavash* ("subdue"), and *mashal* (Gen. 3:16; Ps. 8:6), and what each implies for human vocation.
5. **The *gittith* superscription:** comparing Psalms 8, 81, and 84, which share it, for harvest or Gath connections.
6. **David and Goliath (1 Samuel 17)** as a possible setting for the psalm (see BEMA Episode 39).
7. **Heschel's *The Sabbath*** and the theme of human non-essentiality as a gift.
8. **Brueggemann's *The Message of the Psalms*** and the strengths and limits of genre categories.
9. **Neuroscience of awe:** research on how awe and "zooming out" change our sense of time and self.
10. **Artistic responses:** Switchfoot's "Stars" and other music inspired by Psalm 8.

### Personalized Summary

Psalm 8 asks you to step outside, look up, and feel how small you are. Under that vast sky, set in place by God's fingers, you are not essential, because the world was finished before you arrived and will go on without you. And yet God is mindful of you, visits you, and crowns you with the glory and honor that belong to God. The episode's main point is that both truths are needed together. Carry "dust and ashes" in one pocket and "the world was created for my sake" in the other. That balance is what makes you fit to rule, and ruling in Psalm 8 is not pillaging or lording it over others. It looks more like a parent kneeling to look a child in the eye, or like Jesus washing feet: seeing each creature and each person, naming them, and drawing them gently into God's mission of blessing. Elle's pastor was right that this may be the one psalm you most need to memorize. Read it this week under a dark sky and let it reorient you.
