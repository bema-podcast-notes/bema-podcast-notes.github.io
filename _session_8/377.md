### BEMA Episode 377 Study Notes: The Fruit of Faithfulness

#### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** 377 — The Fruit of Faithfulness  
**Hosts:** Marty Solomon and Brent Billings  
**Focus:** Isaiah 56–66 (Third Isaiah), plus a review of the whole book of Isaiah

This episode ends the BEMA series on Isaiah with the book's closing chapters. These are prophetic visions of a "new world of hope" for a people coming out of exile. Marty and Brent show that this world is not really new. It renews what God wanted from Eden onward: justice, righteousness, a welcome for outsiders, and shalom. Isaiah 56–66 is not all comfort, though. It also contains warnings about idolatry, empty religious ritual, and the failures of Israel's own leaders. The hosts follow how hope changes what God's people talk about, want, and do. Their main text is Isaiah 58's picture of "true fasting," and they move on to the vision of a new heavens and a new earth. Brent suggests a chiastic (mirror-image) structure for these chapters, and Marty closes with a revised review of all of Isaiah built around the picture of a father disciplining his son.

#### Key Takeaways

- **The "new world of hope" is a renewed world, not a new one.** It brings back God's original intention from the garden and from Israel's calling to live at the crossroads of the nations.
- **Outsiders are included.** Isaiah 56 welcomes eunuchs and foreigners who hold fast to God's covenant, even though Deuteronomy 23 excluded certain people from the assembly.
- **God's people are often their own worst enemy.** The obstacle to hope is Israel's own "watchmen" (prophets, priests, and leaders), who are blind, idle, and self-serving. Pagan nations are not the main problem.
- **God is willing to forgive and heal.** "I have seen their ways, but I will heal them" (Isaiah 57:18). The invitation to return is always open.
- **True fasting is justice.** Isaiah 58 defines acceptable worship as freeing the oppressed, feeding the hungry, sheltering the poor wanderer, and clothing the naked. Religious routine alone is not enough.
- **Sabbath is about joining God's delight, not self-indulgence.** The Sabbath section at the end of Isaiah 58 warns against making the holy day all about "doing as you please."
- **Sin separates us from what God is doing in the world.** Read in context, Isaiah 59:1–2 is an immediate, relational statement: God's arm is "not too short to save" right now. It is not mainly a statement about humanity's fallen nature.
- **Guilt is not the same as shame.** Isaiah 64 admits real guilt ("all our righteous acts are like filthy rags") but refuses to make sin the people's identity: "You, Lord, are our Father; we are the clay."
- **Isaiah 61 was true for Isaiah and "even more true" for Jesus.** Jesus fulfills the words in Nazareth by doing the same anointed work, only more fully.
- **The new heavens and new earth (Isaiah 65) describe Olam Haba, the age to come.** The passage pictures the quality of shalom in that age. It is not a literal timeline.
- **Judgment and hope belong together for oppressed people.** For people without power, hope includes the promise that the powerful will be held accountable.

#### Main Concepts & Theories

##### 1. The New World of Hope (Isaiah 56:1–8)

- God calls people to "maintain justice and do what is right," and to keep the Sabbath and hold fast to the covenant.
- **Eunuchs:** Deuteronomy 23:1 barred eunuchs from the assembly. Isaiah promises them "a memorial and a name better than sons and daughters" within God's temple. The "dry tree" image ties back to BEMA Episode 137 and to the Ethiopian eunuch in Acts 8.
- **Foreigners:** God's house "will be called a house of prayer for all nations" (56:7). Jesus later quotes this line when he cleanses the temple.
- **Not a transaction:** Keeping the Sabbath here does not earn a reward. It is a poetic way of describing people who "trust the story" of God's grace and love.
- The vision moves from *exclusion* (Torah's temple-assembly rules) to *inclusion* in God's mission, covenant, and people.

##### 2. Our Own Worst Enemy (Isaiah 56:9–57:13)

- **Watchmen:** The NET notes that the term most likely means spiritual leaders, prophets and priests responsible for the people's moral direction. Isaiah calls them blind, "mute dogs" that cannot bark, and shepherds who seek their own gain.
- **Idolatry under "every spreading tree":** Large, mature trees were sites of pagan worship and shrine prostitution and are often linked with Asherah poles. That is why the passage connects them with lust. Jeremiah 17 uses the same imagery.
- **A possible historical setting:** Marty suggests Isaiah may be criticizing exiles who chose to stay in Babylon or Persia because they liked the comforts and compromises of empire, not because they cared about God's mission.

##### 3. God Is Willing to Forgive (Isaiah 57:14–21)

- God lives "in a high and holy place, but also with the one who is contrite and lowly in spirit."
- "Peace, peace, to those far and near," while "there is no peace… for the wicked." Forgiveness is offered, but people have to want it and take part.
- **Chapter breaks are not original.** The quotation at the end of chapter 57 runs straight into chapter 58, which is a reminder to read across modern chapter divisions.

##### 4. The Substance of Obedience: True Fasting (Isaiah 58)

- **The complaint:** "Why have we fasted, and you have not seen it?" The people seem eager to know God, but they do not see him acting. Marty suggests this frustration may help explain why they turned to idols that *seemed* to work.
- **God's answer:** You fast, but on the same day you exploit your workers, quarrel, and fight. Going through the motions does not move God.
- **The fast God chooses (58:6–12):**
  1. Loose the chains of injustice and break every yoke.
  2. Share your food with the hungry.
  3. Give the poor wanderer shelter.
  4. Clothe the naked.
  5. Do not turn away from your own flesh and blood.
  6. Do away with the pointing finger and malicious talk.
- **The result:** "Your light will break forth like the dawn." God answers, "Here am I." The people become "Repairer of Broken Walls, Restorer of Streets with Dwellings." This reverses the broken walls from the start of Isaiah.
- **Justice "as described by the Bible":** Marty insists this is not partisan politics but a plain reading of the text. Brent adds that this is a *community* calling. No single person has to carry the whole burden of feeding every hungry person.
- **Sabbath (58:13–14):** Marty's summary of Sabbath is "we rest, we play, no work, God loves us." It can be twisted into self-indulgence. Real Sabbath means taking part in *God's* delight, which echoes Eden, whose name means "delight."

##### 5. Sin, Separation, and God's Reach (Isaiah 59)

- "Surely the arm of the Lord is not too short to save… but your iniquities have separated you from your God."
- **Context over proof-text:** The verse is often used to make a systematic theological point about sinful human nature. In Isaiah it is more poetic and immediate: living in rebellion cuts you off from God's work, and God is ready to repair the relationship now.
- Verses 3–15 describe a society without justice. The people "hatch the eggs of vipers" and "the way of peace they do not know."
- **A possible redaction:** Isaiah 59:21 is printed as prose in most translations, while chapters 59 and 60 are poetry. Marty wonders whether two sources were woven together there, while admitting the guess is uncertain.

##### 6. The Glory of Hope and the Anointed Servant (Isaiah 60–61)

- **Isaiah 60:** "Arise, shine, for your light has come." Nations and kings come to Zion's light, and scattered children come home.
- **Isaiah 61:1–3:** "The Spirit of the Sovereign Lord is on me…"
  - The "me" was first Isaiah, the prophet anointed to proclaim good news.
  - Jesus reads the passage in Nazareth (Luke 4) and says it is fulfilled. Marty's comparison: Isaiah set a record, and Jesus broke it.
- **Brent's connection:** The "oaks of righteousness" in 61:3 contrast with the idolatrous "oaks" of Isaiah 57:5. Marty calls this a small two-person *chavruta* (paired study) moment.

##### 7. Not Giving Up (Isaiah 62–63)

- "For Zion's sake I will not keep silent." Zion gets new names: *Hephzibah* ("my delight is in her") and *Beulah* ("married").
- **Who is speaking?** The hosts can't decide between God and the prophet. The NET says the speaker in verse 6 is "probably the prophet." If the watchmen are prophets, Isaiah may be pointing to disciples or a prophetic school, which fits the theory that later Isaiah came from a prophetic lineage.
- **Pathos:** The question matters less because the prophet speaks in solidarity with God's own feelings, a theme from the start of the series.
- **Isaiah 63:7–10:** The prophet retells "the kindnesses of the Lord." To keep from giving up on a people, you have to keep telling the truth about who God has been.
- **Holy Spirit:** In the Hebrew Bible the phrase appears only here and in Psalm 51.

##### 8. Guilt, Shame, and Conviction (Isaiah 64)

- "Oh, that you would rend the heavens and come down!"
- **Guilt is admitted:** "All our righteous acts are like filthy rags."
- **Shame is refused:** "Yet you, Lord, are our Father. We are the clay, you are the potter."
- **The distinction:**
  - *Guilt:* "I have lied." It names an action.
  - *Shame:* "I am a liar." It makes the action an identity.
  - *Conviction:* Admitting guilt while staying secure in who you are, which leads to repentance: "Do not remember our sins forever."

##### 9. Juice in the Cluster and the New Creation (Isaiah 65)

- Verses 1–7 contain severe judgment on a people who provoke God "to my very face."
- **Verse 8:** "As when juice is still found in a cluster of grapes and people say, 'Don't destroy it, there is still a blessing in it'…" God will not destroy everyone. Something in his people is still worth redeeming.
- **New heavens and new earth (65:17–25):**
  - No more weeping and no infant deaths.
  - People live in the houses they build and eat from the vineyards they plant.
  - "The wolf and the lamb will feed together."
- **Olam Haba:** The vision describes the *quality* of the age to come, its shalom. It is not a literal, mechanical forecast.

##### 10. Critique of the Cult and the Final Word (Isaiah 66)

- "Heaven is my throne… Where is the house you will build for me?"
- Sacrificing a bull "is like one who kills a person." Marty wonders whether Isaiah is condemning the temple system (the "cult," in the technical academic sense of formal liturgical practice), or simply balancing a reliance on ritual, as in Isaiah 1. Some interpreters read it as aimed only at pagan sacrifice, but Marty doesn't see that explicitly in the text.
- **Maternal imagery (66:12–13):** "Dandled on her knees" (the NET has "play on her knees"). Marty sees a possible link to Jesus' lament over Jerusalem, "as a hen gathers her chicks," which he tied to Euripides' *The Trojan Women* in an earlier session.
- **Judgment as hope:** For Isaiah's audience, who were politically oppressed, God holding the powerful accountable was itself good news. Western listeners, who usually hold more power, may find this harder to relate to.

##### 11. Brent's Structural Proposal (Chiasm)

Following the BibleProject's outline of Isaiah 56–66:

- **A** — 56a / 66b: All nations invited into God's covenant family
  - **B** — 56b–58 / 65–66a: Contrast between the wicked and the servants
    - **C** — 59 / 63–64: Prayers of repentance
      - **D** — 60–62: The servant announces God's kingdom

Brent's additional observations:

- In this section, the phrase "Sovereign Lord" appears only in chapters 56, 61 (twice, framing the chapter), and 65.
- Isaiah 61 has a series of "instead of" exchanges: beauty instead of ashes, joy instead of mourning, and a double portion instead of shame.
- He proposes **Isaiah 61:4–6** as the center: rebuilding the ancient ruins, foreigners working the fields, and God's people called "priests of the Lord."

##### 12. Marty's Review of All of Isaiah

**Part One (First Isaiah): As a Father Disciplines His Son**

1. You're in a mess.
2. Trust the story; God will save you.
3. Well, that didn't work.
4. Consequences.

**Part Two (Second and Third Isaiah): Partnering with Dad**

1. Suffering is the way to restoration.
2. Perseverance produces fruit.
3. Say yes to building a new world with Dad.

Marty draws on his own experience of disciplining his son. Healthy discipline is not driven by revenge. It requires staying with the consequences even after the lesson seems learned, and it leads to a chance to build something better together.

#### Examples & Applications

- **Fasting today:** Any spiritual practice (fasting, church attendance, devotions, worship music) can become "going through the motions." Isaiah 58 asks whether those practices make us more just toward workers, neighbors, and the vulnerable. One test: does your spiritual discipline cost you anything on behalf of someone else?
- **How we treat employees:** "On the day of your fasting… you exploit all your workers." A modern parallel is a business owner who is devout on Sunday but underpays staff, or a consumer who never asks how the things they buy were made.
- **Rethinking "love":** Brent warns that everyone has their own idea of what is loving. Isaiah 58 gives concrete content: food, shelter, clothing, and freedom.
- **Sabbath:** A Sabbath spent entirely on personal pleasure can drift from its purpose. Practices like a shared meal, hospitality, rest that restores others, or celebrating God's goodness with community line up better with "God's delight."
- **Guilt versus shame in practice:** A parent who says "you did something wrong" instead of "you are bad," or a person in recovery who owns their actions without reducing their identity to them, shows the posture of Isaiah 64.
- **Welcoming outsiders:** Churches and communities can ask who today plays the role of the "eunuch" or "foreigner": people who feel structurally excluded and say, "I am only a dry tree."
- **Feeling God is absent:** Like the people asking "Why have we fasted and you have not seen it?", believers may feel God isn't moving. The temptation is to turn to whatever "works," such as money, comfort, or distraction. Isaiah calls people back toward justice-shaped faithfulness instead.
- **Community-scale justice:** As Brent notes, food banks, shelters, refugee support, and advocacy work best as shared responsibilities, not individual guilt trips.
- **Reading in context:** Isaiah 59:2 and Isaiah 61:1–3 show how familiar "proof texts" gain depth when read inside their original setting.

#### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

- **Deuteronomy 23 compared with Isaiah 56:** How does Scripture develop its understanding of who belongs among God's people? Trace this through Acts 8 (the Ethiopian eunuch) and Acts 10 (Cornelius).
- **The authorship of Isaiah:** Look into the theory of First, Second, and Third Isaiah, and the idea of a prophetic school continuing Isaiah's message.
- **Redaction in Isaiah 59:21:** Why is the verse rendered as prose? Robert Alter's translation and commentary on the Hebrew Bible may help.
- **Chiastic structure:** Study the BibleProject's Isaiah poster and test Brent's proposal that Isaiah 61:4–6 is the center.
- **Sacred trees and Asherah worship:** Read Nogah Hareuveni's work on biblical landscape and plants, along with Jeremiah 17 and related passages.
- **Jesus' use of Isaiah:** Luke 4 (Isaiah 61), Mark 11 (Isaiah 56:7), and Jesus' attitude toward the temple, both respecting it and predicting its end.
- **"Holy Spirit" in the Hebrew Bible:** Compare Isaiah 63:10–11 with Psalm 51:11.
- **Olam Haba:** How did Jewish and early Christian thought understand "the age to come"? How should the new heavens and new earth be read?
- **Aleph Beta resources:** Rabbi David Fohrman's series *King Hezekiah, the Almost Messiah* explores the Immanuel sign in Isaiah 7 and the purpose of Isaiah 1–39.
- **Earlier BEMA connections:** Episode 137 (the dry tree), the "prophetic table" episode at the end of Session 2, and Session 3's discussion of the hen-and-chicks lament and Euripides.
- **Guilt, shame, and conviction:** Psychological and theological work on the difference between shame and guilt, applied to spiritual formation.

#### Comprehension Questions

1. How does Isaiah 56:1–8 change the picture of who may belong to God's people, compared with Deuteronomy 23? Why do the hosts say the "new world of hope" is really a *renewed* world?
2. In Isaiah 58, what complaint do the people bring to God about their fasting? How does God answer, and what does he say true fasting involves?
3. Why does Marty argue that Isaiah 59:1–2 should not be read mainly as a statement about humanity's sinful nature? What does the passage mean in its immediate context?
4. Using Isaiah 64, explain the difference between guilt, shame, and conviction. How does the prophet admit sin without letting it define the people's identity?
5. Describe Marty's two-part review of Isaiah through the picture of a father disciplining his son. What are the steps in each part?

#### Personalized Summary

Isaiah ends with hope that is grounded in reality, not with easy triumph. The new world God promises is the world he wanted from the beginning. In it, eunuchs and foreigners are welcomed, the hungry are fed, the oppressed go free, and God's people become "Repairers of Broken Walls." Marty and Brent are honest that the main obstacle to this world is usually God's own people: blind watchmen, comfortable compromisers, and worshipers who fast while exploiting others. Even so, God keeps saying "Here am I," his arm is not too short to save, and there is still juice in the cluster of grapes. For you, the challenge is personal and shared: let your spiritual practices produce justice, keep Sabbath as a way into God's delight, own your guilt without taking on shame, and keep partnering with your Father in building the age to come. As Marty's review puts it, discipline leads to restoration, perseverance produces fruit, and faithfulness means saying yes to building a new world with Dad.
