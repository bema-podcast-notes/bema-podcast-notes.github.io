### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** BEMA 365: Isaiah 6, Cultural Context (Unclean Lips and the Burning Coal)  
**Hosts:** Brent Billings, with guests Elle Grover Fricks and Josh Bossé (Marty Solomon not present)  
**Focus:** Isaiah 6:1–7, with references to Isaiah 1:18, the nature of biblical prophecy, and Assyrian divination rituals

This is a deep-dive episode. Brent, Elle, and Josh stop at Isaiah 6, the call narrative of Isaiah (Yeshayahu), to look at it more closely. They start by tying up loose ends from the last episode: what the Hebrew behind "Come now, let us reason together" means, and what archaeology says about Israel's early kings. Next they review what a prophet is. They describe prophets as people who feel deep grief over the world, who share a vision of something better, and who try to put an overwhelming experience into human words. They then look at the opening verses of Isaiah 6: the meaning of King Uzziah's name and death, Isaiah's confession of "unclean lips," and the tension between collective responsibility and individual guilt. Finally, Elle sets Isaiah's mouth-cleansing next to the purification rituals of Assyrian *bārû* diviner-priests. She shows how Isaiah's vision borrows a familiar cultural pattern and then reverses it to say something new about God and about us.

### Key Takeaways

- **"Come now, let us reason together" is an invitation, not an order.** The Hebrew uses a plural imperative ("walk, y'all") softened with "please," followed by the cohortative mood, which expresses desire: *"Oh, that we might reason together."*
- **Archaeology confirms kings well before Josiah.** Inscriptions mention the "House of David" and King Ahab, so the historical record of Israel's monarchy reaches back further than is sometimes assumed.
- **Prophets speak in images and approximations.** The phrase usually translated "Thus says the Lord" (*koh amar*) carries the sense of "This is *like* what God says." The prophet is a human doing their best to put divine experience into words.
- **The Hebrew word for prophet (*navi*) is linked to the image of dripping.** God is like a honeycomb, and the prophet catches a single drop and holds it up for people to see.
- **Prophecy is conditional and calls for a response.** Even pronouncements of judgment can be withdrawn, as with Jonah and Nineveh, because the future stays open to human participation.
- **Prophets tend to appear when people aren't listening.** When God's voice is being ignored, prophets "bang on the scene." A prophetic tone is not something to adopt casually or permanently.
- **Priests and prophets sit on a spectrum.** Priests are collaborative and depend on consent. Prophets are disruptive, like the body's pain response: necessary, but no one should want more of it than needed.
- **Uzziah's name carries meaning.** *Oz* means fierce strength and *Yah* refers to God. "In the year that King Uzziah died" can be read as "in the year that the fierce strength of God died." Uzziah's fall came from taking over the priest's role, and that priest's name, Azariah, contains *ezer*, "help."
- **Isaiah confesses collective uncleanness.** He says "I am a man of unclean lips, and I live among a people of unclean lips." He accepts responsibility without first trying to work out his personal guilt, and only then is he cleansed.
- **Torah never mentions "unclean lips."** No purity law covers it. Elle suggests the image makes more sense against Assyrian ritual practice.
- **The Assyrian *bārû* ritual is reversed in three ways.** (1) The coal is already burning, so God is present before anyone calls on him. (2) Isaiah says "I am impure" instead of the ritual's "my lips are pure." (3) One act cleanses completely, with no list of ritual steps.

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. Loose Ends from the Previous Episode

##### "Come Now, Let Us Reason Together" (Isaiah 1:18)

- The opening verb is a **plural imperative** ("walk," "come"), addressed to the community as a whole.
- It is followed by **"please"** (the particle *na*), which softens the command.
- "Let us reason together" is in the **cohortative**, a Hebrew form that expresses desire or longing.
- Elle compares this to the Exodus story. God doesn't flatly declare that Pharaoh *will* see his glory. The cohortative reads more like *"Oh, that he might see my heaviness."*
- **Result:** The verse is God's heartfelt wish to sit down with his people, not a legal summons.

##### The Archaeological Record

- Marty had said that history becomes firmly verifiable around **Josiah**. Elle offered a friendly correction.
- **King David:** An inscription that names David's dynasty (commonly identified as the Tel Dan Stele) shows he was a real figure, not a legend like King Arthur.
- **King Ahab:** A northern inscription records Ahab supplying a large force of chariots and horses for a battle.
- **Omri** and other kings before Josiah are also attested.
- **Wider point:** Science keeps updating itself as new evidence appears. Elle suggests the humanities, and theology, could learn to "change my mind publicly" in the same way.

#### 2. What Is a Prophet? (Review and Expansion)

##### Three Elements of Prophetic Experience

Earlier episodes with Reid and Marty named two elements. Josh adds a third:

1. **Pathos:** Feeling what is wrong in the world very deeply.
2. **Imagination:** A God-given ability to describe how things *could* be.
3. **Human limitation:** The overwhelming experience goes beyond human words. The rabbis noted that prophetic visions vary a great deal. Some, like Isaiah's, are cosmic and overpowering. Others, like Moses's, are calm and conversational. This variety reflects the limits of human expression, and it is not a way to rank prophets.

##### "This Is Like What God Says"

- *Koh* means "thus" or "like this," and *amar* means "said."
- Elle argues this phrasing reduces the "thundering from Mount Sinai" tone often attached to prophecy.
- The prophet stands among the people, not above them, and says, "This is what I think I'm hearing from the Spirit."
- Josh points out the humor in it: the Hebrew sounds a lot like the modern English "God was like…"

##### *Navi* and the Drip Image

- *Navi* (prophet) is linked to an image of **dripping**.
- God is the honeycomb. The prophet catches one drop and shows it to the people, aware that there is far more they cannot grasp.

##### Communal and Personal Prophecy

- Prophets usually speak to the **community**, which is an important correction for individualistic Western readers.
- Prophecy can also be **personal**. Nathan confronts David, and Samuel speaks to Eli.
- The **Didache**, an early Christian handbook, warns against traveling "prophets" who try to direct people in communities they don't belong to.

##### Prophecy Is About the Present, and the Future Stays Open

- If the future were fixed, there would be no need for trust.
- Judgment pronouncements are often reversible. In Jonah, God doesn't say "repent or else." He announces judgment, the people repent, and God relents.
- The purpose of prophecy is to **provoke a response**.

##### When Prophets Appear

- Elle traces a pattern: prophets show up when people are **not practicing *shema*** (hearing and obeying).
  - Abimelech heeds his dream from God and does what is right.
  - Samuel listens to God's voice and acts.
  - David does not listen, so Nathan has to come.
  - Ahab does not listen, so Elijah has to come.
- Life with God can be seen as a cycle of repentance (*teshuvah*), "living out the book of Judges all the time." As we mature, repentance becomes smaller and more frequent, like Brother Lawrence's *Practice of the Presence of God*.
- **A caution:** Being loud or obnoxious is not the same as being prophetic.

#### 3. The Priest–Prophet Continuum

| Priest | Prophet |
| --- | --- |
| Collaborative and facilitative | Disruptive and confrontational |
| Requires consent: people bring their offerings | Doesn't wait for permission (e.g., lying naked on the ground) |
| A steady rhythm of returning and repenting | Points to the elephant in the room |
| Our primary calling ("a kingdom of priests") | Necessary, but best kept to a minimum |

- Josh compares prophets to **the body's pain response**: unpleasant, necessary, and a sign that something is wrong if it is happening all the time.
- **Application:** Be "more priestly" in conversations, especially on social media.

#### 4. Isaiah 6:1–7, Surface-Level Observations

##### The Placement of the Call Narrative

- Isaiah's call comes in chapter 6, not chapter 1.
- Chapters 1–5 are a downward spiral of failed expectations: the vineyard that produced bad fruit, and accusation after accusation.
- In the middle of that pain, God **invites Isaiah into his presence**. People are not left to "sit there and think about what you've done."
- Elle says we leave sin behind not by obsessing over it, but by seeing how vast and weighty (*kavod*) God is. In comparison, our sin looks like "walking around with our pants down." We pull them up and get on with the work.

##### "In the Year That King Uzziah Died"

- *Uzziah* combines *oz* (fierce strength, likened to the strength of a female goat) and *Yah* (God).
- Read this way, it means "in the year the fierce strength of God died."
- Uzziah was a great king until he **offered incense in the temple**, a priestly act, and was struck with leprosy (2 Chronicles 26).
- The high priest who confronted him was **Azariah**, whose name contains *ezer*, "help."
- **Lesson:** Fierce strength is not supposed to *replace* help. When we are in prophetic "fierce strength" mode, we can push aside the people who could help us, which is the priestly role.

##### A Moment of Lost Hope

- Isaiah expected God to step in, defend his people, and restore justice and purity. Instead, the king was dead and exile was coming.
- Into that disappointment comes the vision: "I saw the Lord, high and exalted."

#### 5. Collective Responsibility vs. Individual Guilt

- Marty's refrain: **"All are responsible; some are guilty."**
- Isaiah confesses: "Woe to me… I am a man of unclean lips, and I live among a people of unclean lips."
  - Robert Alter notes that "I am ruined" could also be read as "I am struck dumb."
  - Isaiah does *not* say "Why was I born into this generation?" or "I'm one of the good Israelites."
- **The individualist trap:** "I'm a Christian, but not *that* kind of Christian." The instinct to separate ourselves from the "bad ones" keeps us from seeing our own involvement.
- **A modern example:** We didn't decide to have minerals mined by exploited workers in the Congo or garments made in Bangladeshi sweatshops, but we benefit from both.
- Elle adds a nuance. When the culture ranks groups by how much they suffer, people may respond with self-pity or try to prove their own suffering in order to re-establish their "purity."
- **The pattern in Isaiah:**
  1. Honest acknowledgment of being part of an unclean people.
  2. God cleanses immediately.
  3. Isaiah says, "Here am I, send me."
- God doesn't wait for a long self-justifying speech. In effect he says, "You are welcome here. Now stop dithering and go do what I need."

#### 6. Why "Unclean Lips"? The Cultural Context

##### The Puzzle

- The Torah has many purity categories: immersion in a *mikveh*, cleansing a house with mold, and so on. None of them involves **unclean lips**.
- The Psalms do touch on this in a general way ("May the words of my mouth…"), but lips as a *ritual purity* category doesn't come from Torah.
- Compare Zechariah 3, where the high priest receives clean clothes. That image *does* come from Exodus 19, where Moses has the people wash their clothes before meeting God.

##### Rabbinic and Other Explanations

- Isaiah sinned in speech, or sin in his heart came out through his mouth ("out of the overflow of the heart the mouth speaks").
- He was defiled while caring for the leprous king. Elle notes that such a detail would probably have been mentioned in the text.
- Rabbinic tradition links leprosy to **gossip and slander**, which connects to the lips.

##### Assyria's Four Types of Magicians

Assyria was the dominant culture of the region. Depending on how Isaiah is dated, its practices were either generally influential or literally "happening in the building next to you."

| Type | Role | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Bārû** | Diviner-priest: reads bird flight and animal livers | Good |
| **Āšipu** | Exorcist-priest: removes curses, performs atonement | Good |
| **Kaššāpu** | Dangerous sorcerer, male or female; Hammurabi's laws prescribe drowning | Bad |
| **Maqlû-type** (witch) | Steals beauty from women to ensnare men; burned | Bad |

- *Kaššāpu* is related to the word behind Exodus 22:18 ("Do not suffer a witch to live," KJV). The law was aimed at specific practices of that region, not at fairy-tale witches.
- Example: Sennacherib asked a *bārû* to find out why his father died.

##### The *Bārû* Purification and Divination Ritual

1. Wash, clean clothing, and **anoint with oil**. Anointing marks a person or place as a portal to heaven, as when Jacob anoints the stone at Bethel.
2. **Purify the lips with cedar**, sometimes by chewing it. Ritual texts repeat the formula *"I am the one whose lips are pure."*
3. Put cedar in the hair and in the fringes of the garment.
4. **Set the cedar on fire** to summon the gods.
5. When the gods appear, seated on great thrones, offer water, a special drink, tribute, and plenty of bread.
6. Throughout, plead for reliable information.
7. The gods announce that they have written their decision in the **entrails of a sacrificial animal**.

##### Similarities

- A mouth-cleansing act involving wood or fire prepares a person to speak before the divine.
- The deity is seated on a throne.

##### Three Key Reversals in Isaiah 6

1. **The coal is already burning.** In the Assyrian rite, the human lights the cedar to *summon* the gods. In Isaiah, the ember is already lit. God is present before Isaiah calls, seeks, or asks, even "in the year the fierce strength of God died." It is also more frightening: facing a fiery, winged being holding a burning coal is far more intense than a ritual you control yourself.
2. **"Impure," not "pure."** The *bārû* claims "my lips are pure." Isaiah says "I am a man of unclean lips." Standing before God rests on humility and honesty, not on having checked every box.
3. **One act, completely sufficient.** The Assyrian ritual has many stages. In Isaiah, one touch of the coal removes guilt and atones for sin. Nothing more is needed to be ready for intimacy with God and for his calling.

##### A Bonus Possibility: The Lamentation Priest

- Elle also describes an Assyrian **lamentation priest** (*kalû*) ritual for making a mourning drum. A black bull is surrounded by a curtain and has incantations whispered into its ears. The priest purifies the mouth, cedar resin and incense are used, flour is circled around the bull, and the bull's hide becomes the drum.
- Seen this way, Isaiah's mouth-purification could hint that he is being commissioned as **Israel's funeral officiant**.
- Josh suggests that fits Jeremiah better. Isaiah's name means "Yah saves."

### Examples & Applications

- **Social media prophets:** Josh and Elle warn against the "thundering from the mountaintop" posture online. Most situations call for a priestly, collaborative voice, not a prophetic one.
- **Responding to a mass shooting:** Josh imagines a prophet speaking to such a tragedy. The words address the immediate event and also reflect much larger systemic issues, which is why prophetic language struggles to say it all.
- **Supply chains and global injustice:** Phones that depend on Congolese minerals and clothing from Bangladeshi factories show collective responsibility without personal guilt.
- **"Not *that* kind of Christian":** Our instinct to manage how others see us is the opposite of Isaiah's confession.
- **Small, constant repentance:** Brother Lawrence's practice of returning one's attention to God in tiny moments shows a mature rhythm of *teshuvah*.
- **Over-processing sin:** Elle describes the urge to add layers of steps (telling the small group, getting accountability texts, and so on). These can be good, but they aren't prerequisites for intimacy with God. The coal is enough.
- **"Finish sinning first, then get right with God":** The Assyrian model puts the person in control of when they meet the divine. Isaiah's already-burning coal says God has been there the whole time.
- **Changing your mind publicly:** Science constantly revises its conclusions, which is a model for humility in theology and conversation.

### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

1. **Hebrew verb forms:** Study the cohortative and jussive and how they change the meaning of familiar verses (e.g., Isaiah 1:18 and Exodus 14).
2. **Biblical archaeology:** Look at the Tel Dan Stele, the Kurkh Monolith (Ahab), and the Mesha Stele (Omri) to see what they confirm about the divided monarchy.
3. **Abraham Joshua Heschel and Walter Brueggemann on prophecy:** Compare *The Prophets* and *The Prophetic Imagination* with the "this is like what God says" reading.
4. **The Didache:** Explore early Christian guidance on testing traveling prophets.
5. **Uzziah's story:** Read 2 Chronicles 26 and consider the boundaries between royal and priestly roles.
6. **Assyrian religion:** Research the *bārû*, *āšipu*, and *kalû*. The hosts recommend Lewis Spence's *Myths and Legends of Babylonia and Assyria*, the Wikipedia entry on *bārû*, and a longer JSTOR article listed in the show notes.
7. **Seraphim:** Consider the link between *saraph* ("burning" or "fiery serpent"), Numbers 21, and Isaiah's vision, including Robert Alter's question about why burning beings need tongs.
8. **Purity in Torah vs. prophetic texts:** Compare Zechariah 3, Exodus 19, and Isaiah 6 to see how the prophets adapt purity imagery.
9. **Collective responsibility in Scripture:** Look at Daniel 9 and Nehemiah 9, where individuals confess the sins of the nation as their own.
10. **Isaiah 36–39:** Elle plans to return when Marty reaches these chapters to explore more Assyrian context.

### Comprehension Questions

1. How does the cohortative mood change the meaning of "Come now, let us reason together" in Isaiah 1:18, and what does it say about God's posture toward his people?
2. What does the phrase *koh amar* ("this is like what God says") suggest about how prophets communicate, and how does that challenge a "thundering from Sinai" picture of prophecy?
3. What does the name "Uzziah" mean, and how does his story (including the high priest Azariah) deepen the meaning of "in the year that King Uzziah died"?
4. Why is "unclean lips" a puzzling phrase from a Torah perspective, and what Assyrian ritual may explain why Isaiah uses it?
5. What are the three ways Isaiah 6 reverses the Assyrian *bārû* purification ritual, and what does each one teach about God and about how we come before him?

### Personal Summary

This episode shows how much more there is in Isaiah 6 than the familiar Sunday-school version. Prophets are not angry people shouting from a mountaintop. They are humans catching one drop of honey from an enormous hive and saying, "This is *like* what God is saying." Isaiah's call comes in the year Israel's "fierce strength of God" died, after five chapters of failure. In that moment he confesses that he and his people are unclean, and he accepts responsibility without trying to prove he is one of the good ones. Against the Assyrian diviner-priests, who chewed cedar, lit fires to summon their gods, and declared "my lips are pure," Isaiah's vision reverses every step. God is already present, honesty about impurity replaces claims of purity, and one burning coal is enough. The message for us is to be more priestly than prophetic, to take collective responsibility without drowning in guilt, and to stop dithering, receive the cleansing, and say, "Here am I. Send me."
