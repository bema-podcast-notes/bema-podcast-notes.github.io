### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** BEMA 380 — Psalm 13  
**Hosts:** Brent Billings, with guests Reed Dent and Josh Bosse  
**Focus:** Psalm 13 (with cross-references to Genesis 4, Exodus 3 and 33, Deuteronomy 31–34, Job 13, Isaiah 54, Ezekiel 39, and Revelation 6)

This episode is part of BEMA's series on the Psalms and is Josh Bosse's first appearance in it. Brent, Reed, and Josh walk slowly through Psalm 13, a short and raw lament that opens with the question "How long, O Lord?" They use Walter Brueggemann's framework of Psalms of orientation, disorientation, and new orientation. They look at Hebrew word choices such as *shakach* (forget), *gamal* (deal fully with), *hesed* (steadfast love), and the word for rejoice, and they trace the idiom of God's "hidden face" through Scripture. Along the way they talk about how to care for people who feel abandoned by God. Their main point is that Scripture gives honest space to the experience of God's absence, and that trust and anguish can be held together, "danced" with, until something new arrives.

### Key Takeaways

- Psalm 13 is a classic **psalm of disorientation**, but it ends with a sudden **turn toward trust and rejoicing**. Many psalms are mixed, the way real life is.
- The psalm skips any polite opening and starts with **"How long?"**, a cry also heard in Job and from the martyrs in Revelation 6:10.
- The Hebrew word for "forget" here, ***shakach***, carries a sense of **misplacing or losing track of** something. It is less like willful rejection (*nashah*) and more like Kevin being left behind in *Home Alone*. That changes how the psalmist experiences God's absence.
- **God's "hidden face"** is a biblical idiom that can mean exile caused by sin (Ezekiel 39), a temporary withdrawal that ends in compassion (Isaiah 54), or absence with no visible cause (Job 13).
- Laments like Psalm 13 are **as fully Scripture as Romans 8**. As Frederick Buechner argued, we have to admit God's *visible absence* before we can speak credibly of God's *invisible presence*.
- When caring for someone in this place, **easy answers and triumphalism hurt**. Grief is "a dance": let the hurting person lead.
- The enemy grows from **singular to plural** (verses 2–4), building a crescendo of despair. Then verse 5 flips it, and the *same Hebrew word* for rejoice moves from the enemies to the psalmist.
- The root idea of "rejoice" may involve **circling or dancing**. That links the psalm's movement to the image of grief as a dance.
- Read through Christ, the psalm suggests that **salvation may lie beyond the fall, not instead of it**. Resurrection means things can go wrong and still end well.

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. Brueggemann's Framework: Orientation, Disorientation, New Orientation

Reed brings in a framework from Walter Brueggemann's *The Message of the Psalms*, which his community has used for years:

1. **Psalms of orientation** come from a place of well-being. They express gratitude, security, and confidence in an orderly creation.
2. **Psalms of disorientation** come from seasons of hurt, suffering, alienation, or death. They often sound angry, self-pitying, resentful, fearful, or anxious.
3. **Psalms of new orientation** celebrate a surprising breakthrough: rescue from the pit, despair turned to joy.

Reed stresses that this is a helpful lens, not a structure built into the Psalter. Psalm 13 is mostly disorientation, but its ending reaches toward new orientation. That mixture matches lived experience: hard seasons still hold moments of remembering and thanksgiving.

#### 2. "How Long?": A Cry Without Preamble

- The psalm starts with no formal introduction and goes "straight for the jugular."
- "How long" appears four times in verses 1–2, which makes the repetition itself a form of protest.
- The specific situation is never named. That openness lets readers in many different circumstances pray it as their own.
- Two things exist side by side: the **expectation** that God *must* act ("I'm not going to make it forever"), and the **fear** that God might not.
- Brent pictures David on the run in the wilderness, walking in his thoughts until he finally snaps: "How long am I going to do this, Lord?"

#### 3. Two Hebrew Words for "Forget"

With help from the show's Hebrew teacher, Elle, Reed explains the difference:

| Hebrew Word | Sense | Picture |
|---|---|---|
| *Nashah* | Willful neglect, deliberate abandonment | Lovers breaking a commitment and resolving to forget each other |
| *Shakach* (used in Psalm 13) | Misplacing, losing track of, being unable to locate | The Hebrew title of *Home Alone* uses this word: Kevin wasn't rejected, he was overlooked |

- *Shakach* is also the word in Deuteronomy's warnings not to "forget" God once Israel prospers. It means losing sight of a treasured thing because life got in the way.
- The hosts are careful about calling anything "accidental" on God's part. The point is the **psalmist's perception**: "I haven't been thrown out. I've been mislaid."
- **Why it matters:** Brent suggests this helps explain the ending. The psalmist gives God the benefit of the doubt (*ayin tovah*, a "good eye"): "Surely you didn't mean to throw me out." That makes trust in verse 5 possible.
- Josh adds that trust and anguish **coexist from start to finish**. Believing God will eventually show up does not remove the raw pain of waiting now.

#### 4. The Hidden Face of God

"How long will you hide your face from me?" adds a sharper edge. If God is *hiding*, the psalmist cannot force God to appear. The hosts trace the idiom through Scripture:

- **Genesis 4:14 (Cain):** Josh notes that the first use of this word for "hide" reverses the idiom. Cain says *he* will be hidden from God's face, cut off by his own guilt. This raises the question the sufferer asks: "Did I do something?"
- **Ezekiel 39:23:** God hides God's face as a description of **exile because of Israel's unfaithfulness**.
- **Isaiah 54:8:** "In a surge of anger I hid my face from you for a moment, but with everlasting kindness I will have compassion on you." The hidden face is **not forever**, so "How long?" has an answer, even if no one can say when.
- **Job 13:24:** Right after insisting he has done nothing wrong, Job asks, "Why do you hide your face?" Sometimes no guilt can be found. Josh reads Job as wrestling with the righteous person caught up in a national exile they did nothing personally to cause.
- **Moses:** Moses hides his own face at the burning bush (Exodus 3:6), is told no one can see God's face (Exodus 33), and is remembered as one who knew God "face to face" (Deuteronomy 34:10).

Josh also points out that exile happens on **two levels**, national and individual. Some people in exile will personally never see the return, which deepens the anguish of "Will you forget me *forever*?"

Reed's conclusion: the text covers "every possible permutation" of God's face, near and far, hidden and seen, which fits the Psalms as a book for every season of life.

#### 5. Verse 2: Wrestling Alone with One's Thoughts

- "Wrestle with my thoughts" (literally, taking counsel in one's own soul) and "sorrow in my heart day after day" describe what the hidden face *feels like*: endless inner churning with no one to shed light.
- Seeing God's face means joy, shalom, and trust. Its absence shows up as constant anguish and loneliness.

#### 6. Verses 3–4: Escalation and Desperation

- The tone shifts from *questions* to *demands*: "Look on me and answer." Honesty can replace decorum in prayer. That doesn't license irreverence for its own sake, but God can take blunt words.
- **"Give light to my eyes" versus "sleep in death"**: light in the eyes signals vitality, the opposite of dullness. Deuteronomy 34:7 says Moses' eyes were "not dim" even at death.
- "Sleep of death" doubles the darkness. Josh compares it to **Jonah asleep in the storm**, so drowned in despair that you stop fighting.
- The psalmist asks for a **response, not necessarily a solution**: "If I hear your voice, I'll have the courage to uncurl from this fetal position."
- **Crescendo:** "my enemy" (singular) becomes "my foes" (plural). The weight piles up, one thing on top of another.

#### 7. Verses 5–6: The Turn

- Fear of being forgotten becomes **remembering** God's goodness. Anguish turns into rejoicing, and desperation turns into trust.
- **Possible readings of the turn:**
  - *Brent:* A pot boiling over and then a **deep breath**, a release valve. Once everything is out, the psalmist can say, "But you've been good to me."
  - *Josh:* Having played the worst-case scenario to its end, the psalmist **snaps back**: "No, that could never happen. I remember Egypt, I remember the desert." It's like an anime hero throwing off a pile of attackers.
- **Same word, new subject:** The Hebrew word for "rejoice" is the same in verses 4 and 5. The enemies' rejoicing is replaced by the psalmist's.
- **Rejoicing as a dance:** According to Gesenius, the root idea may involve going in a circle or dancing, which ties into Reed's grief-as-dance image.
- **"Good to me" (*gamal*):** This is not the usual word for good (*tov*). *Gamal* is associated with **weaning a child** (compare Genesis 21:8). Josh hears a child who feels abandoned yet knows the parent has always cared for them: "I don't doubt you, I'm just so alone right now."
- **"Unfailing love" (*hesed*):** Josh notes the word's first appearance is Lot fleeing Sodom (Genesis 19:19). Lot's story looks like a fall, but traced far enough it leads to Ruth and then David. Over a long enough span, *hesed* proves unfailing.

#### 8. A Christ-Shaped Reading

Reed reads the psalm through Jesus' life. What if the enemies really *do* overcome and the fall really *does* happen? Salvation is not escaping the fall but **being raised beyond it**. Josh concludes: "If resurrection is on our side, then it is actually okay if everything goes wrong."

### Examples & Applications

#### Pastoral Care for People in "Psalm 13 Seasons"

Josh, who works in a pastoral role, gives practical guidance:

- **Unhelpful:** Quick spiritual platitudes such as "Don't you know God is everywhere? I see God in the coffee stain on my napkin!" Also jumping to "just get a better attitude" or "go to therapy and it'll all go away."
- **Helpful:**
  1. **Get concrete.** Ask what the suffering actually is. "Who is this enemy? What thoughts are you wrestling with?" Ask without dismissing.
  2. **Offer parental presence.** Scripture describes our relationship with God as both partnership and *parent and child*. Someone who is suffering often needs the parent: "What's wrong, buddy? Did a bee scare you, or is your leg broken?"
  3. **Sometimes the answer is a hug**, or simply saying, "You don't deserve what's happening to you."
  4. **Help them notice where God may already be present**, for example through a comforting person in their life.

#### "Grief Is a Dance"

Reed shares advice from his friend Chris Nation, a pastor of grief and loss in St. Louis, before visiting a friend who had lost a child:

- Grief is a dance, so **let the grieving person lead**.
- Quoting "we are more than conquerors" too early is like stepping on their feet. Now both people stumble, and it hurts more.
- Josh adds: dancers move in circles but rarely end where they started. Lament psalms help us move with our feelings and arrive somewhere new.
- Reed's contrast: a dance is not a **three-legged race** aiming at a finish line. The point is moving together.

#### Everyday Analogies for Divine Silence

- **The unanswered text:** "Did you forget to check your phone, or are you ignoring me? Did I do something?" Silence can mean a million things.
- **The bullied child** waiting for a parent to come pick them up.
- **Halftime in a losing game:** "We're getting our butts kicked, but we're going to win."
- **Divine peekaboo:** a hidden face you still believe you'll see again.

#### Honest Faith in the Wider World

Buechner's quote (from *Telling the Truth*) applies beyond the church. A faith that only speaks in optimistic terms ("the optimist club") won't be taken seriously by people living through real darkness. Being honest about God's absence makes testimony about God's presence believable.

### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

1. **Brueggemann's *The Message of the Psalms*:** Sort other psalms (e.g., Psalms 22, 42–43, 88, 30, 139) into the orientation, disorientation, and new orientation categories.
2. **The "hidden face" (*hester panim*) theme:** Follow it through Deuteronomy 31:17–18, Psalms 27, 30, 44, 88, and Micah 3:4, and see how later Jewish thought used the idea.
3. **Lament structure:** Study the usual parts of a lament psalm (address, complaint, petition, expression of trust, vow of praise) and compare them with Psalm 13.
4. **Hebrew vocabulary:** *shakach*, *nashah*, *satar* (hide), *gamal*, *hesed*, and the "rejoice" root. Use a lexicon (Gesenius or BDB) to check the nuances discussed.
5. **Job as an exile text:** Explore Josh's reading of Job as an answer to the righteous who suffered in exile.
6. **Psalm 22 and Jesus:** Psalm 22 also moves from "Why have you forsaken me?" to praise. Study how the Gospels use it at the crucifixion.
7. **Frederick Buechner's *Telling the Truth*:** His treatment of the gospel as tragedy, comedy, and fairy tale.
8. **Contrast with Psalm 139:** A psalm about God's inescapable presence, as a counterpoint to Psalm 13 (covered later in the series).
9. **Jewish traditions:** The "life psalm" practice and the Shema as last words; gematria (*ahavah*, "love," equals 13).

### Comprehension Questions

1. What are the three categories in Brueggemann's framework for the Psalms, and why does Psalm 13 fit mostly, but not entirely, in one of them?
2. How does *shakach* (misplace) differ from *nashah* (willful neglect), and how might that difference shape the psalmist's ability to trust God by the end?
3. Name three biblical passages that use the "hidden face" idiom and explain how each gives a different reason for, or result of, God hiding God's face.
4. What literary features in verses 2–5 create a crescendo and then a reversal? Consider the enemy moving from singular to plural, and the repeated word for "rejoice."
5. According to Josh and Reed, what responses help, and what responses hurt, when someone feels that God's face is hidden from them? How does the image of "grief as a dance" capture that?

### Summary

Psalm 13 shows that faith does not mean pretending God always feels close. In six short verses, the psalmist moves from being forgotten (more misplaced than rejected), to the pain of God's hidden face, to desperate demands for an answer, and then, almost suddenly, to trust in God's *hesed* and a vow to sing. The hosts suggest the turn might be a deep breath after letting it all out, or a snap back to remembering what God has done before. Either way, it does not cancel the anguish. The anguish and the trust sit side by side.

The lesson for you is twofold. First, you have permission to pray your own "How long?", bluntly and without dressing it up, because those words are as much Scripture as any victory verse. Second, when others are in that place, the faithful response is not a quick fix but a willingness to join the dance and let them lead. Seen through the resurrection, Psalm 13 points to a hope that does not depend on avoiding the fall, because God's unfailing love reaches past it.
