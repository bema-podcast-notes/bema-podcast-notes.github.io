#### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** 325 — Sanctuary: Waking Up in the Mishkan
**Hosts:** Marty Solomon and Brent Billings, with guest Josh Bossé
**Focus:** Genesis 1:1–5 (Day One of creation) and Exodus 25 (the Ark, the Table of the Presence, and the Menorah)

This episode launches a new series in the "Bossé Anthology" exploring how the construction of the tabernacle (the *Mishkan*, "dwelling place") parallels the creation account of Genesis 1 — and, more importantly, how those parallels become tangible spiritual practices. Josh Bossé reduces Day One of creation to its most basic literary elements (darkness, light, God's hovering presence, God's speech, and the ordered cycle of evening and morning), then reads Exodus 25 through that lens. The Ark corresponds to darkness holding testimonies, the Table of the Presence corresponds to nourishment drawn from God's presence, and the Menorah corresponds to light that shines outward toward others. The episode closes with four reflection questions — testimonies, bread, lamp, and dance — that turn the tabernacle's furniture into a rhythm for incarnating God's presence in everyday life.

#### Key Takeaways

- The tabernacle is not a "diorama" God wanted built for its own sake; its pattern (*tavnit*) mirrors creation so that God's people can learn to participate in the same creative, ordering work.
- Day One of creation contains three moves that become the template for every day that follows: God hovers over darkness, God speaks light into existence, and God orders light and darkness into a cyclical "dance."
- God's first recorded words in Scripture are "Let there be light." Speech is self-disclosure — so light is God's first self-revelation, telling us who God is even though God also created the darkness.
- God was already present in the chaos before speaking. The Spirit hovering over the deep means God's presence precedes order, not the other way around.
- The Ark of the Covenant is the "darkness" of the Mishkan — a closed box whose contents (broken tablets, manna, Aaron's budded staff) are testimonies that God was there even when it could not be seen.
- The Table of the Presence (literally "table of faces") represents the nourishment that comes from experientially knowing (*yada*) God's presence — God's presence as bread.
- The Menorah is a golden almond tree on fire — a deliberate echo of the burning bush at Sinai — and it must "light the space in front of it," i.e., shine outward toward others.
- The Ark's contents (bread, almond staff, testimonies) form a miniature of the whole tabernacle (table of bread, almond-branch menorah, the Ark itself), revealing a fractal, possibly chiastic structure.
- Contributions to the tabernacle were voluntary offerings (*terumah*); the invitation to "make space for God" is a calling, not a rigid obligation.
- These practices only work when woven into a restful rhythm — a dance of receiving testimonies, being nourished, giving light, and returning again.

#### Main Concepts & Theories

##### 1. Day One as the Foundational Building Block

Josh breaks Genesis 1:1–5 into its minimal story elements:

| Element | God's Interaction |
|---|---|
| Darkness (over the deep/waters) | God's Spirit *hovers* (*merachefet*) |
| Light | God *speaks* it into existence and *names* it |
| Light and Darkness together | God *separates* and *orders* them into a cycle (evening/morning) |

Because every subsequent day of creation closes with "there was evening and there was morning," Day One functions as the pattern for all of creation. The tension between light and darkness is not eliminated but ordered into "parameters" within which everything else can exist.

##### 2. Relational Truth, Not Propositional Truth

The episode's stated theme is *truth* — but not truth as the Greek/Hellenistic idea of a correct proposition. It is **relational truth**: knowing where you stand in relation to everything around you. Darkness for a person alone in the wilderness means not knowing if there is a cliff, a predator, or shelter nearby. Light restores that relational knowledge. Vision is what lets God (and us) "see that it is good."

##### 3. Speech as Self-Disclosure

Speech brings what is inside a person out where others can perceive it. Since "Let there be light" (*yehi or*, mirrored by *vayehi or*) is God's first speech — pure, uncomplicated expression, not built on anything prior — light is God's first self-revelation. Josh reads this as God's answer to the problem of evil, not logically but relationally: "Yes, I made the darkness too, and I'm present in it. But if you want to know who I *am* — I bring light."

##### 4. The Dance: Order as Invitation to Partnership

Light and darkness make no difference to God ("darkness is as light to you"). So the ordering of the cycle is *for us*. A dance requires partners; God orders creation so that humans can get their hands around it, ride its rhythm, and participate in wrangling chaos. This is partnership, not a finished fix.

##### 5. *Tavnit* — The Pattern

Exodus 25 uses the Hebrew word *tavnit* ("pattern," rooted in the idea of building) twice: at the beginning ("make it according to the pattern I will show you") and at the very end of the chapter ("see that you make them according to the pattern shown you on the mountain"). Marty notes that later Hellenistic Judaism (and the book of Hebrews) read this through Plato's forms — an earthly copy of a heavenly reality. Josh's emphasis is different: *tavnit* is less about architectural blueprints and more about preserving the essential *relationships* among the functioning parts. Rabbinic tradition (e.g., David Fohrman) even describes the tabernacle as a body or life-form.

##### 6. The Three Furnishings as Day One

**The Ark (Exodus 25:10–22) — Darkness and Hovering Presence**
- God says, "There, above the cover, between the cherubim, I will meet with you and speak" — the presence hovering over the surface, with winged cherubim echoing the fluttering connotation of *merachefet*.
- Beneath that presence is the darkness of a closed box. Inside are the "testimonies" — the more literal Hebrew than "tablets of the covenant."
- The contents are testimonies that God was present: manna (bread that was a Torah-test and God's nourishment), Aaron's staff (dead wood sprouting life), and the tablets. Marty suggests it is the *broken* tablets that reside there — reminding Israel they broke the covenant yet God did not call it off.
- The inside of the box is gold, but in darkness you can't see it — just as one in chaos can't see that creation is good and stewarded.

**The Table of the Presence (Exodus 25:23–30) — Nourishment**
- Hebrew *lechem panim*, "bread of faces." *Panim* (face/surface) runs throughout Genesis 1:2 ("the face of the deep," "the face of the waters"). Marty recalls the rabbinic image of *merachefet* as a "kiss" between faces.
- Once you *know* God's presence is there — not as abstract theology but as *yada*, experiential knowledge built on testimonies — that presence itself becomes bread. Brent connects this to Israel's prior experience of manna and Marty to the John 6 "bread of heaven" teaching.

**The Menorah (Exodus 25:31–40) — Light Facing Outward**
- All about light, made of one hammered piece (a single unified expression, like God's utterance), with organic imagery: almond flowers, buds, blossoms, six branches plus a central shaft (three-plus-three-plus-one, echoing the week).
- A tree with fire on its branches that is not consumed = the burning bush. God's sign to Moses at the bush was, "You will come back and worship at this mountain." Moses receives these instructions while *living out that sign* on Sinai.
- The odd instruction to "light the space in front of it" (literally "toward its face") is paired with the second *tavnit* command. God wants Moses to *see* and *remember* the mountain so that when he descends, his face carries Sinai to others — telling the next runaway or failure, "You too will meet God here."

##### 7. The Fractal Structure

Inside the Ark: bread (manna), an almond branch (Aaron's staff), and testimonies (covenant). In the Holy Place: the table of bread, an almond-branch Menorah, and the Ark. The Ark is a miniature of the whole tent. Marty proposes a chiastic reading — testimonies of the people (Ark) and Moses' testimony (Menorah/burning bush) bracketing the Presence in the middle — reinforcing that intact testimonies are what usher people into God's presence.

#### Examples & Applications

- **The naked person in the wilderness:** Josh's thought experiment for understanding what darkness means — not knowing time, terrain, threats, or shelter. Light lets that person identify a rocky outcropping for shelter or test whether berries are poisonous.
- **Chaotic seasons of life:** In the past few years of collective upheaval, it became hard to distinguish what is good, what is bad, what is habit, and what is intentional. The light of God's presence restores the ability to "see that it is good."
- **Bible Project on Eden/Sinai concentric zones:** Brent references the nested structure (Eden → garden → tree; camp → partway up the mountain → summit) that will matter later in the series.
- **Masada scale model:** Marty's analogy for the Hellenistic "heavenly pattern" reading of *tavnit* — an earthly model of a larger reality.
- **The divine council as offering-bringers:** Marty's speculative connection between the list of raw materials in Exodus 25 (metal, yarn, skins, wood) and the chaos God slowly orders in Genesis 1.
- **Josh's personal testimony:** God renaming Jacob a second time (Genesis 35) after the wrestling match spoke to Josh that God would "come down just to say your name." He now carries that outward by simply naming and being present with people who are struggling.
- **Knowing when to speak and when to be present:** Brent's application of God's rhythm — hovering silently, then speaking — to the pastoral challenge of timing.
- **"Take words with you" (Hosea 14:2):** Marty's favorite passage as the missional summary — carrying testimonies into captivity, suffering, and doubt.

##### The Four Reflection Questions

1. **Testimonies:** What physical objects or practices remind you of the felt reality of God's presence, even in darkness? (Standing stones, *matzevot*; a verse, a ritual, a grave visit, a leaf picked up off the ground.)
2. **Bread:** What tangible actions, spaces, or objects genuinely nourish your spirit?
3. **Lamp:** How are you turning these personal tools outward to incarnate God's presence for others?
4. **Dance:** How can these be woven into a natural, restful, intentional rhythm — a breathing in and out?

- The rabbinic concept of *tohu vavohu* as undifferentiated chaos (light and dark mixed) rather than mere darkness, and what "ordering the darkness" means theologically.
- The role of *water* in Genesis 1:2 and its counterpart in the tabernacle (deliberately set aside as a "rabbit trail").
- *Merachefet* (hovering) and its use in Deuteronomy 32:11 (an eagle over its nest) — the "wings" connection to the cherubim.
- The debate over which tablets (broken, whole, or both) were placed in the Ark, drawing on Talmudic tradition (Bava Batra 14b).
- The tabernacle as a human body in David Fohrman's teaching and broader rabbinic literature.
- Hebrews 8–9 and Platonic "forms" as a Hellenistic interpretation of *tavnit*.
- The Menorah as Tree of Life and the reversal of Genesis 3 (a tree that gives light rather than shade).

#### Summary

This episode reframes the tabernacle instructions from a dry building code into a pattern for living. Day One of creation gives us the template: God is already present in the chaos, God reveals who God is by speaking light, and God orders light and darkness into a dance we are invited to join. Exodus 25 puts that template into furniture. The Ark holds testimonies in the dark so you can know God was there even when you couldn't see the gold around you. The Table of the Presence turns that experiential knowledge into bread — God's presence itself as sustenance. The Menorah, a burning almond tree that is not consumed, takes that nourishment and shines it outward toward the faces of others, so that whoever meets you can hear the same promise Moses heard at the bush: you, too, will come back to this mountain and meet God. The word *tavnit* holds it together — build what you saw on the mountain, and don't lose the relationships between the parts. The application is not a checklist but a rhythm: gather your testimonies, be nourished, give light, and repeat.
