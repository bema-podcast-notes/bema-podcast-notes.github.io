### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** BEMA Episode 312: Parables, Part 1 (What Is a Parable and Why Teach This Way?)  
**Hosts:** Marty Solomon and Brent Billings, with guest teacher Reed  
**Focus:** Matthew 13:10–13, 34–35; Psalm 78; Matthew 18 and 20; Luke 12:13–21; 2 Samuel 12; what parables are and how they work as a way of teaching

This episode opens a new "anthology" on the BEMA podcast. Anthologies are short series led by BEMA teachers other than Marty, and Reed leads this one on the parables of Jesus. The conversation starts with a question about big questions. When we're asked something important, Western church culture expects clear, accurate, step-by-step answers. Jesus, by contrast, chose to put his deepest teaching about God, people, and the kingdom into short, earthy stories. Reed, Marty, and Brent look at what the word "parable" means and why Matthew links Jesus' parables to Psalm 78. They discuss why Jesus would risk being misunderstood, and why a story can change a person when a direct statement can't. Reed ends with practical tips for reading parables and a warning about over-explaining them.

### Key Takeaways

- **Our culture prizes clarity.** Church, university, and Western culture tend to value detail, accuracy, and a straight line of reasoning, especially on important topics. In church settings, leaving something unclear can feel almost like a betrayal of faith.
- **Jesus doesn't seem to share that priority.** In Matthew 13 he explains that he speaks in parables partly so that "seeing they do not see, and hearing they do not hear." He seems to make the meaning hard to grasp on purpose.
- **Parables deal with the biggest subjects.** Matthew 13:35 quotes Psalm 78:2, a psalm that retells Israel's whole story of rescue, rebellion, and restoration. That link signals that the parables are about matters just as large.
- **"Parable" means "something thrown alongside."** A parable sets something unfamiliar, like God or the kingdom, next to something familiar. It works like a metaphor stretched out into a full story with characters and a plot.
- **Jesus' imagery is everyday, not religious.** He pictures God as a farmer, a winemaker, a baker, a cranky judge, and a king. He pictures people as day laborers, party guests, and runaway kids, and the kingdom as yeast, mustard seed, and buried treasure.
- **Jesus worked in images, not arguments.** Kenneth Bailey calls him a "metaphorical theologian" who made meaning "like a dramatist and a poet rather than like a philosopher."
- **Parables create meaning, they don't just carry it.** A parable is less like a conveyor belt delivering facts and more like a house with windows on every side that you are invited to live in.
- **Straightforward speech has its own risks.** Saying "God is omnipotent" can be misunderstood too. Knowing every fact about something is not the same as knowing it, and direct statements rarely change a person deep down.
- **What we most need to hear, we can't hear directly.** This is true of hard truths about ourselves and of the most important truths about God. Both have to come at us "slant."
- **Parables use "left-handed power."** Robert Farrar Capon describes this as power that draws people in, like the smell of baking bread, rather than forcing them, like a hammer on a nail.
- **Explaining parables involves an irony.** Teaching a parable can kill it, like explaining a joke or dissecting a bird. Listeners need to engage the parables themselves, ideally in community.
- **Reed's four tips for reading parables:** Engage, Wonder, Identify, and hope for Discovery.

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. The Problem of Big Questions

Reed opens by asking whether anyone has ever been asked a really big question. Examples include:

- Personal questions: "How do I forgive someone who keeps hurting me?"
- Ministry questions: "Should we let people who believe X lead in our church?"
- Theological questions: "What is grace? What is judgment? What is wrath?"
- Big moments of communication, such as a Christmas sermon or a vision talk to an organization

Marty describes two instincts when facing a question like this:

- **Zoom out to principles.** The question is too complex to answer directly, so you step back and talk about general principles that might guide a decision.
- **Feel pressure for a clear answer.** The church has built a culture where everyone "should have an answer for every question." Vagueness feels "almost sinful."

Reed sums up the cultural expectation: when the topic is important, you must be clear, accurate, thorough, and well informed. Jesus "doesn't seem to care about any of that list." Reed is careful to say that accurate information isn't bad. Jesus just doesn't treat it as the main tool.

#### 2. Matthew 13 and Psalm 78

**Matthew 13:10–13.** When the disciples ask why he speaks in parables, Jesus answers that the "secrets of the kingdom" have been given to them but not to others. The crowds see without seeing and hear without understanding. Read plainly, this suggests the parables are meant to be hard to understand.

**Matthew 13:34–35.** Matthew notes that Jesus "did not say anything to them without using a parable," fulfilling the words: *"I will open my mouth in parables; I will utter things hidden since the creation of the world."*

- **The tension:** A phrase like "hidden since the creation of the world" raises expectations of world-shaking revelation. Reed compares it to *National Treasure*. When something that important is promised, we expect it to be stated plainly. Instead, Jesus tells stories.
- **The hyperlink:** Matthew calls it "the prophet," but the quotation comes from **Psalm 78:2**. This is the BEMA idea of *remez*, where quoting part of a passage calls the whole passage to mind.
- **What Psalm 78 contains:** Israel's entire story. It covers the Exodus, water from the rock, idolatry and rebellion, God's anger, exile, and being shepherded under King David. In short, the full arc of rescue, rebellion, and restoration.
- **Why it matters:** Matthew is signaling that the parables deal with subjects as large and as central as Israel's whole story of salvation. These include rebellion and judgment, grace and mercy, God's care, and the nature of the kingdom.

#### 3. Abstract vs. Concrete: Two Ways to Handle Big Truth

Marty describes two directions you can take a truth that is too big to grasp as it stands:

| Western Approach | Eastern/Hebraic Approach |
|---|---|
| Move the truth into the **abstract** | Move the truth into the **concrete** |
| Treat it philosophically, through definitions, propositions, systematic theology | Put it in a story, image, or lived situation |
| Aims at understanding through explanation | Aims at wisdom through experience |

Marty argues that **wisdom can't be fully reached through abstraction. It has to be experienced.** A parable takes a big idea and pulls it into a concrete form, creating a space where the listener can actually enter into that wisdom.

Reed connects this to an earlier BEMA conversation about **"truth" vs. "fact."** We tend to "factify," stating propositions about God as though stating them means we understand them. Jesus tends to put truth into story instead.

#### 4. What Is a Parable?

- **Origin of the word:** The Greek *parabolē* means "something thrown alongside."
- **How it works:** Something unfamiliar or impossible to grasp directly (God, human nature, the kingdom) is placed beside something familiar.
- **Metaphor, expanded:** "You take a metaphor and you explode it out into a whole story with an arc and characters." Not every parable works this way, but many of the major ones do.
- **The casting:**
  - **God** appears as a farmer, a winemaker, a baker, a cranky judge, and a king.
  - **We** appear as itinerant laborers, party guests, and runaway kids.
  - **The kingdom** appears as yeast, a mustard seed, and buried treasure.
- **Not religious:** Eugene Peterson (*Tell It Slant*) points out that almost nothing in the parables is overtly religious. They are set in daily, routine, earthy life: "the blood, the soil, the sweat."

#### 5. Jesus as a Metaphorical Theologian

Kenneth Bailey's key ideas:

- Jesus was a **metaphorical theologian** who created meaning "like a dramatist and a poet rather than like a philosopher."
- **"Parables do more than explain meaning; they create meaning."**

Reed's illustration: if Jesus had written a systematic theology, it wouldn't be five volumes of dense argument. It would be a single slim volume, "mostly just a collection of short stories," like a small poetry chapbook from an indie publisher. Marty adds: "Be a pamphlet."

**Implication:** This should change what we value. We tend to admire the person with the five-volume systematic theology, but Jesus "just doesn't really seem to work like that."

#### 6. The Parable as a House with Windows

Reed contrasts two models:

- **Conveyor belt (how we usually treat teaching):** Facts like "God is omnipotent" come down the line, and you pick them up and analyze them, usually by chasing definitions.
- **House with windows (how parables work):** You are invited to move in and live there. From inside, you can look out different windows at evangelism, discipleship, the nature of evil, the church, or justice.

**The catch:** Much of the work is left to the listener. It isn't "Here's the claim, agree or disagree." That is why Jesus is "maybe not the guy you'd pay to come to your church conference."

Marty adds a quote via Brian McLaren's *The Great Spiritual Migration*. A rabbi tells the same parable every morning, and every group responds differently. He assumes "God's going to do about 54 different things with that parable," because each person meets it in a different way and at a different time.

#### 7. Questioning the Assumption That Direct Speech Is Best

Reed argues that direct communication is **not risk-free**:

1. **It can still be misunderstood.** Do we really know what we mean by "omnipotent," "eternal," or "perfect"?
2. **Information isn't knowledge.** Having all the facts isn't the same as knowing something. Reed paraphrases Thoreau: people sit around reading books about knots and sails when they should just go out on a boat and start sailing.
3. **It isn't always effective.** Detail and accuracy are exactly right for law, engineering, or financial reports. But for the deeper questions of who we are and who God is, plain statement "is actually really depersonalizing and, most of all, it is powerless."

**Reed's example:** Sermons on the Trinity often feel like "busting open a mechanic's manual for an automobile and diagramming all the parts." Even when they make sense, they rarely change us the way the prodigal son or the good Samaritan can.

**Marty and Brent's example:** Clear facts about marriage help, but "80 to 90 percent of all the goodness" isn't found there. Brent notes that a giant spreadsheet of facts about his wife wouldn't make him a better husband.

**On not understanding:** Reed cites G.K. Chesterton (via Peterson). If a teacher gives three illustrations and students still say "we don't understand," the right reply is: "That is correct, you do not understand." Realizing we don't understand "is not a bad place to start." Reed also jokes about the disciples' confident "Yes" when Jesus asks if they've understood (Matthew 13:51).

#### 8. Why Speak in Parables? Addressing Blind Spots

Many parables are told **in response to a specific person's question**, and that question often exposes a **blind spot**. The person thinks they are asking about one thing, but often they are trying to justify themselves.

| Situation | Hidden Blind Spot | Parable Told |
|---|---|---|
| Peter asks how many times he must forgive his brother (Matthew 18:21) | Peter doesn't see his own lack of mercy | The Unforgiving Servant |
| Peter asks what the disciples will get for leaving everything (Matthew 19:27) | He thinks doing more work means deserving more | The Workers in the Vineyard (Matthew 20) |
| A man asks Jesus to make his brother split the inheritance (Luke 12:13) | Wrong priorities; a broken relationship | The Rich Fool (bigger barns) |

Jesus *could* say directly, "Peter, you're unforgiving." But that would be accusatory and powerless. It wouldn't get past Peter's pride. The story does.

#### 9. Concretizing the Abstract: The Luke 12 Example

Brent asks a good question. Isn't Jesus turning a *concrete* problem (money) into an *abstract* story that everyone can see themselves in? Reed clarifies:

- **Concrete problem:** What to do with the inheritance.
- **Abstract problem underneath:** The broken relationship between the brothers. In Greek, "Who made me a judge or arbitrator over you?" literally calls the arbitrator a **"divider."** Jesus refuses to be the divider between them.
- **What the parable does:** It takes the abstract problem (greed, broken relationship) and makes it **concrete**: barns, grain, storing up. That is how parables generally work.
- **The ripple effect (Brent's insight):** Bystanders who were laughing at the "clowns fighting over money" suddenly see themselves in the story too.

#### 10. Saving Face and Inviting Change: Nathan and David

Marty brings up **2 Samuel 12**. Nathan doesn't open by accusing David about Bathsheba and Uriah. He tells a story about a rich man who takes a poor man's only lamb.

- An opening accusation would make David defensive, leading him to deflect or lie.
- The story moves David off center, lowers his defenses, and lets him judge the situation before he realizes it is about him.

**Marty's metaphor:** In Western pastoral care, we wonder whether to "play the grace card or the rebuke card." Jesus' parables "play a whole hand of cards." They open up options A through G and hand the truth over to the listener.

**Marty's caveat:** Saving face doesn't mean dodging accountability. Abusers still have to go through confession, repentance, reconciliation, repair, and changed behavior. Marty contrasts parables with **cancel culture**. "Lack of accountability ain't it, but cancel culture rejection isn't it either." Accountability systems alone can't change a heart. The parable can provoke that change.

**Reed's two-sided summary:**

1. **The things about ourselves that are hardest to hear, we cannot hear directly.**
2. **The things about God that are most crucial to understand, we cannot understand directly.** We can't take them in deep down that way. "God is merciful" is true. But the father in the prodigal son running out to embrace his son, after the son has "crapped all over him," lands in a way the statement never will.

#### 11. Right-Handed vs. Left-Handed Power

From Robert Farrar Capon (*Kingdom, Grace, Judgment*):

| Right-Handed Power | Left-Handed Power |
|---|---|
| Works like a **hammer on a nail** | Works like an **aroma** (bread baking in the oven) |
| Forces and compels from the outside | Draws people in from within |
| Rewards and consequences ("No dessert if you don't eat") | The thing itself is attractive |
| Gets results and changes behavior | Can change a person deep down |
| Direct statement: "Take it or leave it" | Parable: an invitation to look inside |

#### 12. Telling It Slant (Eugene Peterson)

Peterson's description of how a parable works:

- It **keeps the message at a distance**, which goes against our instincts.
- It **slows down comprehension**.
- It **blocks automatic prejudiced reactions** and **dismantles stereotypes**.
- It **comes at the listener from an angle**, "on the slant."
- It talks about God without throwing the word "God" in the listener's face.
- John Dominic Crossan: "The parable is an earthquake opening up the ground at your feet." Reed adds: *if you choose to stand there.*

**The core principle:** A parable leaves the power with the hearer. It is open to rejection and misunderstanding, and Jesus accepts that. It is the only way real change happens: through engagement, wonder, identification, and finally **discovery**. Marty echoes this: Jesus never stops to clarify after a parable. He "just tells a parable and lets it go," even knowing people will misunderstand and misuse it.

#### 13. The Irony of Teaching Parables

Reed admits it is ironic to explain stories Jesus was content to leave unexplained. He argues it is still necessary, because we are thousands of years, half a world, and a whole culture away from the original listeners. He tells two parables of his own about this:

1. **The comedian:** A comic's whole act is explaining other people's jokes. Every week the audience leaves "in contented silence, understanding why the jokes were funny, but unable to laugh at them."
2. **The swallow:** A man loves the flight of a swallow nesting in his yard. To show his friends, he catches it and dissects it while they take notes on its muscles and skeleton. Then he tosses it into a grave he dug beforehand.

**The lesson:** Don't let teachers' explanations be the end of it. Reed found the discussion groups afterward far more fruitful than the sermons. Groups of around 20 people each brought different angles and explored the metaphor "to its limits," while watching out for interpretations that stretch it too far.

#### 14. Remez and the Work of the Spirit

- **Marty:** People often ask, "What's *the* remez of this parable?" But reading parables "is not an objective science." It is a dynamic process led by the Spirit. Finding "the right remez" isn't the goal. Poor interpretation is still possible, though.
- **Reed:** Spotting a remez or a chiasm (a mirrored literary structure) can turn into a "Where's Waldo" game. If you find the connection and then go on living in the house as an unforgiving person, "I don't care. It doesn't mean anything."
- **Brent:** In PaRDeS (the traditional Jewish method of reading on four levels), the Spirit is often treated as the final step after the mechanical work is done. The Spirit needs to be welcome throughout the whole process.

### Examples & Applications

1. **Answering big questions in ministry.** When someone asks how to forgive a repeat offender, instead of offering a definition of forgiveness, you might sit together with the Unforgiving Servant or the Prodigal Son and ask where they see themselves.

2. **Confronting without shaming.** Like Nathan with David, a mentor can share a story, a case study, or a hypothetical that lets the other person recognize their own blind spot. Accountability still follows. The point is to lower defenses so real repentance can happen.

3. **Parenting with left-handed power.** Rewards and punishments get compliance. Making goodness attractive and modeling it is what forms character, like the smell of bread drawing everyone to the table.

4. **Marriage and relationships.** A spreadsheet of facts about your spouse doesn't make you a good partner. Shared experience does. The same applies to knowing God.

5. **Preaching and teaching.** Instead of diagramming the Trinity like a car manual, a teacher might use a story or image that invites listeners in. Teachers can also resist the urge to tie up every loose end.

6. **Group Bible study.** Reed's experience suggests discussion groups, where many voices bring different angles, unlock parables better than any single sermon.

7. **Modern parables.** Peter Rollins' *The Orthodox Heretic* offers contemporary parables that work in the same "sneaky, subversive, slant" way and can be more accessible to modern readers.

8. **Reed's four tips in practice:**
   - **Engage:** Track the connections (remez) and dig into first-century cultural details.
   - **Wonder (and wander):** Explore the background. What happens to your hands when you knead dough? How wide was the road from Jerusalem to Jericho? How long did the prodigal's walk home take, and what was going through his head?
   - **Identify:** Take turns being each character. In the Great Banquet, how does it sound to the invited guest, the poor of the city, someone lurking on the highway, or the messenger?
   - **Discover:** Hope and pray that God will "worm his way down into us and change something in the soul."

9. **Billy Collins' "Introduction to Poetry."** Students "tie the poem to a chair with rope and torture a confession out of it." Marty: "If that doesn't describe our Bible study, I don't know what does." The application: hold a parable up to the light, feel along its walls for the light switch, and stop beating it to find out "what it really means."

### Summary

This episode lays the groundwork for BEMA's parables anthology by asking why Jesus taught this way at all. Our culture assumes that the more important a subject is, the more clearly and directly we should state it. Jesus did the opposite. Matthew connects the parables to Psalm 78, a retelling of Israel's whole story of salvation, which shows that these stories carry the weightiest subjects there are. Yet Jesus delivered them in the language of farmers, bakers, runaway kids, yeast, and barns.

Reed, Marty, and Brent explain that parables work because the truths we most need are the ones we can't take in directly. Hard truths about ourselves trigger our defenses, and the deepest truths about God reduce to lifeless definitions. A parable comes at us slant. It lets us see ourselves before we realize it is about us, it leaves room to save face, and it invites us in like the smell of bread. It is risky, because it can be rejected and misunderstood, and Jesus accepted that risk because it is the only way real change happens.

For your own study, the challenge is to step into the parables and live in them rather than dissecting them. Engage the text and its background, wander through the details, take on each character's point of view, and leave room for the Spirit to bring discovery. Like a joke, a parable that only gets explained may be understood but never land.
