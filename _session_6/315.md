### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** 315 – The Lost Son (Luke 15)  
**Hosts:** Marty Solomon and Brent Billings, with guest teacher Reed  
**Focus:** Luke 15:1–3, 11–32 (the parable of the lost son, often called the prodigal son), with connections to Exodus 10 and Exodus 16

This episode belongs to the BEMA series on Jesus' parables. It follows the Good Samaritan and the Unforgiving Servant, and comes just before the Rich Fool. Reed leads the discussion of Luke 15, where Jesus answers Pharisees and teachers of the law who are "grumbling" because he welcomes tax collectors and sinners and eats with them. The hosts start by giving the Pharisees a fuller, more human picture. Then they read the story the way a Middle Eastern villager would have heard it. A son's request for his inheritance is a shocking insult to his father. The son's rehearsed speech copies Pharaoh's empty "repentance." The father runs out to protect his son from the village before the son has said a word. The story ends without an ending, and that open ending is aimed straight at the religious listeners. The main point is that God takes the first step. His kindness is what leads people to repentance, and the people who see themselves as righteous may turn out to be the last lost thing the Father goes out to find.

### Key Takeaways

- **The Pharisees were not all the same.** Some may even have followed Jesus and taken his teaching seriously. If we treat them as stock villains, we miss how much modern religious people have in common with them.
- **"Grumbling" points back to the wilderness.** Luke uses the same Greek word found in the Exodus 16 story of Israel grumbling. That suggests the Pharisees are in unfamiliar spiritual territory and want security, and not simply that they are wicked.
- **Asking for the inheritance was the real shock.** In that culture it meant "I wish you were dead." The first audience would have expected the father to beat his son. Instead, he divides his property.
- **The son's speech copies Pharaoh's.** "I have sinned against heaven and against you" echoes Pharaoh's words in Exodus 10:16, and Pharaoh was trying to get out of trouble, not truly repenting.
- **"Make me like one of your hired servants" is a plan to pay the father back.** It is not a plan to restore the relationship. The son wants to "save himself" and wants no grace, in Kenneth Bailey's words.
- **The father acts first.** He sees his son, is moved with compassion deep in his gut, runs (something a dignified man did not do), embraces him and kisses him, all before any confession.
- **Repentance does not have to come before forgiveness.** God's kindness is what leads us to repentance (Romans 2:4). Grace changes people, and people do not change in order to earn grace.
- **The father may be running to save his son from the community,** which could have shamed him or even killed him. We are not saved *from* God. God saves us from those who would condemn us.
- **The older brother is the final lost thing.** The pattern narrows from one sheep in 100, to one coin in 10, to one son in 2, and finally to one in 1. As Tim Keller put it, one son is cut off from the father by rebellion and the other by his own righteousness.
- **The story has no ending.** It never says whether the older brother joins the party. The Pharisees, and readers today, have to finish it themselves.

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. The Setting: Who Is Listening?

- **Luke 15:1–2:** Tax collectors and sinners gather around Jesus. The Pharisees and teachers of the law mutter, "This man welcomes sinners and eats with them."
- **The "travel narrative" question:** Eugene Peterson describes Luke 9–19 as a travel narrative, with Jesus moving through and along the edge of Samaria. Marty doubts that Luke means ten chapters of literal travel, but he finds it interesting that Luke might want readers to *see* it that way.
- **Pharisees who followed Jesus?** If Pharisees are with Jesus in Samaria, Peterson wonders whether some of them were real followers. They would not be among the Twelve, but they would be invested in his teaching.
- **Different kinds of Pharisees:** There were Shammai Pharisees and Hillel Pharisees, and there was Nicodemus. The hosts recommend *The Pharisees*, edited by Joseph Sievers and Amy-Jill Levine, which challenges the usual stereotypes.
- **Why it matters:** Reed argues that American evangelicals are probably most like the Pharisees. Marty adds his own distinction:
  - Some people are **"self-righteously religious"**, which is the obvious, extreme version.
  - Others are **"religiously self-righteous"**. These people sincerely want to follow Jesus, but their religious habits keep getting in the way. Marty says he relates to Nicodemus for this reason.

#### 2. "Grumbling" and the Wilderness

- The Greek word used here (*diagongyzō*) appears only in Luke among the Gospels, and Luke uses it twice. It matches the Greek translation of Exodus 16, where Israel grumbles in the wilderness.
- **What the wilderness grumbling was really about:**
  - Fear and lack of trust in unfamiliar, hostile surroundings
  - A Hebrew sense of rebellion against leadership and teaching (Moses then, Jesus now)
  - A longing to go back to what they knew ("At least in Egypt we knew what we had")
- **How this applies to the Pharisees:** Jesus is moving the ground under their feet. Their sincere wish to stay "set apart" and "on God's team" makes them ask whether eating with sinners can possibly be right. Their blind spot is that in trying to stay holy, they push away the very people they are supposed to be seeking.

#### 3. One Parable in Three Stories

Jesus tells "this parable" (v. 3) in three parts:

| Story | What is lost | Ratio | How it ends |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lost sheep | 1 sheep | 1 of 100 | "Rejoice with me!" (celebration) |
| Lost coin | 1 coin | 1 of 10 | "Rejoice with me!" (celebration) |
| Lost son | younger son | 1 of 2 | "They began to celebrate" |
| Epilogue | older son | 1 of 1 | **No ending, no celebration mentioned** |

- Reed mentions, without developing it, a prophetic passage (Ezekiel 34) behind the lost sheep story about whose job it is to seek the lost.
- The fact that the third story breaks the pattern is the key to the whole parable.

#### 4. "Give Me My Share": A Shocking Request

- **How modern readers hear it:** a young man eager to start his own life, maybe even a sensible financial move. We assume the sin is the wild living and prostitutes that come later.
- **How villagers heard it:** Kenneth Bailey (*Poet and Peasant* and *Through Peasant Eyes*) spent 15 years asking people across the Middle East about this request:
  - "Has anyone ever made a request like this?" "Never."
  - "Could anyone?" "Impossible."
  - "What would happen?" "His father would beat him, of course."
  - "Why?" "This request means he wants his father to die."
- **Marty's addition:** While the father is alive, the property *is* the father. The son is acting as if the father's presence, name and stewardship no longer exist.
- **The word *bios*:** The father divides his "living" (v. 12), meaning his very life and substance.
- **The father's response:** "So he divided his property between them." The son says, in effect, "drop dead," and the father says "okay." To the audience he would look weak and not much of a man.

#### 5. God Portrayed as "Crazy" in the Parables

Reed notes that Jesus regularly casts God in roles that seem foolish or reckless:

- A king who lets a servant build up a debt no one could ever repay (the Unforgiving Servant)
- A vineyard owner who pays one-hour workers a full day's wage
- A shepherd who leaves 99 sheep to find one
- A father who gives in to an insulting son

**Right-handed and left-handed power:** Marty says God holds back "right-handed" (forceful, controlling) power and uses "left-handed" power, meaning patience and letting go. In his words: "I ain't making bread yet." The father lets his son make mistakes at the father's own expense.

#### 6. The Far Country and the Pigs

- A "distant country" means **Gentile territory**. The son has broken with his Jewish community and is wasting his family's wealth among Gentiles.
- **"Hired himself out"** literally means **"glued himself"** to a citizen. Marty connects this to the Hebrew idea of "cleaving," the word used for marriage. The son is binding himself to the very opposite of where he belongs.
- **Feeding pigs:** Bailey explains that giving someone an awful task was the polite way to get rid of them. The son takes even this job, which shows how desperate he is.
- **Nobody gives him anything.** He is completely rejected.
- **The *kezazah* ceremony:** Bailey describes a public ritual in which a village cuts off someone who has lost the family's wealth among Gentiles. If the son comes home, the village may shame, beat, or even kill him.

#### 7. The Rehearsed Speech: Pharaoh's Words

- **Luke 15:18–19:** "Father, I have sinned against heaven and against you… make me like one of your hired servants."
- **Exodus 10:16–17:** After the plague of locusts, Pharaoh says, "I have sinned against the Lord your God and against you. Now forgive my sin once more and pray… to take this deadly plague away from me."
- **The link:** Both men face famine or ruin, and both use words of confession to get relief. Pharaoh is nobody's model of repentance.
- **"Heaven" for "God":** Jews used "heaven" as a reverent substitute for God's name, the same reason Matthew says "kingdom of heaven."
- **Hired servants:** These were contract workers who lived independently in the village and not in the master's house. If the father agreed, the son would earn back the money but stay estranged as a son. He would be a paying equal, not a son reconciled to his father.
- **Why go back at all?** Brent asks why he wouldn't just go somewhere nobody knows him. Marty suggests the son assumes his father is a pushover he can take advantage of again.

#### 8. The Running Father

- **"Filled with compassion"** translates *splanchnizomai*, to be moved deep in the gut, almost doubled over. In the parables and in the Gospels' descriptions of Jesus, this compassion **always leads to urgent action to save**.
- **The father runs.** Men in that culture did not run, and Reed noticed this himself on a trip to Israel. Running was shameful.
- **Why he runs:** He has to reach his son *before the village does*, to protect him from shame and violence.
- **What the father knows:** nothing. The son has not repented, confessed, or given his speech, and his motives may still be selfish. The father has apparently been watching for him.
- **The theology:** God takes the first step. Repentance is not a gate we must pass through to be forgiven. All the son brings is that he has reached the end of himself.

#### 9. The Missing Line

- In verse 21 the son repeats his speech **but leaves out "make me like one of your hired servants."**
- **Two readings:**
  - **Brent and Marty:** The father interrupts ("Quick! Bring the best robe…"). Squeezed in an embrace, the son never gets the rest out.
  - **Reed:** Jesus deliberately leaves the line out. The son has dropped his plan to pay his way back, and the embrace has already changed him.
- **Where they agree:** Either way, it is the father's mercy that changes the son, and the change comes *after* the embrace. "It's the kindness of God that leads us to repentance" (Romans 2:4).

#### 10. The Celebration and the Missing Ending

- Robe, ring, sandals, and the fattened calf mean full restoration as a son. (Reed points to Robert Farrar Capon's writing on the "party" as central to the parable.)
- **The party feeds the village.** A fattened calf feeds a lot of people. The same villagers who might have attacked the son are now eating at his celebration, and perhaps they too have been won over by the father's compassion.
- **Brent's point:** The father goes over the top so everyone will understand that he is serious. This is not politeness on the road followed by a stoning behind the house.
- **"They began to celebrate"** is where the story should end if it matched the sheep and coin. Reed imagines Jesus looking straight at the grumbling Pharisees as he says it, as if the house lights have come up on the audience.

#### 11. The Older Brother: The Last Lost Thing

- The older son is outside the walls, listening to the music, and refuses to go in.
- **The father goes out again** and pleads with him. He is searching for one more lost son.
- **Tim Keller (*The Prodigal God*):** The younger son is cut off from the father by rebellion. The older son is cut off by his own righteousness.
- **"This son of yours" and "this brother of yours":**
  - The older son says "this son of yours," refusing to call him his brother and perhaps mocking the father's "this son of mine."
  - The father answers gently: "this brother of yours." In other words, he belongs to you because you both belong to me.
- **The deeper blind spot:** The Pharisees are not only pushing away the lost. They *are* lost, and they are being sought and offered a way to be found.
- **Brent's travel-narrative link:** In the Good Samaritan, the Pharisees expected to appear and didn't. Here they discover that they were in the story all along.
- **No ending:** No celebration is mentioned. Nobody keeps the older brother out of the party except himself. The Pharisees in the crowd have to write the ending.

### Examples & Applications

1. **Inheritance today and then:** Brent's grandmother happily gave him a set of drawers he admired before she died. Reed's parents have done something similar. These warm modern gifts show why we miss how offensive the son's request would have sounded.
2. **Discomfort in church:** Reed describes worshipping alongside people whose morals, doctrine or politics seem to threaten what we feel called to protect. That unease is the same wilderness grumbling the Pharisees felt.
3. **Letting people make their own mistakes:** Parents, friends and mentors all face the moment when someone they love is heading toward a mistake. The father shows that sometimes love means letting go, even when it costs you.
4. **Evangelism and preaching:** If kindness leads to repentance, then lecturing people and listing their sins before offering welcome is backwards. Reed says the order should be a surprising, undeserved embrace first and conversation later.
5. **Protecting people from the "village":** Churches can become the community the outsider has to be rescued from. Following the running father means standing between the returning outsider and the people ready to condemn them.
6. **Checking your own religion:** Marty's line, "religiously self-righteous," invites sincere believers to notice where their faith habits keep them outside the party, grumbling about who God lets in.
7. **"This brother of yours":** When we describe someone as "that person" or "their kind," the father's reply reminds us that if God has received them, they are our brother or sister.

### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

- **Kenneth Bailey**, *Poet and Peasant* and *Through Peasant Eyes*: Middle Eastern village culture, the *kezazah* ceremony, and honor and shame in Luke 15.
- **Robert Farrar Capon**, *Kingdom, Grace, Judgment*: the parables as stories about grace, especially the celebration in this one.
- **Tim Keller**, *The Prodigal God*: the two lost sons and what "prodigal" really means (recklessly extravagant).
- **Joseph Sievers and Amy-Jill Levine (eds.)**, *The Pharisees*: correcting stereotypes about Second Temple Pharisaism.
- **Eugene Peterson**, *Tell It Slant*: Luke's travel narrative (Luke 9–19) and the Samaritan setting.
- **Ezekiel 34:** God as the shepherd who seeks the lost, the background to the lost sheep.
- **Exodus 10 and 16:** Pharaoh's false confession and Israel's grumbling as keys to reading Luke 15.
- **Romans 2:4:** Paul on God's kindness leading to repentance, and how it fits this parable's picture of grace.
- **The word *splanchnizomai* across the Gospels:** how often compassion leads straight to saving action (compare the Good Samaritan, Luke 10:33).
- **Right-handed and left-handed power:** how God chooses patience over force, from BEMA's introductory discussion.
- **Luke 19:7:** Luke's second use of "grumble," at Zacchaeus' house, and how it compares to Luke 15.

### Comprehension Questions

1. Why does Reed suggest that the Pharisees' "grumbling" in Luke 15:2 connects to Israel in the wilderness (Exodus 16), and what does that suggest about their motives?
2. According to Kenneth Bailey's interviews, how would a Middle Eastern villager understand the younger son's request for his inheritance, and how would they expect the father to respond?
3. Which Old Testament figure does the younger son echo in his rehearsed speech, and why does that link make us question whether he has truly repented?
4. Why is the father's running significant culturally, and what might he be trying to save his son from?
5. How does the pattern of "lost things" (1 of 100, 1 of 10, 1 of 2) point to the older brother as the final lost thing, and why does it matter that the parable has no ending?

### Personalized Summary

The parable we call the prodigal son is really about a father who refuses to stop searching. The younger son treats his father as dead, wastes his inheritance among Gentiles, and comes home with a self-serving speech borrowed from Pharaoh. Even so, the father has been watching for him. He runs out, risks his own dignity, and shields his son from the village with an embrace before any word of repentance. Grace comes first, and change follows. The story doesn't end there, though. The older brother, faithful, hardworking and resentful, stands outside the party, and the father goes out to plead with him too. For those of us who read Scripture, go to church and want to stay on God's team, this is the challenge. We may be the last lost thing the Father is looking for. The door to the party is open, the people we look down on are our brothers and sisters, and Jesus leaves the ending for each of us to finish.
