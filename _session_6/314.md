### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** BEMA 314: The Barn Builder (The Parable of the Rich Fool)  
**Hosts:** Brent Billings with guest co-host Reed Dent (Marty Solomon absent)  
**Focus:** Luke 12:13–21, with supporting passages from Psalm 39, Psalm 49, Ecclesiastes 8, Jeremiah 22, and the Gospel of Thomas (non-canonical)

Brent and Reed look at the parable Reed calls "the barn builder," a title he borrowed from Eugene Peterson's *Tell It Slant*. It is also known as the parable of the rich fool. The parable sits at the center of the season's chiastic series on parables. Jesus tells it after a man in the crowd interrupts him and demands that Jesus make his brother divide their inheritance. Jesus refuses to be a "divider" between the brothers. Instead he warns against greed and tells a story about a man who gets a windfall harvest, stores it all for himself, and dies that same night with no one to leave it to. Reed argues that the parable is less about whether wealth is moral and more about relationships. Possessions can be a bridge that connects us to other people, or a gulf that leaves us isolated. A strict demand for "fairness" can choke out grace.

### Key Takeaways

- **The context drives the parable.** Jesus tells this story because of a real interruption. A man wants Jesus to force his brother to divide an inheritance, and the parable answers the dispute behind his request.
- **Jesus refuses to be a "divider."** The Greek word translated "arbiter" (*meristēs*, "divider") appears only here in the New Testament. Jesus will not deepen a split that already exists between two brothers. His mission is to reconcile people.
- **Inheritance fights are rarely about the stuff.** Then as now, quarrels over a will usually expose problems that already existed in the relationships.
- **"Life" here is *zoē*.** This is the life of the kingdom and the age to come, the same "eternal life" the lawyer asks about in the Good Samaritan story. Jesus says plainly that this life is not found in having lots of possessions.
- **The question is not "Is wealth wrong?"** Reed argues that Jesus isn't setting a moral limit on bank balances or house sizes. He is asking, "Is your stuff actually giving you the life you want?"
- **The rich man is completely alone.** He talks only to himself ("I," "my," "myself") and consults no family, friends, or community. That isolation is the heart of his folly.
- **God's final question is a "who" question.** "Whose will they be?" puts the weight on relationships rather than on obedience, piety, or how the wealth gets managed.
- **Being "rich toward God" means being generous toward people.** Brent and Reed connect this to "whatever you did for the least of these" (Matthew 25) and Jeremiah 22:16, where doing justice for the poor is called "knowing" God.
- **Insisting on fairness can kill grace.** Paul's "Why not rather be wronged?" (1 Corinthians 6:7) challenges a bookkeeping approach to relationships.

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. Literary Setting: Luke 12 and the Crowd

- At the end of Luke 11, the Pharisees and teachers of the law are hostile and trying to trap Jesus.
- Luke 12 opens with a crowd of "many thousands," so large that people are trampling one another. Jesus begins teaching his disciples first.
- The audience keeps shifting through the chapter:
  1. Jesus speaks to his disciples (12:1).
  2. Someone in the crowd interrupts (12:13).
  3. Jesus turns back to the disciples (12:22).
  4. Peter asks, "Are you telling this parable to us, or to everyone?" (12:41), and Jesus doesn't answer directly.
  5. Jesus speaks to the crowd (12:54).
- **Parallel to the Sermon on the Mount:** Matthew 5–7 also begins with Jesus addressing his disciples and ends with the crowds amazed at his teaching. Luke 12 contains material that closely parallels that sermon, including the teaching on worry and storing up treasure that follows this parable.
- **The tone is almost comic.** Matthew's sermon feels like one uninterrupted block of teaching. Here, someone breaks in with a personal grievance. Reed compares it to a Monty Python sketch.

#### 2. The Younger Brother's Demand

- For convenience, the hosts call the petitioner the "younger brother." They assume the older brother controls the estate, since the eldest traditionally received a double portion (Deuteronomy 21:17).
- **How he asks matters.** He doesn't ask a rabbi to help mediate a fair settlement, which was a normal request. He issues a command: "*Tell* my brother to divide the inheritance with me."
- **What he assumes:**
  - The only problem is his greedy brother.
  - Getting what's "his" will fix everything.
  - He is simply the victim.
- **The unknowns.** Brent points out how little we know. The older brother may have reasons of his own, such as having been away when the father died, or needing more to support a large household. Jesus doesn't ask for details. He goes past the specifics to a general principle that, lived out, would have kept the dispute from arising.

#### 3. "Who Made Me a Divider?"

- **"Man" is not harsh.** Brent notes that the address "Man" (*anthrōpe*) is as neutral as "Woman" in John 2. Interpreters work hard to soften "Woman" when Jesus speaks to his mother, but read "Man" as annoyed. It is probably just an ordinary way of addressing someone Jesus doesn't know.
- **The word study.** *Meristēs* ("divider" or "apportioner") is used only once in the New Testament. Its root also shows up in Jesus' saying about a house or kingdom "divided against itself" (Luke 11:17).
- **Reed's reading.** Jesus isn't only refusing to divide property. He is refusing to divide *people*: "Don't make me do something that will further break an already broken relationship."
- **Gospel of Thomas, saying 72.** This non-canonical text has the same exchange, with an added line in which Jesus turns to his disciples and asks, "I am not a divider, am I?" The hosts cite it as an interesting interpretive echo, not as an authority.
- **What forcing the issue would do.** If Jesus ordered the division, the older brother might hand it over and then cut ties. That is the familiar story of families who say, "Mom and Dad were the only thing holding us together."

#### 4. The Warning: Life Does Not Consist in Possessions

- "Watch out! Be on your guard against all kinds of greed" (Luke 12:15).
- **Zoē life:** This is the abundant life of God's kingdom. With the lawyer in the Good Samaritan story, Jesus answered with a question. Here he answers more directly by saying where life is *not* found.
- **The modern defensive reaction.** Wealthy listeners quickly ask, "Is it immoral to have a retirement account, or a big house?" Reed thinks Jesus isn't interested in drawing that line. Even if wealth is perfectly fine, the real question is: "How's that working for you?"
- **The unspoken promise of possessions.** Things carry an implied message: "Get this and you'll be set." Christmas shows the pattern clearly. The anticipation often turns out to be more exciting than owning the thing.
- **Psalm 39:6–7 (Reed's paraphrase of the Alter translation):** People go through life "as mere ghosts," piling up "worthless wealth without knowing who will eventually haul it away. But now, O Lord, upon what am I relying? You are my only hope."
- **Brent's addition:** Wealth such as a retirement fund should have a purpose and a plan, including who will receive it after you die.

#### 5. The Parable Itself: The Barn Builder

| Story element | Observation |
|---|---|
| "The ground... yielded an abundant harvest" | He wins the "agricultural lottery." The windfall isn't his doing; the ground produces it. |
| "He thought to himself" | He is alone, with no family, friends, or community to consult. That would have been culturally unusual. |
| "I... my... myself" | His speech is entirely self-referential. Reed pictures him as frantic and jittery, like someone rehearsing a confession in a mirror. |
| "I will tear down my barns and build bigger ones" | This is not wrong in itself. Storing grain so it doesn't rot is sensible. |
| "Plenty of grain laid up for many years" | False security. Prosperity makes him feel permanent. |
| "Take life easy; eat, drink and be merry" | He quotes Ecclesiastes 8:15 but ignores its context: no one knows the day of their death, and sometimes the wicked receive what the righteous deserve. |
| "You fool! This very night..." | God interrupts. His life will be shorter than he thought, and he will never enjoy the wealth. |
| "Then who will get what you have prepared?" | The story closes on an unanswered question, like the book of Jonah. |

**Psalm 49 (Brent's parallel):** The wise and the foolish both die and leave their wealth to others. They name lands after themselves but do not last, "like the beasts that perish." The irony is that the rich fool doesn't even have followers or heirs. He is a step worse than the person Psalm 49 describes.

#### 6. Why the Final Question Is a "Who" Question

God doesn't ask:

- **How** will the wealth be distributed or preserved?
- **Why** did you store it, or why didn't you obey?

God asks **"Whose will it be?"** That question points to relationships:

- The surplus could have enriched someone's life.
- Augustine's line, as Reed cites it: "The bellies of the poor would have made better storehouses for this man's grain than his barns."
- By keeping everything, the man isolated himself. He had no one to consult while he lived and no one to inherit when he died.
- The question is rhetorical, and the implied answer is "no one."

#### 7. Turning the Question Back on the Younger Brother

- Jesus turns the man's demand ("Give me what's mine") into a question that reverses the focus. Suppose you get everything, the full inheritance. If it costs you your brother, who will get what's yours when you die?
- This echoes "What good is it to gain the whole world, yet lose your soul?"
- **Bridge or gulf:** Whatever you have can be used to build relationships, or it can open a gulf between you and others.

#### 8. Rich Toward God = Generous Toward People

- "This is how it will be with whoever stores up things for themselves but is not rich toward God" (Luke 12:21).
- **Matthew 25:** "Whatever you did for one of the least of these... you did for me."
- **Jeremiah 22:16:** "He defended the cause of the poor and needy... Is that not what it means to know me?" Caring for the poor is equated with knowing God.
- **"It's about people":** This is a recurring BEMA theme from the Sermon on the Mount discussions. Faith is not morality, obedience, or wealth management in a vacuum. It is about people.

#### 9. Fairness vs. Grace

- **Kenneth Bailey's poem** (*Through Peasant Eyes*): Two brothers inherit their father's land. One lives alone and the other has a family. Each privately believes the *other* deserves more. Each sneaks out at night to move the boundary stone so his brother's field gets bigger. They meet in the dark, realize what is happening, and embrace in tears.
- **1 Corinthians 6:7** (Reed recalled it as possibly 2 Corinthians): "Why not rather be wronged? Why not rather be cheated?"
- **The principle:** When everything comes down to the bottom line and balanced books, "fairness can really kill grace and mercy in our relationships."

### Examples & Applications

- **Modern inheritance disputes:** Siblings fighting over the family ring, the bedroom set, or the money after a parent dies. The fight usually brings existing relational wounds to the surface. The parable asks: Is the item worth losing your sibling?
- **The $17.50 text:** Reed's everyday example. A friend still owes you their share of a joint gift. Maybe you don't send the reminder text. "Just let it go and see what happens."
- **Christmas and children:**
  - Brent's four-year-old, Darius, was more interested in his younger brother Torin's gift than in his own. Torin, the one-year-old, cared more about climbing on the box than about the toy.
  - Fights over "Whose is this?" alternate with moments of surprising generosity, like Darius deciding to give Torin two of his dinosaurs.
  - Reed notes that a child can ignore a toy for months and then shout "That's mine!" the moment a sibling picks it up.
  - The parenting tension: keep the magic of Christmas without teaching that life is about getting stuff.
- **Retirement and estate planning:** Saving isn't condemned. The question is what the savings are for and who will receive them. Wealth without a purpose or a relationship attached is the "worthless wealth" of Psalm 39.
- **Marketing and anticipation:** Ads promise that a product will finally satisfy. Most people find that the anticipation beats owning it, which is a small, everyday picture of "life does not consist in possessions."
- **Generosity as relationship-building:** Spending money, time, and resources on others, especially the poor, is what it looks like to be "rich toward God."

### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

1. **The chiastic structure of the parables series:** Why place the barn builder at the center? How does it relate to the episodes on either side, including the Good Samaritan, the Lost Son (next episode), and the Unforgiving Servant (the finale)?
2. **Luke 12 and the Sermon on the Mount:** Compare Luke 12:22–34 (ravens, lilies, treasure in heaven) with Matthew 6:19–34. Why does Luke place this material right after the rich fool?
3. **Inheritance law in Torah and Second Temple Judaism:** The double portion (Deuteronomy 21:15–17), the role of rabbis as arbiters, and how disputes were settled.
4. **Ecclesiastes 8 in context:** Read the full chapter to see how Jesus' quotation of "eat, drink and be merry" subverts the rich man's confidence.
5. **Psalms 39 and 49 as wisdom backdrop:** Wealth, death, and where security is found.
6. **The Gospel of Thomas, saying 72:** What non-canonical parallels can and can't tell us about how early Christians understood Jesus' sayings.
7. **Kenneth Bailey's *Through Peasant Eyes*:** Middle Eastern cultural readings of Luke's parables.
8. **Eugene Peterson's *Tell It Slant*:** Peterson's treatment of Jesus' "in-between" parables in Luke's travel narrative.
9. **Patristic views on wealth:** Augustine, Ambrose, and Basil the Great (whose sermon "I Will Tear Down My Barns" is directly on this parable).
10. **Jeremiah 22:16 and the meaning of "knowing God":** Justice for the poor as relational knowledge rather than intellectual knowledge.
11. **1 Corinthians 6:1–8:** Paul on lawsuits among believers, and the ethic of choosing to be wronged.
12. **Contrasting stories:** The rich young ruler (Luke 18), Zacchaeus (Luke 19), and the rich man and Lazarus (Luke 16) as further studies of wealth and relationship in Luke.

### Comprehension Questions

1. What prompts Jesus to tell the parable of the rich fool, and what does the way the man phrases his request ("Tell my brother...") suggest about his assumptions?
2. The Greek word translated "arbiter" (*meristēs*) means "divider." How does Reed use this word, along with the parallel in the Gospel of Thomas, to argue that Jesus refuses to be a divider of *people* and not only of property?
3. According to the hosts, why is "Is it wrong to be wealthy?" the wrong question to bring to this passage? What question does Jesus seem to be asking instead?
4. Name at least three details in the parable that highlight the rich man's isolation. Why would making his decision alone have stood out in Jesus' culture?
5. Why does Reed see God's closing question, "Whose will they be?", as the linchpin of the story? How does it connect back to the younger brother's inheritance dispute?

### Personal Summary

This episode turns a familiar warning about greed into something more personal and more searching. The barn builder isn't condemned for being rich or for building barns. His folly is that he did both alone. He talks only to himself, plans only for himself, and dies with no one to receive what he stored. Jesus tells the story to a man so focused on getting "what's his" that he is willing to lose his brother over it. God's question, "Whose will it be?", is aimed at him and at us. Whatever we have, whether a lot or a little, can become a bridge to other people or a gulf that separates us from them. Being "rich toward God" means being generous toward people. Insisting on perfect fairness may win us the inheritance and cost us the relationship. Bailey's picture of two brothers secretly moving the boundary stone *against* themselves shows the alternative: grace that gives up its rights so that relationships can be restored.
