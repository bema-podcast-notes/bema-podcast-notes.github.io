### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** 313: The Good Samaritan  
**Hosts:** Brent Billings, Reed Dent, and guest co-host Elle Grover-Fricks (Marty Solomon is not on this episode)  
**Focus:** Luke 10:25–37 (The Parable of the Good Samaritan), with links to Deuteronomy 6:5, Leviticus 19:18, Numbers 19, Genesis 1:2, and 2 Chronicles 28

This is the first time two BEMA co-hosts have recorded without Marty. It is also the first full parable discussion in the series after the introduction. Reed and Elle look at the Good Samaritan as an answer to a Torah expert who asks what he must do to "inherit eternal life" and then asks "who is my neighbor?" Drawing on Kenneth Bailey's cultural insights and on Elle's knowledge of Hebrew and Jewish tradition, they push back on readings that make the priest and Levite simple villains. They explain why a Samaritan hero would have shocked Jesus' audience, and they use the idea of "near enemies" to make that shock land today. The episode centers on two observations: how close each traveler's feet carry him to the wounded man, and what the Greek word *splagchnizomai* means. In the Gospels that gut-level compassion always belongs to Jesus or to a God figure, and it always leads to rescue.

### Key Takeaways

- The Torah expert's question is not neutral. It may test Jesus' authority and orthodoxy, or it may be the normal back-and-forth between a student and a rabbi, much like checking a church's doctrinal statement today.
- "Eternal life" is not mainly about how long life lasts or about going to heaven after death. It means a *quality* of life: the life of the age to come (*Olam Haba*), or experiencing God's kingdom now.
- Jesus answers a question with a question ("How do you read it?"). That question sits behind every parable and invites listeners to read themselves into the story.
- "Who is my neighbor?" may come from a wish to draw boundaries around who we have to love. All of us ask some version of it.
- The wounded man is naked and unconscious, so he has neither of the two quick ways to identify someone: clothing and dialect. All we know is that he is human and in desperate need.
- The priest and Levite should not be written off as heartless monsters. Purity concerns and personal cost were real, but Jewish law also held that saving a human life overrides other commandments.
- The Samaritan is a deliberately shocking choice. Samaritans saw themselves as the true guardians of Torah, which makes them a "near enemy": the group that claims to have your own faith right.
- The key difference among the three travelers is how close they get. The priest crosses over early, the Levite comes to the place, and the Samaritan comes to where the man actually was.
- *Splagchnizomai* (gut-deep compassion) is used in the Gospels only of Jesus or of God figures in parables, and it is always followed by saving action. That suggests the Samaritan portrays God.
- Jesus turns the question around: it is no longer "Who is my neighbor?" but "Who will you be a neighbor to?"

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. The Setting: A Question From a Torah Expert

- **Who he is:** The man is a *nomikos*, an expert in the law. That means Torah, not civil law. Elle traces this scholar class (*soferim*, scribes) back to the Babylonian exile, when interpreting and preserving Torah became essential for resisting assimilation into empire. Since literacy was rare, scribes held high social standing and were well known in their communities.
- **Standing up:** Kenneth Bailey notes that a student customarily stood to show respect to a teacher. Standing puts the lawyer in the student's posture, yet he is the one testing Jesus. That irony hints that he may have a motive beyond simple curiosity.
- **Two ways to read the question:**
  1. **A test of authority.** Luke 10 has just described the 72 coming back with reports of healing and demons submitting. The lawyer may be asking, in effect, "Is this ministry built on sound teaching?" Reed compares it to one evangelical preacher asking another, whose church is growing fast, "What must I do to be saved?"
  2. **Peer dialogue.** Elle points out that asking a rabbi to sum up his teaching was the normal way to engage him. It need not be hostile. Brent compares it to looking up a church's doctrinal statement on its website.

#### 2. What "Eternal Life" and "Inherit" Mean

- **Eternal life** is about a *kind* of life, not mainly a length of time or a place. It is a life that no ruler, career, possession, or advertisement can supply, the life of the age to come when God reigns.
- In Jewish theology, the present world will pass away and *Olam Haba* (the world to come) will remain. The question amounts to: "How can I live in the next world's reality now?" or "How do I experience the kingdom?"
- **Inheritance** is a gift by definition, so "What must I *do* to inherit?" is a bit paradoxical. Elle adds the setting of patronage: God is the patriarch of the household and gives inheritances to members who live out the household's calling. Read that way, the question means "How do I live faithfully as a member of God's household?"

#### 3. Jesus Answers With a Question

- Jesus speaks on the lawyer's own ground: "What is written in the Law? How do you read it?"
- The lawyer answers by combining the **Shema** (Deuteronomy 6:5, love God with heart, soul, strength, and mind) with **Leviticus 19:18** (love your neighbor as yourself).
- Jesus affirms him: "Do this and you will live." The lawyer then "wanted to justify himself" and asks, "And who is my neighbor?"

#### 4. Why Ask "Who Is My Neighbor?"

The hosts suggest two motives. Each one challenges a different kind of listener.

- **Elle's reading: wanting to impress.** The lawyer may be "auditioning" to become a disciple. Jesus has moved on, and the lawyer wants to keep the conversation going with a good question. Elle relates to this from her own past, when she wanted to be noticed by important people (she spent a long time choosing her question before meeting John Piper as a teenager).
- **Reed's reading: drawing boundaries.** Having just said "love your neighbor," the lawyer may realize that Jesus applies the command without limit, even to enemies. His question may be an attempt to fence it in.
- **The word "neighbor":** Hebrew *re'a* sounds very close to *ra* (evil). It comes from a root connected to pasture, so a neighbor is literally the person whose field is next to yours. That built-in narrowness helps explain why first-century Jews debated who counted: fellow Israelites, proselytes, Gentiles?
- **Parables as houses:** As the series introduction put it, a parable is like a house whose windows look out from different sides. Both readings can be valid. The question to ask is which window exposes your own blind spot. As Elle says, "If we get too jacked up on which one's correct, we might miss the point."

#### 5. The Road and the Wounded Man

- **The road from Jerusalem to Jericho** is steep and full of switchbacks. Travelers would have seen the man from far above and kept seeing him after they passed. Ignoring him was a choice made over a long time, not a split-second decision.
- **"Half dead"** (Greek: literally "half" + "dead") means he is alive but will die without help. From a distance, nobody could tell whether he was alive.
- **No identity markers (Bailey):** In a region with many ethnic and religious groups, people quickly placed strangers by **dialect** and **dress**. The unconscious, naked man offers neither. Jesus leaves his identity open on purpose. The only facts that matter are that he is human and in great need.

#### 6. Reconsidering the Priest and the Levite

- **Avoid caricature:** Reed admits his evangelical upbringing taught him to look down on priests, Levites, and Pharisees. He wants to resist that habit. Elle warns that reflexive caricatures of Jewish figures can slide into antisemitism.
- **Possible costs for the priest (from Bailey):**
  - Getting close to a corpse would make him ritually unclean.
  - That could bring public embarrassment.
  - Bailey describes a costly, time-consuming purification process involving a red heifer.
  - The community might not blame him, since some held that you owed no rescue to a "non-neighbor."
- **Elle's corrections, drawn from Jewish sources:**
  - Ritual impurity was a *routine* part of life. Ordinary things, including marital intimacy, caused it. That is why *mikvehs* (ritual baths) were everywhere.
  - Second Temple Jericho was an administrative and supply center for priests and had an unusually large number of *mikvehs*. The priest could have purified himself easily once he arrived.
  - The red heifer ashes (Numbers 19) were probably kept on hand. Individuals did not have to buy and burn a whole cow.
  - **Saving a human life (*pikuach nefesh*) is the highest obligation.** It overrides other commandments. Jesus makes a similar argument about rescuing an animal from a pit on Shabbat.
- **Brent's observation:** The priest was going *down* from Jerusalem, so he was probably off duty. Elle adds that priestly identity still carried purity expectations away from the temple.
- **The upshot:** Jesus' audience would have felt both sides: purity is a real concern, but a human life is at stake. That is the same internal argument we have when deciding whether to stop for someone in need.

#### 7. The Shocking Third Traveler: A Samaritan

- **Expected punchline:** The story is set up like a "three guys walk into a bar" joke. After a priest and a Levite, listeners expected a Jewish layperson or a Pharisee. Instead Jesus brings in a Samaritan.
- **How deep the hostility ran:**
  - **Josephus** reports that around AD 6–9, Samaritans scattered human bones in the temple courts at Passover, a severe desecration.
  - **Mishnah (Shevi'it 8:10):** Rabbi Eliezer used to say, "One who eats the bread of Samaritans is like one who eats the flesh of a pig."
  - **Ben Sira 50:25–26:** "Two nations my soul detests, and the third is not even a people: those who live on Mount Seir, the Philistines, and the foolish people who live in Shechem."
- **The usual explanation:** Samaritans descended from Israelites who intermarried with peoples that Assyria resettled in the land (2 Kings 17). Sermons often treat the story as being about ethnic or racial reconciliation. That reading is valid.
- **Elle's deeper explanation:** "Samaritan" connects to the Hebrew root *shamar*, "to guard." Samaritans saw themselves as guardians of the *original* faith:
  - They accepted only the Torah (their own Samaritan Pentateuch) as Scripture and did not treat the Prophets as authoritative.
  - They rejected the oral traditions and "fences" around Torah that Jewish teachers developed, some of which Jesus also challenged (handwashing, Shabbat rules).
  - So the conflict was something like a fight between denominations. Each side believed *it* had God's word right.
- **Near enemies:** Elle borrows a Buddhist idea. A "near enemy" is harder to love than a distant one. Loving a far-off, theoretical enemy is easier than loving the church down the road with the yard signs you can't stand.
- **Modern equivalents (Reed):** If Jesus told the story today, the third traveler would depend on the audience. For conservatives it might be a woman Episcopal priest or an LGBTQ+ person. For progressives it might be a MAGA-hat-wearing Southern Baptist. It would be someone who claims the name "Christian" and seems to embody everything wrong with the world, and who is the one actually living out Jesus' way. (An atheist would land very differently.)
- **No rescue narrative:** The crowd would have loved a story about a *good Jew helping a pitiful Samaritan*. Elle criticizes our tendency to use needy people as props in a story where *we* are the heroes, or as a chance to "share Jesus." Here the hated outsider is the rescuer, and the needy person is simply someone who needs help.

#### 8. Where Their Feet Take Them: Three Degrees of Approach

Reed calls this, from a passing note in Bailey, the main thing about the parable for him:

| Traveler | Text | Distance | Modern parallel |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Priest** | "When he saw the man, he passed by on the other side" | Crosses over before getting anywhere near | The "30,000-foot" response: "The Bible says it, I believe it, that settles it." A quick scan of proof texts that offers social-media ammunition but no compassion |
| **Levite** | "Came to the place and saw him, passed by on the other side" | Homes in, comes close, then still crosses | The well-researched response: reads every side and forms a thoughtful but *theoretical* opinion without meeting the actual person |
| **Samaritan** | "Came to where the man was" | Comes all the way to him | Gets close enough to the "stink and the mess" to see a real, raw human being rather than a position on an issue |

- Reed applies this to people who are hurting and marginalized: LGBTQ+ individuals, women who faced hard decisions after an unplanned pregnancy, people experiencing homelessness. "Some of them have even been beaten half to death by our own."
- Compassion only becomes possible once we share space with the person.

#### 9. The Spirit Who "Comes Upon"

- Elle links the Samaritan's "coming upon" the man to Genesis 1:2, where the *Ruach Elohim* (Spirit of God) hovers *over* the face of the deep.
- The Spirit did not stay distant from the chaos. It moved over it and entered it. Rabbinic commentary describes God's Spirit as always hovering over darkness, wounds, and chaos, ready to step in.
- Embodying Jesus' character means entering other people's messiness even when it is uncomfortable, "might even make me need to take a bath later," and risks what others think of us.

#### 10. Splagchnizomai: Compassion From the Gut

- The NIV says "took pity." The Greek *splagchnizomai* comes from *splanchna* (bowels or inner organs) and means being moved from deep inside, almost like a stomachache.
- **In Gospel narratives it is used only of Jesus:**
  - Seeing the crowds "harassed and helpless, like sheep without a shepherd" (Matthew 9:36)
  - Seeing a crowd and healing their sick (Matthew 14:14)
  - Seeing the widow of Nain: "Do not weep" (Luke 7:13)
- **In three parables it is used of the God figure:**
  1. The Good Samaritan (Luke 10:33)
  2. The father of the prodigal son (Luke 15:20)
  3. The king in the parable of the unforgiving servant (Matthew 18:27)
- **What follows:** The Samaritan is not just a nice person. He is a portrait of *God*, shown in the very person the audience saw as the enemy.
- **Compassion always acts.** Every time this word appears, the one moved by compassion acts to save. Real gut-level compassion cannot stay passive.
- **The Hebrew parallel (Elle):** *Rachamim* (compassion) comes from *rechem* (womb). It is a feminine, nurturing image of making room for someone else to grow and flourish. It connects to *Ruach*, which is grammatically feminine in Hebrew.

#### 11. The Epilogue: The Question Flipped

- Jesus asks, "Which of these three do you think was a neighbor to the man?" The lawyer can only answer, "The one who had mercy on him." (He may avoid saying the word "Samaritan.")
- "Go and do likewise."
- **Telling truth indirectly is a mercy:** The hardest truths about ourselves can't be heard head-on. The story lets the lawyer reach the answer himself.
- **The reversal:** "Who is my neighbor?" becomes "Who am I being called to be a neighbor to?"

### Examples & Applications

- **Doctrinal statements:** The lawyer's opening question works like browsing a church's "What We Believe" page. You are checking whether a new, popular ministry is sound.
- **Hazard lights:** Reed admits he rarely stops for cars with hazard lights on, even though it would cost him little more than running late, far less than the priest stood to lose.
- **The workplace:** Elle notes that we get "cultural grace" for *not* loving difficult people. Coworkers bond by complaining about a hard colleague, which builds false intimacy instead of honoring people.
- **Theological debates within Christianity:** Arguments between denominations ("You've added things that aren't biblical!") mirror the Jewish–Samaritan dispute. Each side is sure it is the one "doing Christianity correctly."
- **Social media:** Quoting Bible verses to win approval from your own side is the priest's response. It is cheap, distant, and leaves no room for compassion.
- **"I have a right to be angry at that group":** Elle says this phrase, spoken or thought, is shorthand for "And who is my neighbor?"
- **Hard conversations:** Rather than "shooting Bible verses dropping down like paratroopers," Elle suggests a vulnerable approach: "This is really important to me. This message has caused hurt in my life. I'm not sure what I think about this, but I know for sure this…"
- **Healthcare sharing ministries:** Reed's family belongs to a Christian healthcare-sharing ministry called "Samaritan." It shows how fully this parable has turned the word "Samaritan" into a synonym for goodness, the opposite of what Jesus' first audience heard.

### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

1. **The 2 Chronicles 28 remez:** Reed mentions a "brilliant remez" (allusion) without explaining it. Read 2 Chronicles 28:8–15, where men of Samaria clothe, feed, and anoint captives from Judah, put the weak on donkeys, and bring them to *Jericho*. Compare the details with Luke 10.
2. **Kenneth Bailey's work:** Explore *Jesus Through Middle Eastern Eyes* and *Poet and Peasant / Through Peasant Eyes* for more cultural background on the parables.
3. **Ritual purity in the Second Temple period:** Study Numbers 19 (the red heifer), the *mikveh* system, archaeology at Jericho, and how corpse impurity applied to priests off duty.
4. ***Pikuach nefesh*:** Look into the rabbinic principle that saving a life overrides other commandments, and compare it with Jesus' Sabbath healing debates (Luke 13–14).
5. **Samaritan history and theology:** Study 2 Kings 17, the Samaritan Pentateuch, the temple on Mount Gerizim, and the meaning of *shamar* (guard) in Samaritan self-understanding. Revisit John 4 (the woman at the well).
6. **Where Luke places this story:** Luke 9:51–19:28 (the "travel narrative") and how this parable relates to the sending of the 72, the Samaritan village that rejects the disciples (Luke 9:51–56), and Mary and Martha in Bethany.
7. ***Splagchnizomai* and *rachamim*:** Trace every use of these words in Scripture, including God's "womb-like" compassion in the Hebrew Bible (e.g., Isaiah 49:15, Hosea 11:8).
8. **Genesis 1:2 and the Spirit:** Explore *merachefet* (hovering) and the Spirit as a picture of God entering chaos.
9. **Near enemies:** Think about which "Samaritans" in your own tradition, politics, or family are hardest for you to see as neighbors or as people who carry God's image.

### Comprehension Questions

1. In the context of Luke 10:25, what does "eternal life" most likely mean to the Torah expert, and how does the Jewish idea of *Olam Haba* shape that meaning?
2. Why is the wounded man described as naked and half dead, and how does his lack of identifying markers (clothing and dialect) shape the question "Who is my neighbor?"
3. What reasons might the priest and Levite have had for passing by, and what corrections did Elle offer about ritual impurity, *mikvehs* in Jericho, and the priority of saving a life?
4. Beyond intermarriage, what made Samaritans "near enemies" to Jews in Jesus' day, and how might a modern retelling capture the same shock?
5. How does the Greek word *splagchnizomai* suggest that the Samaritan represents God, and what does its consistent pattern in the Gospels teach about real compassion?

### Personal Summary

This episode shows how the Good Samaritan, a story often reduced to "be nice to people," cuts deeper. A Torah expert asks how to live the life of God's kingdom and then quietly tries to limit whom he must love. Jesus answers with a story that strips the victim of every identity marker except his humanity, makes the hated "near enemy" the hero, and measures faithfulness by how close each traveler is willing to come. The priest crosses early and the Levite comes to the place. Only the Samaritan comes to where the man is. Only he is moved by *splagchnizomai*, the gut-deep compassion that the Gospels reserve for Jesus and for God. The challenge is twofold. First, see God at work in the people you least expect, even those who think you are the one getting it wrong. Second, stop asking "Who is my neighbor?", whether out loud or under your breath, and come all the way to the hurting people in front of you, letting the Spirit who "comes upon" the chaos move you toward them.
