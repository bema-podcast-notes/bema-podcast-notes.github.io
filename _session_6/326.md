#### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** 326 — Sanctuary: Entering the Mishkan
**Hosts:** Marty Solomon, Brent Billings, with guest Josh Bossé
**Focus:** Genesis 1:6–8 (Day Two of creation) and Exodus 26 (the curtains, coverings, frames, and veil of the tabernacle)

This episode continues the "Sanctuary" series, which reads the construction of the tabernacle (mishkan) in Exodus alongside the seven days of creation in Genesis 1. Having paired Day One with the furnishings inside the tent (ark, table, lampstand) in the previous episode, Josh now pairs Day Two — the separation of the waters by the vault/expanse — with the layered curtains, coverings, and veils of Exodus 26. The central argument is that God's separations are not walls but doors: boundaries that exist to be crossed in love. Just as rain passes through the heavens to nourish the earth and cycle back, God's people are invited to move through the boundaries God builds, carrying light and nourishment to others. The conversation ends with a sharp critique of hurried, binary evangelism and a call to build boundaries "for the sake of other people."

#### Key Takeaways

- Day Two is the only one of the six main days of creation not called "good." The rabbis suggest this is because God separates water from water — something from itself — an image of isolation and loneliness.
- The expanse (raqia, "hammered out thin") does not truly keep the waters apart; rain still falls. It functions less like a wall and more like a border or a door — a boundary designed to be passed through.
- God names the expanse *shamayim* (heavens/sky), the same word used for the place where God dwells. God is located in the middle of the hierarchy — between the waters above and the waters below — not at the top.
- The verb *asah* ("made") signals a process, not an instantaneous act. God is continually working on this door.
- Exodus 26 is built on repeated pairs: two sets of curtains joined by clasps, layer upon layer of covering, and two veils. The first set is fastened with gold clasps "so that the tabernacle will become one" (*echad*).
- The gold clasps echo the gold of the ark, table, and lampstand from Day One — what binds the divided halves together is the presence, nourishment, and light of God.
- Cherubim are woven into the curtains and veils because cherubim bear God's presence; looking up anywhere inside the tent tells you, "God is here."
- The word for "plan" in Exodus 26:30 is *mishpat* (justice/uprightness), not the expected *tavnit* (pattern). Uprightness is what holds the whole structure up.
- The tabernacle's separations create a cycle, not a static division: sin goes in daily and is carried out on Yom Kippur; priests go in and must come back out. Rain that stays in the sky is not doing its job.
- The two rooms of the tent represent two realities: the inner room is mystery, darkness, and transcendence; the outer room is ministry, hospitality, and generosity (lamp and table).
- It is not loving to drag people straight into the Holy of Holies. Give people bread and light first; *kavod* (glory) also means "heavy," and the inner room is where we bring burdens too heavy for daily life.
- The tabernacle is not a system of exclusion but a teaching tool with a space for whatever a person is ready for — an alternative to the Western binary of "in or out, lost or saved."
- Efficiency-driven evangelism (the 30-second testimony) is the opposite of God's process; there are no shortcuts through the door God is building.

#### Main Concepts & Theories

##### 1. Day Two of Creation: Separating Water from Water

**The text (Genesis 1:6–8):** God says, "Let there be a vault between the waters to separate water from water." God *makes* the vault, separates the waters below from the waters above, and calls the vault "sky" (*shamayim*). There is evening and morning — but no declaration that it was good.

**Ramban's warning:** Josh notes that the medieval commentator Ramban (Nachmanides) said that those who understand Day Two are not permitted to teach it, and those who do not understand it should not ask about it. Josh treats this as an invitation to curiosity rather than a prohibition, and calls Day Two his most treasured teaching in Torah.

**What makes Day Two strange:**

- It is the only one of the first six days not called "good."
- Unlike the other days of separation (light/dark, sea/dry land), the two things separated here are the *same substance* — water and water.
- Nothing dramatically new is created; God is "just messing around with water."
- The vault (*raqia*) refers to something hammered out very thin — craftsmanship language that connects naturally to the mishkan.

**"Said" versus "made":** On Day One, God spoke and it was. On Day Two, God speaks and then the text says God *made* (*asah*) the vault. *Asah* carries the sense of work that takes time — a process rather than an instantaneous act. This will recur throughout the tabernacle narrative. The implication: God is still working on this boundary.

**Did the expanse do its job?** Read closely, the expanse is a poor wall — water keeps getting through as rain. That is the point. It separates the way a border between countries separates: a line you can cross, and in fact one you are meant to cross.

##### 2. Water as People: Why Day Two Is Not "Good"

Water in the ancient imagination often symbolizes people groups. The rabbis observe that God does not call this day good because God is separating something from itself — and water "likes to be together" (as anyone observing surface tension can see).

Read as an analogy for human life, Day Two says: **you begin isolated.** Just as Day One addressed the chaos and darkness we are born into, Day Two addresses the loneliness we are born into. It is genuinely hard to connect, to communicate what is in our heads and hearts. We start out separated.

**Two layers of separation:**

1. **The individualistic/Hellenistic reading:** We cannot perfectly communicate our thoughts; we have skulls around our brains and no telepathy.
2. **The ancient reading of haves and have-nots:** The waters above are fresh, pure, and life-giving (rain). The waters below are mostly chaotic, dangerous, and undrinkable (the abyss, the sea). Egypt with its Nile has all the fresh water it needs; others are stuck below waiting for rain. The separation becomes an image of the gap between those who have and those who lack.

##### 3. The Door: A Boundary Meant to Be Crossed

Josh asks: what kind of separation between two spaces is not only passable but *intended* to be passed through? A door.

**Key features of the door image:**

- The door both separates and grants access.
- God builds the door but does not cross it for us. "We have to be the ones to fall like rain."
- Crossing the door means taking the first step in relationships, loving before being loved, reaching out to those in greater need rather than using access only for ourselves or our tribe.
- Because God named the expanse *shamayim*, passing through the door means passing through heaven. "If you want to get to heaven, you can only pass through it when you're in the act of reaching across and trying to love someone else. Love is what draws us through that door."

**God in the middle, not at the top:** The division is vertical (up/down), which suggests hierarchy. In a hierarchy we would expect God at the top — but God is located in the heavens, in the *middle*, at the nexus between those above and those below. God sits at the meeting place of separated peoples, continually building and repairing the door. (This theme returns in Day Five.)

##### 4. Exodus 26: Layers, Pairs, and Clasps

The chapter is highly repetitive, so the hosts read a "Frankenstein" abridgment. The structure:

- **Ten curtains** of finely twisted linen in blue, purple, and scarlet, with cherubim woven in by skilled workers — joined in two sets of five, fastened by **fifty gold clasps** "so that the tabernacle is a unit." The literal Hebrew: "so that the tabernacle will become *one*" (*echad*).
- **Eleven curtains of goat hair** over the tent, joined by **bronze clasps**, with a sixth panel overhanging the back.
- **A covering of ram skins dyed red**, and over that **a covering of durable leather** (the notoriously untranslatable word).
- **Upright frames** of acacia wood overlaid with gold, held by gold rings and gold-covered crossbars.
- **The veil** (blue, purple, scarlet, cherubim) hung on four gold-covered posts on silver bases, separating the Holy Place from the Most Holy Place, with the ark behind it.
- **The entrance curtain** (same colors, work of an embroiderer) on five gold-covered posts on bronze bases.

**Interpretive observations:**

- A covering does what the vault does: it separates what is above from what is below. The tabernacle is coverings upon coverings — "all these things separating."
- The layers are practical (a decorative layer, an insulation layer, weatherproofing) and symbolic at once.
- **The gold clasps** are what make two halves into one. Gold elsewhere is the ark, the table, and the lampstand — the Day One tools. What binds separated people together is God's presence held sacredly, God's presence made nourishing, and God's light given to others.
- **Cherubim** are not chubby babies; throughout Scripture they bear God's presence (related in rabbinic thought to God's chariot or steed — *keruv* and *rekhev* are near-anagrams). Woven into every curtain, they announce: God's presence is here.
- **Visual effect:** Inside the tent, everything is gold — gold walls, gold lampstand, gold table, and further in, the gold ark.

##### 5. *Mishpat*, Not *Tavnit*: Uprightness Holds It Up

Exodus 26:30 says to set up the tabernacle "according to the plan shown you on the mountain." The expected word would be *tavnit* (pattern), as elsewhere. Instead the text uses **mishpat** — justice, right judgment. Josh's brief reading: the passage concerns upright frames, and the word choice links *uprightness* to the structural integrity of the whole. Mishpat is a necessary element for the system to function.

**Cosmological mirror:** Ancient Near Eastern people pictured a literal vault held up by pillars of heaven, with pillars of earth and Sheol below. The tabernacle's frames and courtyard walls are a miniature of the cosmos. Marty recommends Othmar Keel's book on symbolism in the Psalms and the diagram in Peter Enns's *Inspiration and Incarnation* for visualizing this.

##### 6. The Cycle: Rain, Yom Kippur, and Priests Who Must Come Out

Day Two's separation creates the water cycle: water evaporates up, is cleansed, and falls back down — a process that produces fresh water and a whole, unified system. The separation does not keep the waters apart; it makes the dance possible.

The tabernacle has the same cycle:

- **Yom Kippur:** Every day sin goes into the tent; on Yom Kippur it is reversed and carried out. Atonement is itself a covering that separates us from sin — "a covering that brings unification and cleansing."
- **The priests' movements:** They go from outside to inside, sprinkle blood toward the veil, and come back out. They must eat their meals in a holy place. Priests can be seen as the rain.
- **Rain that stays in the sky is failing:** If the priests just hang out inside the tent, the system breaks. Josh connects this to Jesus's critique of what the temple had become (and to Marty's teaching on the dove).
- **Fractal readings:** The rain can be God, or us, or the priests — the pattern repeats at every level of division.

Marty ties this to Torah's *chukkah* (statute) imagery: "The rain's desire for the earth and God's desire for us is the same blasted thing."

##### 7. Two Rooms: Mystery and Ministry

Zooming out to the tent as a single unit divided in the middle:

- **The inner room (Most Holy Place):** the ark, darkness, mystery, transcendence — the place for the heaviest burdens. *Kavod* (glory) also means "heavy."
- **The outer room (Holy Place):** the lampstand and table — light and bread, ministry, hospitality, generosity, the work of giving what we have received to others.

**Why separate them?** Josh critiques the testimony culture he grew up in, which tried to bring people straight into the Most Holy Place: "God is everywhere, can't you feel it?" But people who lack your story and your testimonies cannot receive that. Worse, telling someone in misery that "God is in the whole thing" risks telling them God *is* the darkness — the opposite of Day One's "associate me with light."

**Proper order:** Milk before meat. People need bread and light before they can perceive the mystery. They have not come out of Egypt yet; they need tools to build their own story. Throwing someone into the Holy of Holies is overwhelming, not loving. You should be able to see you are in God's house from the outer room — the cherubim and gold walls tell you that before you ever reach the veil.

**Discipleship is a process:** Finite humans cannot experience all of God instantaneously. The boundaries are loving because they give us room to grow.

##### 8. Spaces for Whatever You Are Ready For

Marty: We tend to see the tabernacle as exclusion — "keeping me out." Instead it is a system designed to facilitate and teach. There is a space for the person ready only for the outer courtyard, a space for the next step, and so on. Western Christianity loves binaries (in/out, lost/saved), but the tabernacle offers graded spaces so everyone can experience "a little bit of the nourishing rain."

**Jesus's movement as illustration:** Peter (like the high priest, alone in the holiest place), James and John (like the other two Levite clans with distinct tabernacle tasks), and then everyone else — many followers who died for Jesus were not among the Twelve. "You can't have a Jesus movement of all Peters." It depicts partnership: priests help people navigate to the right space to meet God.

**A door that looks like a wall:** Something that appears to keep you out is actually there "so that I can go in if I want to."

##### 9. No Shortcuts: A Critique of Efficient Evangelism

Josh addresses Western and especially American listeners directly (without "finger-wagging"): our Hellenistic binary plus capitalist logic pushes us to move people across the line from unsaved to saved as quickly and cheaply as possible — the two-minute and thirty-second testimony for elevator encounters.

- God's process shows no concern for speed; it is concerned with genuineness.
- Hurried evangelism risks presenting a dimmed, adulterated light — "if the light within you is darkness, how great is that darkness." The world often sees Christians this way.
- "You go through the door that God is building and it's not the one that's the most convenient."
- Rain does not fall "just enough" — all of it falls. Our hospitality should look like that.

**Boundaries for the sake of others:** What we keep private, which struggles we choose not to poke at yet, where we give openly and where we hold back — the governing question is: *How do you draw those boundaries to ease other people's burdens?*

##### 10. Practical Questions and the Call to Flow

Josh's closing challenges:

- Picture specific faces. What would it look like to make God's presence as nourishing as freshly baked bread — to hide the kingdom in it like a little yeast?
- How can the light God has given you show people, first and foremost, that they are in the house of God — that the house is divided but still *echad*, one?
- The system is not static. Flow between the spaces like water: when do you need to withdraw, sit in darkness, and let God's presence be heavy on you? When do you need to get out of your head and simply share bread with people?
- How do you help God build the door?

##### 11. Closing Postscripts

**Marty's three PSs:**

1. **On allegorizing:** Some will love it; some will object that this is just a physical building and a physical cosmos. But Jewish tradition is full of this reading, and the text's oddities (like *mishpat* replacing *tavnit* in an otherwise identical phrase) demand that "more is going on here."
2. **Creation as Israel's story:** Some Jewish thought reads the creation account as allegory for the creation of God's people. Day Two then becomes God's lesson to Israel: "I have placed you in the firmament between the waters above and below because I have asked you to be priests, and I need you to facilitate this door."
3. **The rich man and Lazarus:** The parable's "great chasm" is not so great — they talk across it. The rich man refuses to engage the door and wants Lazarus sent through it for him, to bring him water. And Lazarus had spent his life lying at the rich man's gate — as close to the door as he could get — and was stepped over.

**Josh's addition:** The New Testament says our bodies are the temple; at Pentecost believers became "mini-Sinais"; Jesus is the firstborn of a new creation. If this is how God's presence works in physical space, creation and tabernacle should be central texts for Christian living.

**On the presentation image:** The diorama includes the golden altar (to be covered with Day Six) and may swap some details — possibly intentionally, since Torah forbids exact recreation of certain sacred elements (e.g., the incense and anointing oil; rabbis advise adding an extra ingredient to avoid accidental replication). The rabbis also debate fine points, such as whether the ark and its cover are one object or two (hinging on whether Moses anointed them together).

#### Examples & Applications

- **Rain as the model for love:** Rain does not stay in the sky, and it does not fall "just enough." Applied to ministry, this means giving openly and generously rather than calculating minimum effort for a conversion.
- **Borders versus walls:** A state or national border is often just a line on the ground — a real separation you can nonetheless cross. The firmament works this way, not like a compartmentalized dinner plate.
- **Egypt and the Nile:** The ancient "haves" who never needed rain versus those below waiting for it — a lens for thinking about economic and relational inequality today.
- **The testimony script:** Josh was trained to keep five-minute, two-minute, and thirty-second versions of his testimony. The tabernacle suggests that pace-driven witness can present darkness as light.
- **Bringing bread to a neighbor:** A concrete picture of the outer room — hospitality that makes God's presence "as tasty as freshly baked bread" before any conversation about mystery.
- **Peter, James, John, and everyone else:** Not everyone is a high priest, and that is by design. Different people occupy different spaces and roles in the body.
- **Yom Kippur and the water cycle:** Sin accumulates inside the tent and is carried out annually — evaporation, cleansing, and return.
- **Two-by-fours:** Modern lumber is no longer literally two by four inches; future readers might be as puzzled by our specifications as we are by Exodus 26's. Clarity is context-dependent.
- **The rich man and Lazarus:** A warning about refusing to cross the door oneself and expecting the person in need to do the crossing.

#### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

- Ramban's commentary on Genesis 1:6–8 and the tradition of reticence around Day Two.
- The Hebrew verbs of creation: *bara* (create), *asah* (make), and *amar* (say) — where each is used and why.
- *Raqia* and Ancient Near Eastern cosmology: Othmar Keel, *The Symbolism of the Biblical World*; Peter Enns, *Inspiration and Incarnation*.
- The distinction between *tavnit* and *mishpat* in Exodus 25–27 and what other commentators make of the switch in 26:30.
- Cherubim across Scripture (Genesis 3, Ezekiel 1 and 10, Psalm 18) and the rabbinic link between *keruv* and *rekhev*.
- The gold clasps versus bronze clasps — what the shift in metal signals in the tabernacle's material hierarchy (gold, silver, bronze).
- Yom Kippur (Leviticus 16) as a cycle of inward and outward movement; the meaning of *kippur* as "covering."
- The three Levitical clans (Kohathites, Gershonites, Merarites) and their distinct duties (Numbers 3–4), in relation to Peter, James, and John.
- The parable of the rich man and Lazarus (Luke 16:19–31) read through door and threshold imagery.
- Jewish readings of Genesis 1 as an allegory for the formation of Israel.
- The Talmudic debate over whether the ark and its cover are one object or two, and Torah prohibitions on recreating the incense and anointing oil (Exodus 30).
- The "milk before meat" developmental image (1 Corinthians 3, Hebrews 5) as a framework for discipleship pacing.
- The untranslatable covering material in Exodus 26:14 (*tachash*) and its history of interpretation.

#### Personal Summary

This episode reframes one of the strangest days in Genesis — God separating water from water and declining to call it good — as a teaching about loneliness and love. The firmament is not a wall keeping the waters apart; rain proves that. It is a door God is still building, placed in the heavens, which means God sits in the middle of the divide rather than above it. The tabernacle in Exodus 26 embodies the same lesson: layer after layer of separation, yet bound together by gold clasps "so that it will become one," with cherubim woven everywhere to announce God's presence in every space. The separations create a cycle rather than a static hierarchy — sin goes in and is carried out, priests enter and must return, rain rises to be cleansed and falls to nourish. The pastoral edge comes when Josh turns the two rooms into a critique of hurried, binary evangelism: the inner room is for mystery and heavy burdens, but what people need first is bread and light — hospitality that tells them, before anything else, that they are already in God's house. There are no shortcuts through the door God is building. Marty's closing image of Lazarus lying at the rich man's gate — as close to the door as he could get, and stepped over — lingers as the challenge: the door is there, and we are the ones who have to walk through it.
