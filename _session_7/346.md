### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** 346: Session Seven Opening Reflection 6 of 7, The Sixth Day: Wonder, Image, and Likeness  
**Hosts:** Marty Solomon and Brent Billings (the reflection follows Josh Bosse's *Mishkan* series, Episode 330)  
**Focus:** Genesis 1:24–31 (the sixth day of creation); Exodus 30:1–38 (the golden altar of incense, the census ransom, the bronze basin, the anointing oil, and the sacred incense)

This is the sixth of seven guided reflections that open Session Seven of the BEMA Discipleship Podcast. It is a contemplative episode, not a teaching episode. Brent reads the account of the sixth day of creation aloud five times. Four of those readings are followed by one of the furnishings or instructions from Exodus 30, and after each pairing Marty gives a short prompt and leaves a long stretch of silence. The episode follows Episode 330, where Josh Bosse linked the sixth day, the creation of humanity in God's image and likeness, to the themes of *wonder* and *incarnation*, and to the next items in the Mishkan (Tabernacle). The main point is that God's redemptive, saving work is something to marvel at. It is not something people own, control, or copy for their own use.

### Key Takeaways

- **Some offerings cost nothing.** The golden altar received only incense, with no burnt, grain, or drink offerings. This invites listeners to think about offerings that simply express what God has done or is doing.
- **Atonement deserves wonder.** The census ransom of a half-shekel shows that every life counted belongs to God. Marty asks whether "bad theology" has dulled our sense of how much atonement means.
- **Rich and poor gave the same.** No one could give more or less than a half-shekel. Everyone stands on equal footing before God's redemptive work.
- **Cleansing points to new beginnings.** The bronze basin for priestly washing calls listeners to accept that they have been reborn, which Marty admits is hard to accept.
- **Holiness spreads outward.** Whatever touched the anointed furnishings became holy. Listeners are asked whether God's holiness "spills" into every space and every person around them.
- **Holiness belongs to God.** The sacred oil and incense could not be copied for personal use. In the same way, God's redemptive work is not ours to claim or control.
- **The pairing of passages carries meaning.** Placing the sixth day (humanity made in God's image) next to the Tabernacle furnishings suggests that image-bearers are meant to be places where God's presence and holiness live.

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. The Structure of Session Seven's Opening Reflections

- Session Seven opens with **seven reflections**, one for each day of creation.
- Each reflection follows an episode of Josh Bosse's *Mishkan* series, which closed Session Six.
- Each day of creation is matched with items from the Tabernacle, following the old Jewish idea that the Tabernacle is a small-scale picture of creation. Just as God "built" a cosmos to dwell in, Israel builds a sanctuary where God can dwell among them.
- The format repeats: a creation reading, a Tabernacle reading, a reflection prompt, then silence.

#### 2. The Sixth Day: Image and Likeness (Genesis 1:24–31)

- God creates the land animals "according to their kinds," then humanity "in our image, in our likeness."
- **Image (*tselem*) and likeness (*demut*):** In the ancient Near East, an "image" was often a statue that stood for a god or king and showed their presence and rule. Humanity is God's living image, placed in creation to represent God's character and care.
- Humanity receives a calling to be fruitful, fill the earth, and rule over creation, meaning to look after it the way God would.
- Only after humanity is created does God call everything "**very good**."
- **Link to incarnation:** If people are made to carry God's image, the idea of God taking on flesh fits the original design. God's presence living inside a human being is a source of wonder.

#### 3. The Golden Altar of Incense (Exodus 30:1–10)

- The altar was small (1 × 1 × 2 cubits), made of acacia wood covered in gold, with horns and carrying poles.
- It stood in front of the curtain that hid the Ark, the place where God says, "I will meet with you."
- Aaron burned incense on it every morning and every evening, so incense burned "regularly" before the Lord.
- **Restriction:** It could hold no other incense and no burnt, grain, or drink offerings.
- Once a year, atonement was made on its horns with blood. This is a reference to the Day of Atonement.
- **Reflection theme:** Incense was rising fragrance, not a payment or exchange. It pictures offerings of gratitude, prayer, and recognition (compare Psalm 141:2 and Revelation 5:8).

#### 4. The Census Ransom (Exodus 30:11–16)

- Every Israelite aged twenty or older who was counted gave a **half-shekel** as a "ransom for his life."
- The payment protected the people from plague during the census. Counting people was a sensitive act, because it could suggest that the community belonged to its leaders rather than to God (compare David's census in 2 Samuel 24).
- **Equal contribution:** "The rich are not to give more… and the poor are not to give less."
- The money paid for the service of the tent of meeting and served as "a memorial… making atonement for your lives."
- **Reflection theme:** Atonement is a serious and wonderful thing. Marty asks whether we have become "desensitized by bad theology," whether familiar or transactional ways of explaining salvation have drained the awe out of it.

#### 5. The Bronze Basin (Exodus 30:17–21)

- The basin stood between the tent of meeting and the altar.
- Priests washed their hands and feet before entering or serving "so that they will not die."
- It was a "lasting ordinance" for every generation.
- **Reflection theme:** Cleansing and new beginnings. Marty asks, "Have you accepted the reality of being reborn?" He notes that rebirth is hard to accept, and then repeats the invitation to "take a moment to sit in wonder."

#### 6. The Anointing Oil and Sacred Incense (Exodus 30:22–38)

- **Anointing oil:** Myrrh, cinnamon, calamus, and cassia blended with olive oil, "the work of a perfumer."
  - It was used to anoint the tent, the Ark, every furnishing, and the priests.
  - "Whatever touches them will be holy," so holiness spreads by contact.
  - Copying the formula or using it on ordinary people was forbidden.
- **Sacred incense:** Gum resin, onycha, galbanum, and frankincense in equal parts, salted, pure, and holy.
  - It was placed "in front of the ark… where I will meet with you."
  - Anyone who made it "to enjoy its fragrance" was to be cut off from the people.
- **Reflection theme:** Does wonder at God's holiness help us notice that holiness spilling into every space and every person?

#### 7. The Concluding Principle: Holiness Belongs to God

- Marty ends with this: "Holiness is something that belongs to God. Just as the incense didn't even belong to Moses, so God's sacred redemptive work is not something that we can claim or control."
- His closing blessing asks that God's love, mercy, and work in the world would "inspire regular wonder."

### Examples & Applications

- **Offerings that cost nothing:**
  - Telling a friend specifically how God has been at work in your life.
  - Keeping a gratitude journal as a daily "morning and evening incense."
  - Pausing before meals or at the start and end of the workday to acknowledge God's goodness.
- **Regaining awe for atonement:**
  - Reading the redemption accounts in Scripture slowly, as if for the first time.
  - Noticing when talk of salvation has become a formula or cliché, and putting it in fresh, personal words.
- **The equal half-shekel:**
  - In a church or community, no one's wealth, status, or giving buys them a better position before God. This can shape how communities welcome and honor people across economic lines.
- **Accepting rebirth:**
  - Someone who keeps defining themselves by past failures can practice receiving forgiveness and a new identity.
  - A simple daily practice, such as washing hands or showering, can become a reminder of cleansing and a fresh start.
- **Holiness that spreads:**
  - Treating coworkers, neighbors, and strangers as image-bearers, people God's holiness can touch.
  - Seeing an ordinary kitchen, office, or commute as a place where God's presence can be found.
- **Not controlling God's work:**
  - Resisting the urge to decide who "deserves" God's grace or to treat faith as a system we manage.
  - Holding our ministries, churches, and spiritual practices with open hands, since the redemptive work is God's.

### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

1. **Tabernacle as microcosm:** How Jewish and early Christian interpreters connected the seven days of creation with the building of the Tabernacle (compare Exodus 25–40 and Genesis 1–2).
2. **The meaning of *tselem Elohim*:** Ancient Near Eastern background on "image" language, and what it suggests about human vocation and dignity.
3. **Incense in Scripture:** How incense works as an image of prayer from Exodus to Psalm 141, Luke 1 (Zechariah at the incense altar), and Revelation 5 and 8.
4. **Census and ransom:** Why counting people was dangerous in Israel's story, and how the half-shekel later became the Temple tax (Matthew 17:24–27).
5. **Theologies of atonement:** What Marty might mean by "bad theology" that desensitizes, and how different atonement frameworks shape a sense of wonder.
6. **Washing and rebirth:** Connections between the bronze basin, *mikveh* immersion, baptism, and Jesus' language of being "born again" (John 3).
7. **Contagious holiness:** How the Hebrew Bible's idea of holiness spreading by contact compares with Jesus touching the unclean and making them clean.
8. **Josh Bosse's *Mishkan* series, Episode 330:** Revisiting the original teaching on wonder and incarnation that guided this reflection.

### Comprehension Questions

1. What was unique about the offerings permitted on the golden altar of incense, and how does Marty use this to invite reflection on offerings that "cost you nothing"?
2. Why did every Israelite pay exactly a half-shekel during the census, and what does the equal amount for rich and poor suggest about atonement?
3. Where was the bronze basin placed, what was it for, and what spiritual reality does Marty connect it to?
4. What happened to anything that touched the anointed Tabernacle furnishings, and how does this shape the question of whether God's holiness spills into "all other spaces and all other people"?
5. According to Marty's conclusion, what do the restrictions on copying the sacred oil and incense teach about our relationship to God's redemptive work?

### Personal Summary

This episode slows everything down. Hearing the sixth day of creation read again and again keeps bringing you back to the claim that humanity was made in God's image, and that only then did God call creation "very good." Each Tabernacle item adds another way to wonder at that. The incense altar invites free offerings of gratitude. The half-shekel reminds us that every life is ransomed at the same price. The basin offers cleansing and a fresh start that can be hard to believe. The oil and incense show a holiness that spreads outward but always belongs to God. The main point to take away is that God's saving work is a gift to receive and marvel at, not something to control, earn, or use for ourselves. The invitation is to let that wonder become a regular rhythm, morning and evening, the way the incense rose before the Lord.
