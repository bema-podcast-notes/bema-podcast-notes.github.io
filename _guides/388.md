### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Psalm 32 let us hear an older David looking back on his failure: hiding wore him down, confession brought relief, and the God he once hid from became his hiding place.

**This week:** Psalm 19 is a psalm of orientation, where creation and God's instruction both shine, and it asks whether we are listening.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Brent leads this episode on Psalm 19, a psalm he thinks about almost every time he flies. He and Reed call it a psalm of orientation, close to the garden before the fall, where sin appears only briefly and from a distance. They admit what many readers feel: the first half, about the silent glory of the heavens, is easy to love, while the second half, about the law, can feel disconnected and repetitive.

Drawing on Rabbi David Fohrman, Jewish commentators, C.S. Lewis and Frederick Buechner, they show how the two halves are one song. The heavens speak without words, and the Torah, made entirely of words, shines like the sun. Reed describes moving away from seeing the law as a test we are bound to fail toward seeing it as a path that is life itself. The psalm closes with "my rock and my redeemer," and the episode closes with two questions: "Are you listening?" and "Will we join in the proclamation?"

### The Heavens Without Words <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-heavens-without-words}

*The heavens never stop speaking, and they never make a sound.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Psalm 19:1–6 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The heavens "pour forth speech" day after day and night after night, yet "they have no speech, they use no words." Fohrman points out that large movements are usually loud, like jet engines, so we might expect the turning of the sky to be deafening; instead it is silent[^2]. Then the sun comes out "like a bridegroom" and "like a champion," which Reed hears as eager and exuberant rather than cold clockwork[^3]. What do you notice about how the psalmist describes the sky and the sun? What feelings does the passage seem to want to stir?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> In verse 4 the Hebrew text has *qav*, "their measuring line," while the Septuagint and other ancient versions have "their voice." The words differ by roughly one letter. Ibn Ezra and Fohrman keep "line," because it fits the sun's fixed circuit across the sky. The notes conclude that translators sometimes have to make judgment calls, and footnotes don't always explain why[^4]. Our traditions hold Scripture in very high regard and talk about translation in different ways. How do you respond when you learn that a verse has a real textual question behind it, and what helps you trust the text while still asking honest questions?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes recall Louis C.K.'s "miracle of flight" bit: we watch the clouds out the window like TV and forget we are actually up in the sky. Familiarity dulls wonder[^5]. Reed's campus minister read Psalm 19:1–4 on that bridge and then asked, "Are you listening?"[^6] What has become so familiar to you that you have stopped noticing it? What would it take to start listening again this week?

</div>

### Torah That Shines <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#torah-that-shines}

*The second half describes God's instruction in the language of sunlight.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Psalm 19:7–11 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes list the Torah's qualities and their sunlight connections: perfect and refreshing the soul, trustworthy, radiant and giving light to the eyes, pure and enduring, firm, and more precious than gold and sweeter than honey, which share the sun's color[^7]. Read the verses again slowly. Which words sound to you like sunlight, and which surprised you once you heard them that way?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Fohrman highlights wordplay in verse 11. The word for "warned," *nizhar*, without its prefix becomes *zohar*, "radiance" or "sunlight," so the servant is both warned and illuminated. Reed links "may they not rule over me" (v. 13) to Genesis 1, where the sun is made to "rule" the day[^8]. If God's instruction is meant to rule our lives the way the sun rules the day, what would that look and feel like?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Reed describes his old view of Torah as a test path toward heaven where one misstep means failure, meant to show you that you are wretched and hopeless. His better view: walking the path doesn't so much lead to life as *is* life, like a hiking loop that ends where it began but leaves you more alive[^9]. Buechner distinguishes laws about how things *should be* ("No Trespassing") from laws about how things *are* (gravity), and puts God's law in the second group, summed up in 1 John 3:14: "Whoever does not love abides in death."[^10] Our traditions have taught different things about the law and grace. Without deciding who is right, what were you taught the law is for, and how does Reed's picture sit with you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Underneath Reed's view is the idea "I am my beloved's and my beloved is mine": the law is a gift from God, and keeping it is a gift we give back[^11]. The psalmist talks about God's instruction the way a lover talks about the beloved. Where in your life does God's way feel like a gift you treasure, and where does it still feel like a test you are afraid of failing?

</div>

### My Rock and My Redeemer <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#my-rock-and-my-redeemer}

*The psalm ends by holding stillness and movement together.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Psalm 19:12–14 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Reed notes that a rock is strong because it doesn't move, while a redeemer is strong because it moves. He offers Harriet Tubman as an example, a redeemer who kept crossing borders and yet was a firm foundation for the people she led, "a rock that's moving." The sun is both: it moves across our sky and ends the darkness, yet from where we stand it is the most stationary thing we know[^12]. When have you needed God to be your rock, and when your redeemer?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> C.S. Lewis called Psalm 19 "the greatest poem in the Psalter and one of the greatest lyrics in the world," and Reed paraphrases another Lewis line: "I believe in Christianity as I believe that the sun has risen: not only because I see it, but because by it I see everything else."[^13] What is one thing you see differently because of your faith, the way everything looks different in daylight?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Psalm 19 can feel like two poems, one about a glorious sky and one about a list of commandments, but it is one song. The heavens speak without words, and the Torah, made of words, shines like the sun. Both are gifts from the same Creator, and both orient us in a world that is easy to get lost in. In Jewish practice the psalm belongs to Sabbath morning prayer, where *Shacharit* (morning prayer) is meant to be prayed near sunrise because creation is "having a party" and you either join the conversation or miss it[^14]. The episode leaves two questions to carry into each morning: are you listening, and once you have listened, will you join in the proclamation?
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** Going around the room in one sentence each, with no comments until everyone has spoken: what is one place or time this week where you will stop and listen?

</div>
