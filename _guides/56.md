### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** 2 Isaiah pronounced woe on every nation, Jerusalem included, because no one cared for others or worked toward restoration. Even so, glimpses of hope broke through, like a feast for all peoples.[^1]

**This week:** Jeremiah, the weeping prophet, opens the Babylonian prophets with the hardest message yet: the time for repentance has passed.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Jeremiah prophesied before, during and after the Babylonian conquest. His message was that Judah had failed to repent, God's discipline had arrived, and the best move was to lay down their weapons and go into captivity to spare bloodshed. He was so pro-Babylon that the Babylonians left him behind as an ally.[^3] He is called the weeping prophet because the calling was so hard: he felt "seduced" by God into a task that brought him no friendships, only rejection.[^4]

The episode highlights three things. Jeremiah sees everything through covenant relationship, especially the marriage between God and Israel. He shows that idolatry and injustice feed each other: the gods we serve shape how we treat people. And he introduces the Valley of Hinnom, the real place behind the New Testament word *gehenna*, usually translated "hell."[^5] Some of these topics (modern idols, refugees, hell) touch convictions held differently in the room, so the questions ask us to describe and notice rather than to win an argument.

### A Bride Who Wandered <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#a-bride-who-wandered}

*Jeremiah describes sin first as a broken relationship.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Jeremiah 2:1–13 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes point to verse 2 (the devotion of Israel's youth, following God "through the desert") and verse 13 (forsaking the Spring of Living Water to dig broken cisterns).[^6] What pictures of the relationship between God and his people do you notice in this passage, and what feelings do you hear in God's voice?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes say Jeremiah's dominant metaphor is a marriage between God and Israel, and that "the key problem isn't abstract theology or concepts—it's broken relationship." To the faithless people God says, "I am your husband" (Jeremiah 3:14).[^7] What changes when sin is described as a broken relationship rather than as a broken rule, and which way of describing it did you grow up hearing?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Jeremiah cried out, "You seduced me, God, and I was seduced." The notes explain that he thought he was signing up for a glorious calling and found it unbearably hard, with no friendships or accolades. His scribe Baruch even had to rewrite the whole scroll after it was burned.[^8] When has being faithful cost you more than you expected, and what kept you going (or nearly made you quit)?

</div>

### Idolatry Feeds Injustice <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#idolatry-feeds-injustice}

*The gods we serve show up in how we treat the foreigner, the orphan and the widow.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Jeremiah 7:1–11 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes observe that this passage first addresses injustice (dealing justly, not oppressing the foreigner, the fatherless or the widow) and then idolatry (burning incense to Baal), and that the people trusted the temple to keep them safe regardless of their behavior.[^9] What do the people keep repeating in this passage, what does God list against them, and what does verse 11 say God has been doing all along?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes point out that Jesus quotes Jeremiah 7:11 when he cleanses the temple (Matthew 21:13, Mark 11:17, Luke 19:46), showing that "religious devotion without justice" had persisted.[^10] How does knowing Jeremiah's setting change the way you hear Jesus' words in the temple?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Marty explains: "Idolatry feeds the injustice. If we were to talk about the god of comfort or the god of leisure, what does that come at the expense of? Our idolatry is directly connected to our injustice, and injustice feeds our idolatry." The episode names modern examples: a god of consumerism (cheap goods at the expense of exploited workers), a god of security (closing doors to refugees, the modern foreigner, fatherless and widow), and a god of comfort or leisure (ignoring those in need). The proposed remedy is a "reverse catch-22": look out for the alien, orphan and widow, and let them remind you of "the appropriate story."[^11] We may disagree about policy, so set that aside for a moment. Which of these gods do you find hardest to notice in your own life, and who might be paying for it? What is one way you could let a vulnerable person remind you of that story this month?

</div>

### A Valley Outside the City <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#a-valley-outside-the-city}

*Hell, in Jeremiah, starts as a place on the map.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Jeremiah 7:30–34 and Jeremiah 19:10–11 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes explain that the Valley of Ben Hinnom (also called Tophet) was used for child sacrifice, and that Jeremiah smashed a potter's jar in front of the people as a piece of street theater.[^12] What do you notice about what happened in this valley, what it will be renamed, and what the broken jar is meant to say?

*Read 2 Chronicles 36:20–21 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say Chronicles, written with hindsight, ties the seventy years to the land enjoying its Sabbath rest. That points to the people's failure to keep the *shemitah* (the seventh-year rest for the land) and the Year of Jubilee (debt forgiveness and land restoration), which provided for those on the bottom.[^13] What does it tell you that the writer of Chronicles explains the exile in economic terms?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes trace the history of the valley: Hebrew *Gei Hinnom*, Greek *gehenna*, used twelve times in the New Testament, eleven of them by Jesus. It became Jerusalem's garbage dump with a perpetual trash fire. The episode suggests that when Jesus used the image, he was pointing to a physical place his hearers knew, and it describes hell as "the utter desolation of choosing to not be a part of what God's doing," a present reality and not only a future one. The notes say this is a topic Session 3 will take up again.[^14] Our traditions hold a range of views on hell, and no one needs to change theirs tonight. What does this background add to how you read Jesus' warnings, and where do you see the "desolation of choosing to not be a part of what God's doing" in the world, or in yourself, right now?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Jeremiah was called to say what no one wanted to hear: the time for repentance had passed, and the way through was to accept discipline. He wept because he saw everything as a broken covenant, a bride who had run after other lovers and dug cisterns that could not hold water. He showed that the gods we serve shape how we treat the foreigner, the orphan and the widow, and that a temple without justice becomes a den of robbers. The valley outside the city where children were sacrificed became a garbage dump and, in time, an image of what it means to refuse God's work in the world.[^15]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, name one "broken cistern" you are tempted to dig instead of returning to the Spring of Living Water.

</div>
