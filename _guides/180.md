### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Sardis looked alive but had twice fallen because its guards slept, and Jesus told the church to wake up. The episode weighed whether Sardis's synagogue in the gymnasium showed missional engagement or compromise, and set the promise of white robes against the blood-stained robes of Cybele's festival.

**This week:** Philadelphia was the door to Asia Minor, a city shaken by earthquakes, rebuilt by emperors, and renamed against its will. John's letter speaks of open doors, pillars that never move, and new names, drawing on Isaiah and the temple of Solomon.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The notes describe Philadelphia as the geographic gateway on the main highway, known as "the door to Asia Minor."[^1] Earthquakes in AD 17 and AD 60 led to imperial rebuilding and repeated renaming: Neo-Caesarea after the first, and an attempt at Flavia after the second, which residents rejected because they despised the Flavian dynasty.[^2] That hatred deepened when the Flavians forced the city to uproot more than 75% of its prized grapevines to grow grain that never flourished.[^3]

The hosts make their "Text-to-context" approach explicit: look at the city's culture and at the Old Testament passages John draws on through *remez*.[^4] Isaiah 45 supplies the open door and a Gentile, Cyrus, used by God to save his people; Isaiah 62 supplies the new name; and the named pillars of Solomon's temple, Jachin and Boaz, frame both the door and the name.[^5] Behind "I am coming soon" stands the *parousia*, the official visit an emperor made to inspect a rebuilt city.[^6] Because this is a short episode, the guide gives generous time to reading the passages side by side.

### The Door to Asia Minor <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-door-to-asia-minor}

*Philadelphia's identity was tied to its location, and the letter begins with doors.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Revelation 3:7–9 aloud, then Isaiah 45:1–4.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes say Philadelphia's position on the main highway "earned it the designation as 'the door to Asia Minor,'" so that travelers passed through it in both directions, and they conclude that the letter's door imagery "isn't abstract - it's geographic, economic, and strategic."[^7] Compare Revelation 3:7–8 with Isaiah 45:1–2. What words and images about doors and keys do the two passages share?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** In Isaiah 45, the notes point out, God opens doors that cannot be shut, uses a Gentile, Cyrus, to save and redeem his people, and summons him by name with a title of honor.[^8] The notes say this "Directly addresses the 'synagogue of Satan' issue: Gentiles have been part of God's redemptive work throughout history."[^9] Read Isaiah 45:1–4 again. Why might Gentile believers who were being told they did not belong find this passage especially encouraging?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe the "synagogue of Satan," mentioned also in the Smyrna letter, as a group of Jews who refused to accept Gentiles as legitimate participants in the faith, a position Paul argued misunderstood "the mission and message of Judaism." They call it "strongly stated language addressing a real theological and social conflict in the early church."[^10] In the Smyrna letter (Week 43) we saw how this phrase has been misused. What safeguards help us read strong language like this without turning it against Jewish people today?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Jesus says he has placed before this church "an open door that no one can shut." The notes read it as God "affirming their identity and role as a gateway."[^11] Where might God have placed an open door in front of you or your church right now, and what keeps you from walking through it?

</div>

### Three Names and Two Pillars <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#three-names-and-two-pillars}

*The notes say the letter's repeated naming only looks repetitive until you know the city's story.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Revelation 3:10–12 aloud, then Isaiah 62:1–5 and 2 Chronicles 3:15–17.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes trace Philadelphia's names: Philadelphia originally, Neo-Caesarea after the AD 17 earthquake "to honor the rebuilding Caesar," an attempted Flavia after AD 60 that residents rejected, and then a return to Philadelphia, creating "a unique cultural context of identity instability and resistance to imposed names."[^12] Count how many times a name is mentioned in Revelation 3:12, and then in Isaiah 62:1–5. What do you notice?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes say that without context the threefold naming promise "seems needlessly repetitive," but "Philadelphia had three different names for their city."[^13] From Isaiah 62 they draw being called by a new name the Lord bestows, a crown, vindication, glory, and marriage and divine delight.[^14] From 2 Chronicles 3 they draw two pillars before the temple that were given names, Jachin and Boaz, and which framed its doorway.[^15] How do the pillar and the new name answer a city that had been repeatedly shaken, forced to flee, and renamed by emperors?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes call the pillar promise a hope of "an unshakeable identity and place" for people repeatedly forced from their homes.[^16] They suggest further study of how new names function across Scripture, such as Abram to Abraham and Jacob to Israel.[^17] Which of those biblical renamings speaks most to you, and why?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Philadelphia had names imposed by those who held power over it. What names or labels has the world, or your own past, tried to place on you, and what would it mean to receive instead the name God writes on you?

</div>

### I Am Coming Soon <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#i-am-coming-soon}

*The promise of Jesus's coming meant something specific to a city that had watched emperors arrive.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Revelation 3:11–13 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** Building on Episode 161, the notes explain that after disasters Rome funded rebuilding and the emperor would then make a *parousia* (Greek, an official visit or coming) to inspect the rebuilt city. Philadelphia experienced this pattern several times, so they "know what it means for a powerful figure to come inspect, validate, and celebrate what has been built."[^18] Set that beside the Flavians' order to uproot most of the city's vineyards for grain that never grew.[^19] How does Jesus's coming compare with the emperors' visits in what it promises this city?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say that to a city that lost 75% of its economic base by imperial decree, the promise of vindication and divine favor "carries profound economic and social implications."[^20] Where have you seen a community recover its dignity after decisions made far away stripped it of something it valued?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q11:** Jesus tells this church to hold on to what it has so that no one takes its crown. As you think about what has been built in your own life of faith, what do you most want him to find when he comes?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Philadelphia stood at the door to Asia Minor and kept getting knocked down. Earthquakes shook it, emperors rebuilt it and stamped their own names on it, and one dynasty tore out its vineyards for grain that never grew. To this tired city Jesus speaks in the language of Isaiah and of Solomon's temple. He holds the key, he sets before them a door no one can shut, and he promises to make the faithful pillars that never have to leave, bearing his name, the name of his city, and his own new name. This time the name will not be imposed by an empire. It will be a gift.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one word you hope God would write on you?

</div>
