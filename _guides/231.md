### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Mandy Smith described rest as letting go of the wish to be God, and named the "adult-ish" self that blocks childlike openness with shame and fear.[^1]

**This week:** Reed Dent joins the BEMA teaching team and offers new pictures for deconstruction, asking what exactly is being deconstructed, why, and how to do it well within community.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Reed Dent, who serves with Campus Christian Fellowship at Truman State University, joins the podcast as a new host. The notes say BEMA itself has always been about deconstructing typical evangelical readings of the Bible and reconstructing them around a more contextual, rabbinical hermeneutic, and that deconstruction does not have to mean destruction. Reed offers three alternative pictures: pruning, redrawing maps, and removing scaffolding. He names five common catalysts (new information, cognitive dissonance, disillusionment, disconnection and trauma) and asks whether what is being questioned is an interpretation, a theology, an experience, or our picture of God.[^3]

At the center is a parable Reed credits to his friend Danny Wesley: a Dr. Seuss-like building in the desert, surrounded by straight-lined scaffolding that a later wanderer mistakes for the building itself. The episode ends with what healthy deconstruction needs: friendship, humility, permission to question and permission to challenge, among flesh-and-blood people.[^4] This topic touches real lives. Share only what you choose to share, and listen to one another with care.

### Not Destruction: Pruning and Redrawing Maps <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#not-destruction-pruning-and-redrawing-maps}

*Reed suggests that faith may be less like a building being torn down and more like a plant being pruned or a map being redrawn.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read John 15:1–8 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Reed uses Jesus's picture of pruning as one alternative metaphor for deconstruction. Pruning is not only about correcting character flaws; it can also trim away false ideas about the Bible or God, and "Sometimes God even prunes away painful things like community or church experiences to promote healthier growth."[^5] What does the vinedresser do in this passage, and to which branches? What is the purpose of the cutting?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Reed compares faith to a journey with maps we receive from others. If you have only seen the flat land, you can be fifty yards from the Grand Canyon without knowing it, and refusing to redraw the map can be dangerous. He adds, from building his own kayak, "Why would we expect an 18-year-old leaving home to have no more questions, nothing left to correct, set for life?"[^6] What is one part of the map you were first given about the Bible that you have had to redraw? What did the first map get right?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Reed names five catalysts for deconstruction: new information, cognitive dissonance, disillusionment, disconnection and trauma. Trauma is the darkest of them, and the notes say it is not "training," and that dismissing it that way is deeply harmful.[^7] Why does it matter to ask what is driving someone's questions instead of treating every question as rebellion or weakness? If a friend told you they were deconstructing, what would you want to ask first, and what would you want to avoid saying?

</div>

### The Scaffolding and the Seuss Building <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-scaffolding-and-the-seuss-building}

*Reed's parable distinguishes the text, the constructs we build around it, and the life with God it holds.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** In Reed's parable, the twisty Dr. Seuss building is the biblical text (sometimes messy and paradoxical), the scaffolding is our doctrines, interpretations and systematic theologies, and the inside of the building is the life lived with God. Reed is careful to say that both conservative scaffolding (the Chicago Statement on Inerrancy, biblical literalism) and progressive scaffolding (genre criticism, "Bible as literature") are scaffolding, and that scaffolding is not inherently bad.[^8] What does scaffolding help us do? How could you tell whether a piece of scaffolding is helping you reach the building or blocking its doors and windows?

*Read John 5:39–40 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes paraphrase Jesus to the scribes this way: "You search the scriptures looking for eternal life and you don't realize they are pointing to me." Marty adds a picture from Robert Farrar Capon: every house needs a porch to reach the door, even though porches rot and need replacing, but the point was never the door itself; it is what is on the other side.[^9] What do you notice in this passage about where life is found? How do the scaffolding and porch pictures help you hear it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Reed says, "Sometimes the truth is hard because it's not what you've always been told, but sometimes the truth is hard because it is what you've always been told."[^8] Consider this diagnostically, for yourself and for the tradition you know best. Which half of that sentence is harder for you to accept, and why do you think that is?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Brent observes that even if you have examined every part of a door, being outside it is totally different from being inside.[^9] What is one practice that would help you spend less time analyzing the door and more time dwelling inside with God this week?

</div>

### Doing It Well Together <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#doing-it-well-together}

*Reed insists that deconstruction done well happens among people who know and love each other.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Reed quotes Pope John Paul II: "The appropriate context for theological investigation is love and mutual friendship." He also warns that online tools, however helpful, cannot replace local communities and friends who can look you in the eye, and that online echo chambers can prevent needed pushback.[^10] Why do you think real friendship changes what is possible in a hard conversation about faith?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes describe two permissions a healthy community gives: permission to question (to say something like "I really don't know about inerrancy or what I mean by that" without fear of being cast out) and permission to challenge, lovingly. They describe Jesus as one who "comforts the disturbed and disturbs the comfortable."[^10] What would it take for this group to offer both permissions to one another? Which one do you find harder to give, and which harder to receive?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Reed Dent suggests that deconstruction can be pruning, map-making, or clearing scaffolding that has grown over the doors and windows of the text. Scaffolding helps us reach parts of the building we could never reach alone, and the Bible itself is living and active, a place where we meet God. But the goal was never to admire the scaffolding or study the door; it is to go inside and live with God. That kind of work is best done among friends who love us enough to let us question and to challenge us when we need it.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one way you could be a safer friend for someone who is asking hard questions about faith?

</div>
