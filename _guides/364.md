### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** With Brueggemann, we saw the prophet as a spokesperson who criticizes through grief and energizes through hope, and heard that "prophecy without doxology becomes ideology."[^1]

**This week:** Marty and Brent begin reading Isaiah itself, taking the opening six chapters through the image of a frustrated father pleading with stubborn children.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The hosts frame Isaiah 1–39 as its own section, tied to the reigns of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz and Hezekiah. They then read a comfortable, prosperous Judah that keeps up its worship while ignoring justice, God's sincere invitation to "reason together," and the vision of nations streaming to the mountain of the Lord.[^2]

Two images carry the second half: the rabbinic idea of "backing into the future," and the *netzer* (shoot) that grows from a pruned olive stump. The episode ends with Isaiah's throne-room call in chapter 6, which the hosts read as a picture of prophetic pathos. The hosts also warn against reading Isaiah 1 as being about "those foolish Israelites"; the question is how it speaks to our own comfortable, church-going cultures.[^3]

### A Father and His Comfortable Children <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#a-father-and-his-comfortable-children}

*Isaiah opens not with a judge's verdict but with a parent's grief over a people who are religious, comfortable and blind to injustice.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 1:2–4 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The hosts point to the contrast in verse 3: the ox and the donkey know their master, but Israel "does not know." They say the relationship looks less like a cold legal arrangement and more like a scorned spouse or a frustrated parent.[^4] What words would you use for the tone of these verses? Point to the phrases that give you that impression.

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty shares a midrash from Tommy Brown's *The Ache for Meaning*. A father carries his child through the market on his shoulders and buys everything the child asks for. At the end of the day the child asks a stranger, "Have you seen my daddy?" The father answers, "You foolish child, you are riding on my shoulders." The point is that when God becomes the supplier of what we want, we stop recognizing him as Father.[^5] When has your own faith felt most like a transaction, and what helped you notice?

*Read Isaiah 1:10–17 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Judah is not irreligious. They keep the new moons, Sabbaths, festivals and offerings, and their worship may even feel sincere to them. Yet God rejects it, because how they live and shape their world contradicts it. Unlike the northern kingdom, the hosts say, Judah as a whole was *benefiting* from its economic system. The remedy God names is *mishpat* (justice): seek justice, defend the oppressed, take up the cause of the fatherless and the widow.[^6] Read as being about us rather than "those Israelites," what in this passage is hardest for a comfortable, church-going culture like ours to hear?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The hosts insist that "Come now, let us reason together" (1:18–20) is a sincere invitation, not sarcasm. They describe two sides in today's polarization: one denies anything is wrong, and the other lives only in the critique of verses 5–14 with no hope. Marty affirms that people's faith journeys and deconstruction are valid, and asks whether there is room within them for repair.[^7] Where are you more likely to stall, in denial or in critique without hope? What would one small act of repair look like for you?

</div>

### Backing Into the Future <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#backing-into-the-future}

*Between the judgments sits a vision of hope, and a striking picture of how God's people face what is coming.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> In Isaiah 2:1–5, all nations stream to Zion to learn God's ways and beat their swords into plowshares. The hosts stress that Isaiah does not imagine God giving up on his plan. The hope is Plan A renewed, not a Plan B.[^8] What difference does it make to think of hope as God's original purpose restored rather than a backup plan?

*Read Isaiah 2:2–8 aloud, and compare how your translations render verse 6.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The NIV and NET add "Lord" in verse 6 ("You, Lord, have abandoned your people"), and the NET admits the word was supplied; the ESV reads "you have rejected your people." Following Robert Alter, Marty suggests the "you" is the nation itself: Judah has abandoned its calling, its identity and its story, looking east for superstitions and west to the Philistines for divination.[^9] What does your translation say? Read the verses again assuming the people are the ones doing the abandoning. What changes?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** "In the last days" (2:2) can be read more literally as "in the behind days." In rabbinic thought, pagan nations tried to predict the future through divination, while God's people walk backward into a future only God can see, with their eyes on what God has already done.[^10] The hosts suggest this as a practice for uncertain times. What is one thing God has already done, in Scripture or in your own story, that you need to keep your eyes on right now?

</div>

### Pruned Stumps and a Burning Call <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#pruned-stumps-and-a-burning-call}

*Judgment in Isaiah is real, but it serves renewal, and Isaiah's own call comes out of an encounter he cannot unsee.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** *Netzer* means a shoot or branch, and *Nazareth* likely comes from the same root ("shoot town"). Marty was taught that every few centuries an olive trunk is cut flat below the branches, and a new shoot grows from the stump; without pruning the tree grows wild and useless. Christians rightly connect the branch with Jesus, but Marty suggests Isaiah's primary concern was the remnant of his own people.[^11] What does the pruning image suggest about the purpose of God's judgment in Isaiah 4?

*Read Isaiah 6:1–13 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The hosts read "Here am I, send me" two ways. Brent hears someone just restored and eager to go. Marty hears someone who may be terrified but answers anyway, overwhelmed by an encounter that leaves no real alternative. Brent also hears in "For how long, Lord?" an echo of Abraham bargaining in Genesis 18, except Isaiah never negotiates.[^12] Which reading rings truer to you as you listen to the whole chapter, and why?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The hosts compare Isaiah's yes to Frodo at the Council of Elrond: a reluctant, costly yes to a burden he cannot put down. The answer to "how long" is ruin, yet "the holy seed will be the stump in the land."[^13] When have you said yes to something hard because, having seen what you saw, you could not do otherwise? What is God asking you to keep saying yes to now?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Isaiah begins with a father's grief, not a judge's verdict. His audience was comfortable, prosperous and religious, keeping the festivals while their hands were "full of blood" from injustice. Yet Isaiah does not stop at critique: God says "Come now, let us reason together" and holds out a vision of swords turned into plowshares, the original plan renewed. Backing into the future and the shoot rising from a pruned stump both point to trust in what God has already done and in pruning that serves new life. Isaiah's "Here am I, send me," whether spoken in eagerness or in fear, models a faithfulness that answers God's presence even when the road looks like ruin.[^14]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one thing God has already done that you want to keep your eyes on this week?

</div>
