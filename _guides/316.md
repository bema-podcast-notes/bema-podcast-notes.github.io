### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** In the Lost Son, the father ran to his son before any confession, then went out again to the older brother standing outside the party, and Jesus left the ending for his listeners to write.[^1]

**This week:** In the last episode of the parables series, Brent and Reed work through the story Jesus tells after Peter asks how many times he has to forgive his brother.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Jesus answers Peter with "seventy-seven times," which echoes Lamech's boast about revenge in Genesis 4. Reed, following Robert Farrar Capon, argues that the king's cancellation of an impossible debt is more than one act of mercy: it ends "bookkeeping," the whole system of keeping score of who owes what.[^2] He then points to a mirror in the story, where the second servant begs with almost the same words as the first, and takes it as an invitation to see the people who wrong us as people like ourselves, both victims and perpetrators.[^2]

The episode also explains what forgiveness is and what it is not. Reed is careful not to make real suffering seem small, and he treats the torment of refusing to forgive as a prison we build for ourselves.[^3] Some of this may touch real wounds. Throughout the session, sharing is always optional, and no one needs to name a person or an event.

### Opening <span class="guide-time">10 min</span> {#opening}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-opening" markdown="1">

**Opening Question:** Reed jokes that with three sons sharing bedrooms, 490 offenses could pile up in a few weeks; the people closest to us are usually the ones we have to forgive most often.[^4] Growing up, or in your home now, what small offenses needed forgiving most often, and how did people usually handle them?

### Seventy-Seven Times <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#seventy-seven-times}

*Peter asks for a number, and Jesus answers by pointing back to the first story of escalating revenge.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 18:21–22 aloud, then Genesis 4:23–24.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes give three ways to read Jesus' number: as math (70 × 7 = 490), as a symbol of "completeness upon completeness," and as an echo of Lamech, who brags, "If Cain is avenged seven times, then Lamech seventy-seven times."[^5] Set the two passages side by side. What do you notice about how Jesus' answer relates to Lamech's boast?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Reed suggests Peter may be thinking of his actual brother Andrew, and Brent notes the question comes right after Jesus' steps for handling conflict in Matthew 18:15–20, so Peter may simply be asking where the line is.[^6] Peter's "seven" may come from Jesus' own teaching in Luke 17:3–4, or from a discussion like the later Talmudic ruling that you forgive three times.[^6] What is appealing about having a number? What does wanting one reveal?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Lamech answers a small injury with absurdly large revenge, and the Cain and Lamech story shows where escalating revenge leads. Jesus flips the formula: in the Kingdom, the answer to even the largest or longest-running wrongs is absurd grace.[^5] Where do you see the Lamech pattern of escalation around you, in families, online, or between groups? What would it take to interrupt it in one place you have some influence?

</div>

### The Debt and the Mirror <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-debt-and-the-mirror}

*The story's absurd numbers and its two nearly identical pleas carry Reed's central claims.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 18:23–30 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** A denarius was about a day's wage and a talent about 6,000 denarii. The talent was the largest unit of money and 10,000 the largest named number in Greek, so "10,000 talents" means something like "all the money in the world"; Herod the Great's yearly tax income was only about 800 talents.[^7] The second servant owes about 100 denarii, roughly three months' wages.[^8] What do you notice about the two debts, and about what the first servant does immediately after being released?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Compare the pleas in verses 26 and 29 word for word. The first servant says, "Be patient with me, and I will pay back *everything*"; the second says, "Be patient with me, and I will pay it back."[^9] The missing "everything" keeps the debts from being equal, but the posture matches: kneeling, begging, the same words.[^9] What is the same and what is different? Reed takes the mirror as an invitation to look at the person who wronged us and see something of ourselves looking back.[^9] What do you make of that claim?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The king is moved by *splanchnizomai*, the gut-level compassion that in the parables always drives the God figure to rescue.[^10] Capon calls the cancellation "an end to bookkeeping": if the king has absorbed the largest debt imaginable, counting who owes what no longer makes sense in his Kingdom.[^11] Brent notes people would object that this sets a dangerous precedent, an objection that cares about the principle and ignores the person in front of you.[^10] Where do you hear that "precedent" objection in yourself or in church life?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Reed warns against a reading that says whatever was done to you is "only $100" compared to your debt to God; some wrongs, such as abuse and fraud, are not small, and some losses can never be restored.[^12] He offers Eve's words in Robert Alter's translation, "The serpent beguiled me, and I ate": "the serpent beguiled me" names the victim side, and "I ate" names the perpetrator side.[^13] The perpetrator should own "I ate," while the victim is invited, by grace, to see "you were beguiled too."[^13] How does that pair help, or complicate, the way you think about someone who has wronged you? Answer in general terms if you prefer.

</div>

### What Forgiveness Is and Is Not <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#what-forgiveness-is-and-is-not}

*Drawing on Miroslav Volf, the episode separates forgiveness from several things it is often confused with.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 18:31–35 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes list what forgiveness is not: condoning, forgetting, allowing the behavior to continue, restoring trust ("If someone shot you, forgiving them doesn't mean handing the gun back"), or reconciliation ("It takes one to forgive but two to reconcile").[^14] Drawing on Volf, forgiveness is naming the wrong ("All forgiveness involves accusation"), releasing the debt, cutting the tie between the offense and how we treat the offender, and walking a road rather than having a single moment.[^14] Our traditions have taught forgiveness in different ways. Which item on either list surprises you, or reshapes something you were taught?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Brent calls the king's anger the first reasonable response in an absurd story, while Reed wonders whether the God figure suddenly brings bookkeeping back.[^15] Reed offers another reading: the master lets the servant live in the world he chose, and since the Greek of verse 34 is ambiguous, the line could even mean "until he releases the debt he is owed," so the servant holds the key to his own cell.[^15] Frederick Buechner adds that "Forgiveness that's conditional isn't really forgiveness at all."[^16] How do you read the ending and verse 35 in light of these suggestions?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** Buechner prays: "The pride which keeps us from forgiving is the same pride which keeps us from accepting forgiveness, and will God please help us do something about it."[^16] The episode describes forgiveness as a road that may mean saying "By God's grace, I forgive you" again every day.[^17] Without naming anyone, where would you place yourself on that road right now: naming the wrong, releasing the debt, recommitting, or not yet ready to begin? What would one honest next step look like?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Peter asked for a limit, and Jesus pointed him back to Lamech, replacing seventy-seven-fold vengeance with seventy-seven-fold grace. The king's gut-level compassion absorbed a debt no one could repay and ended the bookkeeping altogether, but the servant walked out still counting. Reed's invitation is the mirror: the person who wronged us was also beguiled, and seeing that can slowly turn bitterness into compassion. Forgiveness does not mean forgetting, excusing, or rushing back into trust; it means naming the wound and releasing the debt, often again and again.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** This is our last week in the parables. In one sentence each, going around the room: which image from these five weeks will you carry with you (the house with windows, the traveler who came close, the barn builder's "who" question, the running father, or the end of bookkeeping), and why?

</div>
