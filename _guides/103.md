### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Four friends carried a paralyzed man through a roof, and Jesus forgave before he healed. As the "Son of Man," Jesus may have been claiming that the one with every right to justice comes to forgive.

**This week:** The rest of Matthew 9 brings a string of healings. At the center is a woman who reaches for the tassel of Jesus' garment because she knows what the prophets promised.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode's guiding claim is that everything Jesus does is "a commentary on the Hebrew Text." Numbers 15 commanded God's people to wear *tzitzit* (tassels) on the *kanafot* (corners) of their garments, and the Hebrew word *kanaf* means both "corner" and "wing." Malachi promised that the sun of righteousness would rise "with healing in its wings." The episode argues that the woman who had bled for twelve years, excluded from worship and assembly, touched Jesus' tassel deliberately, as an act of faith in who he was.

The hosts also look at the "messianic secret," the pattern of Jesus telling people to stay quiet in some places and to spread the word in others. They hear in "sheep without a shepherd" a reference to Ezekiel 34, the prophet's own critique of Israel's leaders, and they offer a fresh reading of "the harvest is plentiful, but the workers are few" that draws on the book of Ruth.

### Healing in the Corners <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#healing-in-the-corners}

*A tassel on a corner of a garment connects a desperate woman to a promise in the prophets.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Numbers 15:37–41 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes explain the vocabulary: the *tallit* is the garment or prayer shawl, the *tzitzit* are the tassels, and the *kanaf* is the corner. The tassels were "tactile, physical reminders" of identity as God's people, of covenant obligations, and of protection under God's "wings."[^1] What does Numbers 15 say the tassels are for? Why might God give people something to touch and see, not just words to remember?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes apply this to David cutting off the corner of Saul's robe in the cave (1 Samuel 24). He was cutting off Saul's *tzitzit*, and his guilt makes sense because the tassel represented God's protection: "David felt he had presumed to decide Saul was no longer under God's protection."[^2] How does knowing about the tassels change the way you read David's guilt?

*Read Malachi 4:1–3, then Matthew 9:18–26 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes quote Malachi 4:2, "for you who fear my name, the sun of righteousness shall rise with healing in its wings," and point out that the word for "wings" is *kanaf*, the same word used for the corners of the garment.[^3] According to the notes, some first-century Jews believed one sign of the Messiah would be healing in his *kanafot*. Her touch was a declaration: "I believe you are Messiah because there is healing in your kanafot."[^4] Put Malachi next to Matthew. What do you notice? What changes when you see the woman as someone acting on a promise rather than simply grabbing at Jesus in desperation?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes observe that because of her bleeding, she would not have been able to go to synagogue or join the assembly, so "She would have had to devote herself to learning the Text with intention." When she touched him, "Jesus knows that someone knows their Text."[^5] Her courage, the notes say, took *chutzpah*.[^6] What might twelve years of exclusion have done to her hunger for Scripture? Who today might know God's word deeply while being kept at the edges of religious life?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The original notes include a simple question: "Which character do you relate to the most and why?"[^7] Among the people in Matthew 9:18–26 (the grieving synagogue leader, the woman, the hired mourners, the crowd, the disciples), whom do you most see yourself in right now?

</div>

### Secrets and Shepherds <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#secrets-and-shepherds}

*Jesus tells some people to keep quiet and others to tell everyone, and he looks at the crowds with compassion.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 9:27–38 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes describe a pattern in the "messianic secret." In pagan areas like the Decapolis, Jesus tells people to go and tell; in the Jewish triangle of towns, he tells people not to tell anyone.[^8] The notes suggest that "Religious self-righteousness can be a greater barrier than paganism."[^9] Recall the man sent home to the Decapolis two weeks ago. Why might a religious setting be harder ground for Jesus' message than a pagan one? Where might that be true of religious settings today, including our own?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Jesus sees the crowds as "harassed and helpless, like sheep without a shepherd." The notes say this echoes Ezekiel 34, where the prophet condemns Israel's own shepherds for caring for themselves and not for the flock.[^10]

*Read Ezekiel 34:1–6 aloud.*

List what Ezekiel says the shepherds failed to do. Then look back at Matthew 9. Which of those failures does Jesus' ministry address?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The original notes put the cause this way: "The misguided focus on obedience without compassion is creating sheep without a shepherd."[^11] Every tradition, including ours, can drift toward obedience without compassion. What does that drift look like in a church? What does it look like in a person?

</div>

### A Plentiful Harvest <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#a-plentiful-harvest}

*A familiar call for more workers may be about a different kind of harvest.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Ruth 2:1–9 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes question the usual reading of "the harvest is plentiful, but the workers are few" as a call for more evangelists or clergy. "In biblical texts, harvest represents God's blessing, not unsaved people," and the only place "workers" and "harvest" appear together is in Ruth, where the poor glean what is left behind.[^12] The alternative: God's blessings are abundant, but no one is gathering them for the harassed and helpless. What do you notice in Ruth 2 about how the harvest reaches someone like Ruth? How does that change what Jesus might be asking for?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The original notes ask, "Which story challenges you to go and be something unique that you weren't before?"[^13] Looking at all of Matthew 9, which story challenges you most? What is one concrete way you could help God's abundance reach someone overlooked this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Every story in Matthew 9 is rooted in the Text. A woman shut out for twelve years reached for the corners of Jesus' garment because she trusted Malachi's promise of healing in his wings. Jesus guarded his identity where religious certainty would twist it and released it where outsiders would receive it. He looked at harassed and helpless people the way Ezekiel said a true shepherd would. And the harvest of God's blessing still lies in the fields, waiting for workers who will make sure it reaches those at the edges.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each: what is one way you could be a shepherd or a harvester for someone this week?

</div>
