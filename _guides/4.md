### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Fear turned Cain's dependence into scarcity, but God's word to him showed that God's view of people had not changed: sin crouches at the door, and we are meant to master it.

**This week:** The flood. The hosts set Genesis beside older flood stories its first readers knew, and find a God who acts against violence, remembers Noah, and hangs up his weapon.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Flood stories were common in the ancient world, and many are older than Genesis. In the Epic of Gilgamesh, the gods flood the earth because humans are noisy, then flee in terror from their own flood, and a hero named Utnapishtim must outsmart them to save life. The episode suggests that Genesis retells this familiar kind of story to show who Israel's God really is: a God who acts against genuine evil, stays in control, and partners with Noah to save creation.

The hosts trace a mirror structure of numbers (7, 7, 40, 150, 150, 40, 7, 7) with Genesis 8:1 at the center: "But God remembered Noah." From there the story echoes Genesis 1 as a re-creation, and it ends with a covenant that overturns the expectations of ancient treaties. The rainbow is literally a "bow," a weapon, and it points up and away from the earth.

### A Different Kind of Flood Story <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#a-different-kind-of-flood-story}

*Genesis retells a story its readers already knew, and changes the God at its center.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes say flood stories "were not unique to the Bible and many of these predate the writing of the Bible." Abraham came from Ur of the Chaldeans, the region of the Gilgamesh hero, so it makes sense that Genesis would retell a story its readers "were used to by painting their G-d in a different light showing who he really is."[^1] What is the difference between borrowing a story and retelling it to make a different point? How does it feel to learn that Genesis is in conversation with other ancient stories?

*Read Genesis 6:5–8 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** In Gilgamesh, the gods destroy humanity because people are "noisy and disruptive to divine sleep," and then the gods flee their own flood "like curs."[^2] Look at the reason Genesis gives and at how God is described. What differences stand out to you? What does each version suggest about what its god cares about?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Brent's first reaction to the story was a problem: everyone was evil all the time except Noah, yet "There has to be someone out there doing something right."[^3] The notes say the episode treats such problems as "gateways to deeper understanding."[^4] What problem do you notice when you read the flood story? How have you been taught to handle problems like that?

</div>

### But God Remembered <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#but-god-remembered}

*At the center of the flood is God's remembering, and what follows looks like creation again.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The flood story is built as a mirror of numbers: 7, 7, 40, 150, then 150, 40, 7, 7. At its center is Genesis 8:1, "But God remembered Noah."[^5] The notes add that the comparison with other flood stories shows God's character: "The god that is trying to destroy creation is simultaneously trying to save it."[^6] Why might the author put God's remembering, rather than the rain or the rescue, at the very center?

*Read Genesis 8:1–12 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes say this part of the story "mirrors the creation story." After the wind (*ruach*, "wind, spirit, breath," the same word as Genesis 1:2), Noah "opens a window and LETS IN THE LIGHT," the waters are separated, dry ground appears, birds go out into the sky, and later the animals and then the humans leave the ark.[^7] Listen back through what was read. Which details remind you of Genesis 1? What does it suggest to call the flood a re-creation rather than only a destruction?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** After creation in Genesis 1 comes Sabbath, so after this re-creation "we should have a sabbath." Instead come verses full of covenant language, and the notes describe God promising to "Sabbath from destroying."[^8] Marty's summary is that this paragraph is about a God who knows when to stop destroying, "in the same way that the Sabbath was about a god who knows when to stop creating." He adds, "Cancer is so bad because it never stops creating."[^9] Where in your own life, or in the world around you, do you see the damage done by something that does not know how to stop?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> At the start of the story God grieves that "every inclination of the human heart is evil." At the end God says, "even though every inclination of the heart of man is evil, **I WILL NOT DESTROY THE EARTH.**"[^10] The flood has not changed human nature. What does it say about God that the promise is made "even though"?

</div>

### A Bow Pointed Toward Heaven <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#a-bow-pointed-toward-heaven}

*God keeps the covenant sign for himself and gives up his leverage.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** In an ancient suzerain-vassal covenant, the suzerain (the stronger party) protected the vassal (the weaker party), and it was the vassal's job to remember and to produce the covenant sign. The Amarna Letters show vassals pleading: "I have paid my tribute and taxes faithfully, yet bandits attack me. Remember our covenant and come rescue me."[^11] In Genesis, God keeps the sign in the clouds and says, "**I will remember the covenant**." The notes add: "Even when the people forget," God will remember.[^12] How does this reverse what ancient listeners would have expected from a powerful god?

*Read Genesis 9:8–17 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The Hebrew word for rainbow, *keshet*, simply means "bow." "Bows are weapons and they face toward the target," and this bow points upward, away from the earth.[^13] The notes also observe that the centers of this paragraph's repeated words (covenant, earth, clouds, bow) form one sentence from verses 14–15: whenever God brings clouds and the bow appears, he will remember his covenant.[^14] What do you notice in this passage about who acts, who remembers, and who is protected?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe God here as voluntarily giving up "His power to threaten destruction."[^15] Many of us carry an image of God as angry and waiting to punish. How does a God who hangs up his weapon challenge or confirm the image of God you carry? Where might that image have come from?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q11:** The notes draw out what this covenant means for us: "God initiates and maintains relationship," and "Divine faithfulness doesn't depend on human performance."[^16] Think of a relationship in which you hold some leverage (as a parent, a boss, a friend who was wronged). What would it look like this week to "hang up the bow" there?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Genesis takes a flood story its readers already knew and tells it differently. Its God acts against violence rather than noise, never loses control, and at the very center of the story remembers Noah. What follows looks like creation again, and it ends not with rest but with God's promise to rest from destroying. The bow in the clouds is a weapon pointed away from us, a sign God keeps for himself so that even when we forget, he remembers.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one thing about God's character that this story invites you to trust more deeply?

</div>
