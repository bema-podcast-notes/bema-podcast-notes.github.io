**This week:** A new series on the women of Joshua and Judges begins by facing the conquest of Canaan head-on and asking what God promised in the first place.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This episode opens the *Women in Joshua and Judges* series, which carries on the approach of the earlier *Forgotten Women* series. Before getting to women like Rahab, Elle Grover Fricks takes on the "elephant in the room": the conquest of Canaan, which many readers find morally hard to accept[^1]. Rather than filing it under mystery right away, she invites us to "lean in" to the text. She reads God's original promise in Exodus 23, Joshua's meeting with the commander of the LORD's army, and the instructions for Jericho, then works through a concordance study of the Hebrew word *ḥerem*, usually translated "destroy."

Her conclusions are a careful reading, and not everyone in the room will be persuaded by all of them. That is fine. The goal tonight is to read closely together, notice what the text actually says, and be honest about what we still find hard. The episode ends with a personal challenge about how we take good gifts from God and add our own agendas to them.

### God's Original Promise <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#gods-original-promise}

*Before Joshua ever crosses the Jordan, God describes how the land will be given, and Elle asks us to start there.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Elle names three common ways people handle the violence in Joshua: reading everything through Christ ("I know who Jesus is"), holding it as a mystery, or treating it as exaggerated ancient Near Eastern war rhetoric. Marty admits he once drew on a "salad" of all three. Elle affirms each one, but thinks people press "the red button of mystery" too quickly[^2]. Which of these postures have you most often seen in your own church tradition, and what has it given you when you meet a hard passage?

*Read Exodus 23:20–33 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** As you listen, make two short lists: what God says God will do, and what Israel is told to do. In Elle's translation, God sends "my dread" (God's, not Israel's), causes a "hubbub" of confusion, sends hornets, and promises to "drive them out" little by little[^3]. What do you notice when you compare your two lists?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Elle points out that the enemy is defined narrowly: God will be "an enemy to your enemies" and "confine those who confine you." Giving Israel "the back of the neck" of its enemies means seeing their backs as they run away, since killing someone from behind was dishonorable. The verb for "drive out," *garash*, is the same one used when Adam and Eve are sent out of Eden: moved to a new place, not killed[^4]. How does reading this promise as liberation, rather than as good people against bad people, change the picture of the conquest you grew up with? Where do you hear the "good people versus bad people" frame in the way Christians talk about others today?

</div>

### Whose Side Is God On? <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#whose-side-is-god-on}

*Israel's entry into the land opens with Passover, a festival of liberation, and a strange encounter outside Jericho.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Joshua 5:13–15 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Joshua asks the man with the drawn sword, "Are you for us or for our adversaries?" The answer is simply "No," which Elle compares to Dwight Schrute's blunt "False." The command to remove his sandal deliberately echoes Moses at the burning bush[^5]. What do you notice about the question Joshua asks, and about what he is told to do instead of getting an answer?

*Read Joshua 6:1–5 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty calls these instructions "almost game-show-esque," and no general would accept them as military strategy. The hosts hear Jubilee everywhere: priests, the ark, and the shofar, the horn blown to announce the Jubilee, which meant freeing slaves, rest for the land, and returning land to its families[^6]. What details in this passage sound more like worship or festival than like a battle plan? What might it mean that Israel's first act in the land is framed this way?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Elle's point is that "God is for God," and for love, freedom, justice and mercy; Marty adds that God is on our side because we are human, "not because we're the right human beings." The notes warn that some voices use the conquest to justify a "God's on our side, destroy whoever" posture toward culture and politics[^7]. Where are you most tempted to assume God is on your team? What would change in how you treat the people on the other side if you started from the commander's "No"?

</div>

### Devoted to God <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#devoted-to-god}

*The heart of the episode is one Hebrew word, and how we translate it changes the whole story.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Joshua 6:16–19 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Elle's concordance study finds *ḥerem* used for fields given to priests (Leviticus 27), for priestly items and garments, and for firstfruits, none of which are destroyed. She defines it as "devoted, set apart, enclosed, or off-limits for any other use" because it belongs to God, much like the Arabic *haram*, "forbidden"[^8]. Read the passage a second time, substituting "devoted" or "set apart" wherever your translation speaks of things "destroyed" or "devoted to destruction." What changes in a line like "Guard yourselves from the *ḥerem*, lest you become *ḥerem*"? How do verses 18–19, about the silver and gold going to the LORD's storehouse, fit each reading?

*Read Joshua 6:20–24 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Elle reads verses 21 and 24 ("they devoted everything, man and woman, young and old") as consecration to God, not slaughter, and suggests the burning may have been about removing fortifications that could later be used to oppress others. She notes that Joshua uses a different word for "destroy" later, in Joshua 11. Brent points out that some translations render *ḥerem* "devoted" and then add footnotes that define it as destruction; in his words, they "shoot themselves in the foot with their footnotes"[^9]. This is the most debated part of Elle's argument. What do you find persuasive in it, and what questions or doubts remain for you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** Achan takes from what was devoted to God, reasoning, in the notes' words, "We're victorious, God made us victorious. I'll honor God by keeping a souvenir." Elle's thesis is that much of the darkness in Joshua comes from Israel adding its own methods to what God actually said. She applies it to us: we take a good gift such as Sabbath, work or ministry and "add our own sparkles," like the spouse who rests on Sabbath while the other handles the kids, or the volunteer whose family hears mostly criticism because "the work is so important"[^10]. Which good gift from God are you most at risk of pursuing your own way, at someone else's expense? Who around you would notice first?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Elle asks us to lean in before reaching for mystery. In Exodus 23, God promises liberation: God sends the dread, the confusion and the hornets, and God drives the nations out, the way Adam and Eve were sent out of Eden. At Jericho the commander of the LORD's army refuses to take sides, and the city falls to shofars and priests rather than strategy. If *ḥerem* means "devoted to God," then Achan's sin and the defeat at Ai become warnings about taking what belongs to God and pursuing it our own way. The question we carry is whether the gifts God has given us are bearing fruit that blesses everyone around us.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: name one gift from God you want to walk out God's way this week.

</div>
