### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Laodicea's "lukewarm" water was about being useless rather than useful, not about sitting on the fence. And the Jesus who knocks at the door is not a Roman soldier demanding a meal but a guest who makes no demands and offers to dine with us as equals.

**This week:** The seven letters are finished, and John is taken up into the throne room. Nearly everything he sees there, from the twenty-four elders to the song and the sealed scroll, answers the ceremony that surrounded Caesar's arrival in his capital cities.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode reads Revelation 4–5 against the arrival, or *parousia*, of Emperor Domitian in Ephesus, which the notes describe with soldiers in shining armor, medallions proclaiming the emperor "Lord and God," twenty-four high priests in white robes and golden crowns, imperial scrolls and songs of praise.[^1] John's vision takes each element and gives it back to the true King. The hosts sum up the message this way: "I've been to the throne room of God and Domitian isn't on it."[^2]

The second half turns to Exodus. The thunder and lightning of Sinai, the slain Passover lamb, and the calling to be "a kingdom and priests" tell a persecuted church that the God who delivered Israel from Egypt will deliver them from Rome.[^3] The episode also reflects on why modern Western readers find apocalyptic literature so hard to understand: most of us have not lived under the oppression that gave it birth.[^4]

**Opening Question:** Think of a large public arrival or ceremony you have seen in person or on screen: a parade, an inauguration, a homecoming, a royal event. What details told you who was being honored and how the crowd was supposed to respond?

### The Throne Room and Caesar's Arrival <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-throne-room-and-caesars-arrival}

*John's first readers had seen, or heard all about, the emperor's grand entrance, and the throne room vision would have looked strangely familiar.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Revelation 4:1–11 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes describe what Domitian's arrival in Ephesus would have involved: soldiers wearing medallions reading "Emperor Domitian Flavius, Lord and God," new statues and emblems throughout the city, the twenty-four high priests of Rome's twenty-four legal religions assembled in white robes and golden crowns, proclamations read from scrolls, and the crowd led in songs declaring Caesar's greatness.[^5] Domitian favored the ox and the lion as his symbols, and the gymnasium gate in Ephesus was decorated with ox heads about every ten feet.[^6] Go back through chapter 4 and list everything that surrounds the throne. Which details would a believer in Ephesus have recognized from the emperor's parade?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** According to the notes, one of the most common songs sung to the emperor went: "Holy, holy, holy, Lord God Almighty, who was, and who is, and who is to come." It was sung about Caesar at the Olympic games and imperial arrivals.[^7] The same words echo Isaiah's throne room vision, and the notes connect the winged creatures circling the throne to Isaiah 6.[^8] Read Isaiah 6:1–4. What do you think it meant for these believers to hear their neighbors' hymn to Caesar sung instead to the one on the heavenly throne?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes call this reclaiming of the emperor's song "a radical act of resistance and reorientation."[^9] Think about the words we sing or say when we worship. Which of them, if you took them seriously, would quietly say "and not Caesar" about something in your own world?

</div>

### The Lamb Who Is Worthy <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-lamb-who-is-worthy}

*A sealed scroll waits for someone worthy to open it, and the one who steps forward is not what anyone expected.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Revelation 5:1–14 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** In the synagogue at Sardis, archaeologists found a plaque above the Moses seat that read: "Only he who is worthy, take, open, read." It referred to the Torah scrolls, which only someone walking in faithful righteousness could open.[^10] In verse 5 John hears that "the Lion of the tribe of Judah, the Root of David" has triumphed, and in verse 6 he turns and sees a Lamb that looks as if it had been slain. What do you notice about the gap between what John hears and what he sees? What might that switch be saying?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes call "Lion of the tribe of Judah" and "Root of David" *remez*, hints that point back to whole Old Testament passages, and suggest Genesis 49:9–10 and Isaiah 11:1, 10 as the places to look.[^11] The hosts deliberately leave much of this tracing to listeners, and Brent encourages doing it in community, because different people find different connections.[^12] Split into two groups. One reads Genesis 49:9–10, the other Isaiah 11:1–10, then report back: what does the surrounding passage add to the title, and why might it have mattered to people under Rome?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes trace Exodus through chapter 5: thunder and lightning from Sinai, the slain lamb that had become for Jewish people "the symbol of revolution" calling them back to deliverance from Egypt, the song of Moses, and the calling to be "a kingdom and priests" from Exodus 19:6.[^13] Read Exodus 19:4–6. The notes say a first-century Jewish reader "would read themselves into the story," so that reading Exodus meant being in the Exodus.[^14] How might reading yourself into the Exodus story have changed how a persecuted believer faced the next week?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes contrast Caesar's large scrolls listing his own greatness and achievements with God's scroll of redemption, opened by a Lamb who was slain.[^15] Where are you tempted to measure worth by a scroll of achievements, and what would it look like to let the slain Lamb define what "worthy" means?

</div>

### What Claims the Throne Now <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#what-claims-the-throne-now}

*The episode ends by turning the vision toward our own world and asking what plays the role of Caesar today.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Revelation 5:11–14 aloud again.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The hosts say apocalyptic literature is hard for modern Western readers because we have not experienced the oppression and persecution that form its context. We read it to predict the future, while the first audience read it looking for hope in present suffering.[^16] Apocalyptic literature says, in the notes' words, "We've been here before... We know God delivers his people from empire."[^17] How might reading from a place of relative comfort change what we hear, or fail to hear, in a passage like Revelation 5?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty's prayer at the end of the episode is: "May this same reminder remain true to us in a world that claims so many things to have power and sway over our present and our future."[^18] The notes ask what promises us security but requires that we compromise our faithfulness.[^19] Finish this sentence for yourself: "I've been to the throne room of God, and ______ isn't on it." What would change this week if you believed it?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> John's first readers lived inside Caesar's ceremony: the priests in white and gold, the ox heads on the gates, the medallions calling Domitian "Lord and God," the song of "Holy, holy, holy" sung to a man. John takes them into the true throne room and shows them every piece of that parade given back to its rightful King. The one worthy to open the scroll is the Lion of Judah, who appears as a slain Lamb, and the Exodus story becomes their story again. The God who delivered his people from Egypt has not left them to Rome, and Domitian is not on the throne.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one thing you want to remember is not on the throne this week?

</div>
