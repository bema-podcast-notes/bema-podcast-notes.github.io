### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We read Christ's return through the Roman *parousia* and heard Paul call the Thessalonians to "physical participation" in rebuilding rather than "disembodied evacuation."[^1]

**This week:** The second letter to Thessalonica reassures a church that fears it has missed the coming age, and it describes a "man of lawlessness" whom the first readers would have recognized in their own world.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode challenges the popular reading of the "man of lawlessness" as a specific future Antichrist. It argues that the passage describes a type of person who opposes God, and that its description of someone who claims divine status in God's temple would immediately have brought Caesar to mind for the original audience.[^2] It introduces the Jewish framework of two ages, *olam hazeh* ("this present age") and *olam haba* ("the age to come"), and the idea that the two overlap now that the Messiah has come.[^3]

Marty also shares honest concerns about whether Paul wrote 2 Thessalonians, while affirming its place and authority in the canon.[^4] End-times teaching differs widely across our traditions and even within them, so this guide asks us to compare what we have received with what the episode proposes and to listen well, not to settle every question. The episode is short, so we will spend more time reading the text closely.

### Reading the Description <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#reading-the-description}

*We start by listening to how the passage describes this figure before deciding who it is.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read 2 Thessalonians 2:1–4 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Make a list of everything this figure does in verses 3–4. What kind of language is this: a description of a character, or a prediction of an event? The notes say the Greek uses "descriptive, qualitative language rather than language pointing to a specific individual," characterizing people who "oppose and exalt themselves over everything that is called God" and "set themselves up in God's temple, proclaiming themselves to be God."[^5] What in the wording do you notice that supports or complicates that reading?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes say the description "perfectly matches Roman imperial ideology where Caesars claimed divine status, demanded worship, and positioned themselves as ultimate authorities," and that Christian communities "faced constant pressure to participate in imperial cult activities."[^6] Read verse 4 again, imagining you are a Thessalonian believer under that pressure. How does the passage sound different when you hear it as a description of the world you are living in?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode compares this to "the cultural critique found in Romans 1."[^7] Where else in Scripture have you noticed a ruler or a culture described as putting itself in the place of God? What does that pattern teach?

</div>

### Two Ages and the Overlap <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#two-ages-and-the-overlap}

*The episode gives a Jewish framework for hearing "the last days" and "the coming age."*
{: .guide-note}

*Read 2 Thessalonians 2:5–12 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Find the word "already" in verse 7. The notes stress that "the secret power of lawlessness is already at work," indicating "present realities of living under Roman imperial ideology."[^8] They explain the Jewish idea of *olam hazeh* (this present age) and *olam haba* (the age to come), and contrast a "two-part" view (this broken age, then a perfect future age) with a "three-part" view in which there is an overlap: "The Messiah has come, inaugurating the age to come, but the old age continues its dying process."[^9] How might the three-part view help explain how Paul can speak of the age to come and the power of lawlessness both being present at once?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes argue that New Testament "last days" language refers to the era the first readers were living in, and warn against "pushing every challenging passage into the distant future while missing its immediate relevance."[^10] Each of our traditions has its own way of talking about the end times, and some of us have changed our views over the years. What has your tradition emphasized about the "last days," what was it protecting or hoping for, and how does the overlap framework sit alongside it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> *Read 2 Thessalonians 2:1–2 and 3:17 aloud.* Marty finds the Greek "distinctly un-Pauline," notes that the letter spends unusual energy convincing readers of its Pauline authorship, and still maintains its canonical authority. The notes describe this as "intellectual honesty combined with faithful commitment to Scripture" and raise "the difference between critical scholarship and destructive skepticism."[^11] How do you hold honest questions about a biblical book alongside trust in it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** If we are living in the overlap, the old age dying and the new age already begun, where do you feel both ages at once in your own life right now?

</div>

### Standing Firm Under Pressure <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#standing-firm-under-pressure}

*The letter's purpose is not speculation but encouragement.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read 2 Thessalonians 2:13–17 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** What does this passage say God has already done for the Thessalonians, and what does it ask them to do? The notes summarize the letter's message: it "reassures believers that they haven't missed the coming age and encourages them to remain faithful amid imperial pressure."[^12] Why would people who feared they had missed God's work need both of those things?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes apply the passage this way: "Instead of speculating about future individuals, believers should recognize 'lawless' characteristics in current leaders and systems," and they warn against "any human leader or system that claims ultimate authority or demands allegiance that belongs only to God."[^13] Why might it be easier to speculate about a distant figure than to notice those characteristics in the systems around us, including ones we like or benefit from?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The early church asked, "Who will win the epic struggle between God's kingdom and Rome's empire?"[^14] Where is your own allegiance being pulled this season (by a leader, a cause, a market, a screen), and what would it look like for you to "stand firm" there this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The Thessalonians were afraid they had missed the day of the Lord. The letter tells them they have not, and it describes a figure they already knew too well: one who exalts himself over everything called God and sits in God's temple claiming to be God. Read that way, the passage is less a timetable for the future than a description of the empire they were living under. The power of lawlessness was already at work, but so was the age to come. The call is to recognize what claims our allegiance and to stand firm.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one place you will choose to give your allegiance to God's kingdom this week?

</div>
