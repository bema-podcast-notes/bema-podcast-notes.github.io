### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** In 1 John, love is not truth's rival or equal partner; it is what makes something true. No one can love the God they cannot see while refusing to love the brother or sister they can see.

**This week:** John's second letter, only thirteen verses long, carries the same theme to a "chosen lady" and her children. The episode is short, so we will read the whole letter closely together.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode works verse by verse through 2 John, starting with the question of who the "chosen lady" is. The hosts lay out three options: a real woman and her children, a church described in feminine language, or both at once, perhaps a woman who led a house church. They note that 2 John may be the only New Testament letter addressed directly to a woman, and that the Greek shifts from feminine to masculine forms as the letter goes on.

The second half follows John's repeated pairing of truth and love: walking in truth means walking in love, and the command "from the beginning" is to love one another. John also warns against deceivers who deny that Jesus came in the flesh and tells the community not to welcome them into the house. The episode ends with a personal challenge: how would John describe your life, or your church, if he wrote you a letter? Since our traditions understand women's roles differently, the question of who the lady is may be one we hold together with care.

### Who Is the Chosen Lady? <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#who-is-the-chosen-lady}

*The letter's first line raises a question readers have never fully settled.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read 2 John 1–3 and 12–13 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** John addresses "the lady chosen by God and to her children," and closes with greetings from "the children of your sister who was chosen by God."[^1] The notes add that the Greek grammar shifts from feminine (addressing "the lady") to masculine when addressing recipients throughout the letter.[^2] What do you notice about how John opens and closes the letter? What clues would make you think he is writing to one person, and what clues point to a community?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode explains that churches are often pictured as brides, and that a major city with surrounding villages was described as a mother with daughters. When Jesus says "daughters of Jerusalem," the notes say, he is addressing outlying towns, not specifically women.[^3] What other places in Scripture can you think of where a community is spoken of as a woman? How does that pattern affect the way you read 2 John?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The hosts present three options: a literal woman (the straightforward reading, which "shouldn't be dismissed simply because it lacks complexity"), a church community, or both, perhaps "an actual woman who leads a house church or larger body of believers."[^4] The notes add that this possibility "challenges assumptions about women's roles in the early church."[^5] Our traditions read questions of women's leadership differently. Which option seems most persuasive to you, and why? How does your tradition's view shape what you expect to find in the text?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Whoever she was, the chosen lady's household was known for walking in the truth, and John expected the letter to be shared beyond her.[^6] Who in your life, woman or man, has walked in the truth in a way that shaped a whole household or community? What did you learn from watching them?

</div>

### Truth and Love, One Walk <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#truth-and-love-one-walk}

*John uses two words over and over, and for him they describe a single way of life.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read 2 John 4–6 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes observe that John repeats "truth" and "love" with remarkable frequency, and that for John these are "not separate categories but deeply integrated realities."[^7] Count how often each word appears in these verses. What do you notice about how John links truth, love, and obedience?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The episode connects this to John's first letter: someone who claims truth but lacks love "is actually a liar," and the command we have had "from the beginning" is to love one another. "Obedience equals love."[^8] How does it change the meaning of "obedience" if its content is love? Where have you seen obedience understood in a way that left love out?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The hosts ask whether John would rejoice that we walk in truth "because of sound doctrine, or because of demonstrated love?"[^9] When your church describes someone as solid or faithful, what does it usually mean? What would it mean if love were the first measure?

</div>

### Deceivers and the Door <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#deceivers-and-the-door}

*John's warm letter turns firm when he speaks of those who deny that Jesus came in the flesh.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read 2 John 7–11 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The episode identifies Docetists, who believed Jesus only appeared to be human, as the most likely deceivers, and lists other possibilities, including legalistic believers creating "holy huddles," the Nicolaitans, and "any group promoting a gospel that divorced belief from love."[^10] What do you notice about how John describes the deceivers and what he asks the community to do?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes explain that traveling teachers depended on believers' hospitality, so refusing to host someone was a serious boundary. They call this a model for keeping doctrinal integrity while living in love: "not all inclusivity serves the community's health."[^11] How does knowing the role of hospitality for traveling teachers change the way John's instruction sounds?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** In the same letter, John insists that love is the heart of truth and tells the community to close the door to certain teachers. How do you hold those two things together? Where is the line in your own church between a boundary that protects people and one that simply keeps out people we find uncomfortable?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q11:** Marty suggests a practical exercise: turn off the podcast, spend the rest of your commute reflecting on love, and let that shape how you respond when someone cuts you off in traffic.[^12] What is your equivalent of the commute, the ordinary place where irritation tends to win? How could you practice walking in love there this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> In thirteen verses, John writes to a chosen lady, a real woman, a church, or both, whose household is walking in the truth. For John, that phrase means walking in love, because the oldest command is to love one another. He also guards the door against teachers who deny that Jesus came in the flesh, a reminder that love is not the same as welcoming everything. The episode leaves us with a mirror: if John wrote a letter about us, what would it say?
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each: if John wrote a letter about your family or church, what is one sentence you would hope he could include?

</div>
