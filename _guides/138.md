### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Stephen died forgiving his killers, modeling a nonviolent way of exposing injustice, and the scattered believers carried the message to Samaria. An Ethiopian eunuch heard Isaiah's promise of a name better than sons and daughters.

**This week:** Two turning points. Saul meets Jesus on the road to Damascus, and Peter's vision of a sheet full of animals leads him into the home of a Roman centurion.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode asks whether the familiar word "conversion" fits Saul's experience. Marty argues that Saul, a student of Gamaliel and a Jew with impeccable credentials, did not switch religions; he followed the same God with a new understanding. (Episode 142 will return to this question.) The episode stresses how thoroughly Jewish the movement still is in Acts 9–11, which is exactly why the next story is such a shock.

Peter's vision of unclean animals, the episode argues, was about people, not food. Peter himself says so. Marty distinguishes the written Torah from oral law (*Halakhah*), the interpretations that grew up around it, and suggests that the barrier Peter had to cross came from tradition about associating with Gentiles. The notes then turn the lens on Christians, who have their own unwritten rules, and name the question that will shape the rest of the New Testament: should Gentiles follow Torah, and if so, how?

### Switching Teams? <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#switching-teams}

*The episode questions whether "conversion" is the right word for what happened to Saul.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Acts 9:1–6 and Acts 22:3 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Notice whom Jesus says Saul is persecuting, and how Paul later describes his own training. The notes explain that Gamaliel the Elder was the grandson of Hillel, "the rabbi Jesus most often aligned with," which makes Saul's background "more compatible with Jesus's teachings than typically assumed."[^1] What do you notice in these verses about who Saul was before Damascus?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Saul carried letters from the high priest to arrest followers of Jesus in the synagogues of Damascus. The notes see this as evidence "that early Christianity operated within Jewish institutional structures," and stress that every early believer, leader and conflict up to this point was an internal Jewish matter.[^2] How does reading Acts 9 as an argument inside one family change the way the story sounds?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Marty puts the question this way: "Did he switch teams, or did Saul/Paul end up following the same God, with the same faith but a new understanding?" The notes suggest Saul's zeal "was misdirected, not fundamentally wrong."[^3] What difference does it make whether we call this a conversion or a repentance? What does each word imply about Judaism and about the God Saul had been serving?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Think about your own story of faith. Where has God corrected the direction of your zeal rather than replaced it? What did you keep, and what did you have to let go?

</div>

### A Sheet Full of Animals <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#a-sheet-full-of-animals}

*Peter's vision prepares him for a door he has never walked through.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Acts 10:9–16, then Acts 10:27–29 and 10:34–35, aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Notice what Peter is shown, how he responds, and how he himself explains the vision once he arrives at Cornelius's house. The episode argues that "Peter's vision was about people, not food," and that the common reading that God declared all foods clean "ignores Peter's own interpretation of his vision and the textual context."[^4] What, in Peter's own words, did God show him?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Cornelius lived in Caesarea Maritima, which Herod the Great built to honor Caesar, "representing Roman values rather than Jewish ones." Cornelius was already a God-fearer, a Gentile drawn to Israel's God.[^5]

*Read Acts 10:44–48 and Acts 11:1–3, 18 aloud.*

What do you notice about the reaction of the Jewish believers, both at Cornelius's house and back in Jerusalem? What does their astonishment tell you about the movement up to this point?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The episode distinguishes the written Torah, including its dietary laws, from oral Torah (*Halakhah*, the body of interpretation and application that grew up around it). It suggests that Peter's struggle involved "oral law restrictions on Gentile association, not biblical food laws," and that Jesus "often upheld written Torah while challenging problematic oral traditions."[^6] Every faith community develops interpretations to help it live faithfully, so this is not a criticism of Judaism as such. How does this distinction change the way you read Peter's vision? Where have you seen a practice meant to protect faithfulness end up keeping people apart?

</div>

### Our Own Unwritten Rules <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#our-own-unwritten-rules}

*The question Peter faced becomes the question of the whole New Testament.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say the New Testament addresses one central question: "Should Gentiles follow Torah, and if so, how?"[^7] We will meet this question again in Galatians and Romans. What do you expect it to change about how you read Paul's letters?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes observe that modern Christians "also have 'oral laws' - unwritten interpretations and practices that sometimes overshadow biblical commands," and that church traditions "can create unnecessary barriers to including different cultural groups."[^8] Every tradition in this room has its own. Name one from your own tradition (not someone else's) that could make it harder for an outsider to belong. What would it look like to hold that practice in a way that opens a door instead?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Saul did not stop serving Israel's God on the Damascus road; his zeal was redirected by the Jesus he had been persecuting. Peter's vision was about people, and he said so himself when he walked into a Gentile home in a Roman city. The astonishment of the Jewish believers shows how Jewish the movement had been until then. The barriers God asked Peter to cross came from tradition, and we are invited to notice our own.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each: whose table might God be asking you to sit at?

</div>
