### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** God pursued Pharaoh's heart through the plagues until Pharaoh saw clearly and chose to resist. Then God invited Israel to become his *bechor* (firstborn), born again through a bloody doorway to be the message, not just carry it.[^1]

**This week:** Israel crosses the sea and begins a forty-day journey to Sinai, where God will test them three times. The first test, at the bitter waters of Marah, is a test of the heart.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode opens with a review of empire and *shalom*: if empire is about fear, *shalom* is about trust; if empire is about coercion, *shalom* is about invitation.[^3] Then Israel heads south toward the sea, apparently lost, and Pharaoh gives chase. On the far shore the people call on the name of the Lord together for the first time. Marty draws on a Jewish teaching he calls the "threefold coming of the kingdom": the finger of God works, people call him Lord, and people respond in obedience.[^4]

The heart of the episode is the idea of testing. In Hebrew thought, God's tests are not pass or fail. They are a way for God to *yada* (know intimately, by experience) what is in his people's hearts, and a chance for them to learn something from him. At Marah the water is bitter, a word that also means "rebellious," and a midrash says the "law and statute" God gave there was that the weak and marginalized drink first. Just around the corner wait twelve springs and seventy palm trees.[^5] Some of this touches how our traditions talk about faith and obedience, and how we read the details of the sea crossing. The questions invite description and curiosity rather than debate.

**Opening Question:** Think of a test you once took (a school exam, a job review, a driving test). How did being tested feel, and what did you assume the person testing you wanted?

### Through the Sea <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#through-the-sea}

*Israel's rescue begins with what looks like a wrong turn.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Exodus 14:1–14 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes explain that God has Israel camp between Migdol and the sea so that Pharaoh will think they are wandering around in the desert. When Pharaoh's army appears, the people turn on Moses and ask why he brought them out to the desert to die instead of leaving them to serve the Egyptians. Moses tells them to stand firm and see God deliver them.[^6] What do you notice about the people's fear, about what they remember of Egypt, and about how Moses answers them?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say "Red Sea" is better translated "Sea of Reeds" (*Yam Suph*). They add that there are about nineteen respected opinions on the location of Sinai and about as many on the crossing, and BEMA will not try to decide. The 2024 notes also observe that Exodus 14 tells the story in more historical language (an east wind, Moses' staff) while the song in Exodus 15 uses poetic language (God's nostrils, his right hand).[^7] Glance at Exodus 15:6–10. How does it help to have the same event told in two different registers, and how do you usually hold questions about where and how it happened?

*Read Exodus 14:15–22 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes describe the "threefold coming of the kingdom." The finger of God works (after one plague the magicians told Pharaoh this was "the finger of God," and it moves again on the sea's western beach). The people call him Lord (on the eastern beach, the first time they do so as a people). The people respond in obedience (at Sinai). The sages argued that calling on the Lord is not enough without obedience, and Marty believes Jesus taught the same idea when he spoke of casting out demons "by the finger of G-d" and of those who say "Lord Lord." The notes observe that some people live righteously without calling God Lord, and many call him Lord without obeying.[^8] Our traditions place different weight on faith, confession and obedience. Which of the three ingredients comes most naturally to you or your tradition, and which tends to go missing?

</div>

### What Kind of Test? <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#what-kind-of-test}

*A Hebrew test is less an exam than an invitation to be known.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes explain that the Hebrew idea of testing (*nasah*) is not pass or fail. The 2024 notes compare it to testing strips in a hot tub, which measure where things are in order to know what is needed next rather than to give a grade. God tests his people to *yada* what is in their hearts, an intimate, experiential knowing rather than a cerebral one.[^9] Compare this with the test you described in the opening. What changes when the one testing you wants to know you rather than grade you?

*Read Deuteronomy 8:1–5 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The rabbis go back to Deuteronomy 8:1–5 to show that Marah was a test of the heart, asking whether the people would willingly wait on every word from God. The notes say a test carries two gifts: one we give to God, showing him what is in our hearts and our willingness to obey ("Not to be perfect but he gets to experience where you are in your growth"), and one God gives to us, a new lesson.[^10] What do you notice in this passage about what God was looking for in the desert, and about the comparison to a father and son?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes explain that when Hebrew says "heart" (*levav*), it means the will. Worship comes from the will, so to the Jewish mind worship and obedience are connected: willingly obeying God is itself an act of worship, because you are submitting your will to his desire.[^11] If worship lives in the will, where in your week might your most real worship be happening, and where might it be missing?

</div>

### Bitter Water and Twelve Springs <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#bitter-water-and-twelve-springs}

*The first test on the road to Sinai turns out to be about community.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Exodus 15:22–27 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes point out that Moses had spent forty years as a shepherd in this region and likely led the people to this well expecting good water. When it was bitter, the place was named Marah, but the rabbis hear more than bitterness: to be *marah* is to be defiantly, stubbornly rebellious. Moses throws in a piece of wood (not his staff), the water becomes drinkable, and God gives "a law and a ruling." The rabbis then ask what that law and statute actually were, since what follows does not really read like one.[^12] What do you notice in these verses, and what seems to be missing from verse 25?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The 2024 notes say that visitors to the Sinai wilderness come away with more sympathy for the Israelites' complaints, since the heat off the canyon walls can feel like "baking in an oven." The notes encourage us to approach these characters with humility rather than condescension.[^13] How does that change the way you hear the people's grumbling, and what complaints of your own might deserve the same humility?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The midrash says the law and statute given at Marah was this: when they went to drink from the well, they had to let the lame, the elderly, the sick and the marginalized go first. The midrash teaches that every test in the desert is meant to teach some aspect of community, how obedience shapes a people's life together. Right around the corner were twelve springs, one for each tribe, and seventy palm trees, another communal number. Had they waited on God's word, there was enough for everyone.[^14] Who in your community most needs to "drink first," and what would letting them go first actually cost you this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Israel walks out of Egypt into what looks like a dead end, and God makes a way through the sea. On the far shore they call him Lord as a people, but the kingdom will not fully come until they also respond in obedience. So God begins to test them, not to grade them but to know their hearts and teach them. At Marah the first lesson is about the will and about community: let the weak drink first, and trust that there is enough. Twelve springs and seventy palm trees were waiting just around the corner.[^15]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one way you could let someone else "drink first" before we meet again?

</div>
