### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Habakkuk argued with God from his watchtower, saw Babylon's sins mirrored in Judah's, and chose to rejoice in God even with every field empty.[^1]

**This week:** Obadiah, the shortest prophetic book, speaks to Edom, Judah's brother nation, about what it did and failed to do on the day of Judah's disaster.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Obadiah stands out among the prophets because it is addressed to a nation outside Israel's covenant: Edom, descended from Esau, the twin brother of Jacob. The episode explains that Edom sat on the southern spice trade route, grew wealthy, and its descendants (later known as the Idumeans and associated with the Nabataeans) carved remarkable cities into rock faces, Petra being the most famous. Herod the Great came from these Idumean roots.[^3]

The heart of the book is a progression of betrayal during the Babylonian invasion, from standing aloof while strangers carried off Judah's wealth to handing survivors over to the enemy. The episode draws out a universal expectation: God holds all people, not only covenant Israel, accountable for how they treat others, especially the vulnerable and those in crisis. The book ends with hope for Jacob.[^4] The questions ask us to look first at the text and then, honestly, at ourselves.

### Nests Among the Stars <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#nests-among-the-stars}

*Edom trusted its rock walls more than anything else.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Obadiah 1–6 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes say the rock fortresses gave the Edomites a sense of invulnerability, and that God declares "no matter how high they build or how secure they feel, He can bring them down." They also note the image of grape pickers and thieves, who normally leave something behind, as hyperbole for how complete Edom's fall will be.[^5] What words in these verses describe Edom's sense of security, and what does God say about it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode describes Petra in modern-day Jordan, with facades carved into rock (one made famous by *Indiana Jones and the Last Crusade*) and narrow passages so defensible that even Rome struggled to conquer the Nabataeans, partly because of the difficulty of getting water to besieging armies in the desert.[^6] If you have seen pictures of Petra, what about the place helps you understand Edom's confidence?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes apply Edom's false security to modern people, who "place trust in financial security, political power, military might, or social status."[^7] Which of these feels most like a rock fortress to you personally, and what would it look like to hold it more loosely?

</div>

### From Standing Aloof to Handing Over <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#from-standing-aloof-to-handing-over}

*The sins of Edom move step by step from doing nothing to doing harm.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Obadiah 10–14 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes trace seven steps: standing aloof, gloating, boasting, marching through the gates, seizing wealth, waiting at the crossroads to cut down fugitives, and handing over survivors.[^8] Find each step in the text. What do you notice about how the passage moves, and about how often the phrase "in the day of" appears?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes describe the scene as Judah fleeing Babylon and running to its brother nation for help, only to be turned away, or worse, pointed out to their pursuers. They say "standing aloof and doing nothing while others suffer is itself a condemnation, but taking advantage of suffering people is even worse."[^9] Why might God count doing nothing as a real wrong, and not merely a missed opportunity?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode stresses that Edom and Judah were brothers, both children of Abraham, so their relationship was "more than merely political—it was covenantal and familial." The notes raise the question: "How do we treat family members in crisis?"[^10] Why does betrayal by family cut deeper than harm from a stranger?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes apply "standing aloof" to modern indifference: ignoring homelessness, turning away from refugee crises, failing to speak up for the marginalized. They also name those who exploit disasters for gain, like price gouging or predatory lending after natural disasters.[^11] We may disagree on how to address these issues, so focus on the personal. Where are you most tempted to stand aloof right now, and what is one small step from watching to helping?

</div>

### Every Nation Accountable <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#every-nation-accountable}

*God expects basic decency from everyone, not only from his covenant people.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Obadiah 15–21 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes observe that "the day of the Lord" appears here, that Edom faces destruction with "no survivors," and that Jacob will experience deliverance and "possess his inheritance."[^12] What do you notice about the contrast between Edom's end and Jacob's in these verses, and whose kingdom it is in the very last line?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes call the grape-picker image in verse 5 hyperbolic language that "emphasizes the severity of God's judgment," and they describe the judgment on Edom as "severe and final."[^13] How do you hold language this harsh, and what helps you remember the situation of the people who first heard it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes say Obadiah establishes that "moral accountability exists for all people, not just religious believers," and that God "cares deeply about people and becomes furious when the powerful take advantage of those in need."[^14] How does it change your view of neighbors outside your faith community to know God expects decency from them, and of yourself, to know he expects at least as much from you?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Obadiah is only twenty-one verses long, but it lands hard. Edom, Judah's own brother, watched from its rock fortress as Babylon carried Judah away, then gloated, looted, and handed fugitives over to the enemy. God's verdict is that no height can protect pride, and that standing aloof is already a wrong. God expects every nation and every person to care for those in crisis. And even here, the last word is hope: Jacob will possess its inheritance.[^15]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, name one person or situation you will refuse to stand aloof from this week.

</div>
