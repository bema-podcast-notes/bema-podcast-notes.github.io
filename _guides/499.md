### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Marty opened the four pillars with the text. He told how it became his central spiritual practice, read the passages that convince him God's word never returns void, and urged us to make space for it while not making it an idol.

**This week:** Reed takes up the same pillar without hearing Marty's take first. He asks what "God-breathed" means, argues that the Bible conveys the meaning of human experiences with God, and shows that there is more than one kind of truth.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Reed grew up treating the Bible as a manual for getting saved and living a godly life. In this episode he begins with one Greek word in 2 Timothy 3:16, *theopneustos* ("God-breathed"), and asks whether Scripture is "breathed into" by God or "breathed out by" God. He finds truth in both and uses the creation of Adam, shaped from dirt and filled with God's breath, as a picture of a Bible that is fully human and fully divine.

From there the conversation widens. Reed, Marty, and Brent use a newspaper and a poem about 9/11, a satirical headline, a scene from *The Lord of the Rings*, and the moment Brent became a father to show that facts are not the only kind of truth. They borrow Meir Sternberg's image of three "faders" on a soundboard (history, theology, and aesthetics). Reed closes with practical habits for reading: notice familiar church words, read with Jesus as the lens, read with friends, follow rabbit trails, and judge your reading by its fruit. Some of these ideas may sit differently depending on what each of our traditions has taught about Scripture, and that difference is welcome in the room.

### Breathed Into, Breathed Out <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#breathed-into-breathed-out}

*One Greek word can be heard two ways, and each way protects something important.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read 2 Timothy 3:14–17 aloud. Then read Genesis 2:7 and Isaiah 55:10–11.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Translations render *theopneustos* in 2 Timothy 3:16 as "God-breathed," "breathed out by God," "inspired by God," or "given by inspiration of God." The word joins *theos* (God) with *pneustos* (breathed), related to *pneuma* (breath, wind, spirit). Reed links one reading, "breathed into," to Genesis 2:7, and the other, "breathed out by," to Isaiah 55:11.[^1] Looking at the three passages side by side, what does breath or word *do* in each one? Where does it come from, and where does it go?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Reed sums up the two readings in short phrases: "All Scripture is life-giving" and "All Scripture has a divine nature." Each can be pushed too far. The first can turn into picking out which parts are inspired; the second can imagine a Bible that dropped from the sky and must be flawless, so that any imperfection makes the whole thing useless.[^2] Why do you think holding both readings together guards against both extremes?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Reed's model is the creation of Adam: God shapes something material and human from the dirt and then breathes divine life into it, so the Bible is a human thing and a divine thing at once. He says the popular images of inspiration (dictation, or God using the authors like a glove) hold a kernel of truth but may go "a bridge too far." Marty adds that he holds a "source" view, yet it does not push him toward literalism: if God chose to write a poem, it is an inspired poem.[^3] Our traditions have taught different things about how the Bible came to be. What were you taught about inspiration, what do you think that teaching was trying to protect, and how does Reed's picture sit with you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** If "All Scripture is life-giving," then the breath does not stop at the page. Think of a passage that has breathed life into you at some point. What was happening in your life at the time, and what did the text do?

</div>

### Many Kinds of True <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#many-kinds-of-true}

*A newspaper and a poem can both be true without being true in the same way.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Reed compares two pieces about 9/11. The Wall Street Journal of September 12, 2001, listed flight numbers, times, and souls aboard; if it printed the wrong flight number, it would be in error. Scott Cairns's poem "September 11" opens with Exodus's pillar of cloud and fire and suggests that "we became the cloud and the fire." Calling a poem "in error" means something completely different. Reed's diagnosis: "We only have one category and we call it true/false."[^4] Brent adds his own example: his Apple Watch recorded the exact second his son was born, but he says he truly became a father when he made eye contact with him at the warming table, something "not factually true in any way" that is still the real truth.[^5] Where in your own life do you rely on kinds of truth that a fact sheet could never hold?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Meir Sternberg describes three "faders" on a soundboard: history (what happened?), ideology or theology (what does it mean?), and aesthetics (what style best communicates it?). Different passages mix them at different levels, and none is ever simply at zero.[^6] Go back to Genesis 2:7 from earlier. Where would you set each of the three faders for that verse, and why?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes name a fear underneath all this: "If the Bible fails in the factual category, we assume it cannot be true in any sense."[^7] Asked whether the serpent in Genesis actually spoke, Karl Barth reportedly answered, "Madam, it doesn't matter whether or not the serpent spoke; all that matters is what he said."[^8] What feels freeing about Barth's answer, and what feels unsettling? Where do you think that fear comes from?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Quoting Frederick Buechner, Reed says every doctrine "was a human experience first." The divinity of Christ, for example, began in the experience of ordinary people who knew Jesus of Nazareth and found something happening in their lives that had never happened before.[^9] Pick one belief that matters deeply to you. What experience, yours or someone else's, sits underneath it?

</div>

### Habits for Reading <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#habits-for-reading}

*Reed closes with practices that help us read with love rather than fear.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** Reed borrows the idea of "first-order language" from psychology: words a group finds so obviously true that nobody questions them, until they lose their meaning. He calls this "Christianese," and his examples include *faith*, *righteousness*, and "by grace you have been saved."[^10] Choose one of those words or phrases. How would you explain what you mean by it without using any other church words?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Following Greg Boyd, Reed suggests going back to the person of Jesus when a passage is hard, and asking: "How would Jesus interpret this? How would he encourage his disciples to live it out?" He also recommends reading in *havruta*, reading aloud with friends where no question is off limits and the conversation itself is the fruit.[^11] Which of these two habits do you most need, and what would it take to practice it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q11:** Reed's last test is fruit. If a way of reading produces fear and destruction rather than love, question it. When certainty conflicts with loving your neighbor or your enemy, choose love and re-examine the certainty.[^12] Looking honestly at your own way of reading Scripture, what fruit has it produced in you and in your relationships? What, if anything, might you want to re-examine?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Reed invites us to loosen our grip on a single, factual kind of truth. Scripture is God-breathed in two ways: it comes from God, and it gives life, shaped from something human and filled with divine breath. When we read it as real people conveying the meaning of their encounters with God, poetry, satire, story, and history can all carry truth. The habits Reed offers keep us honest: question familiar words, read through the lens of Jesus, read with others, and test our reading by its fruit. A way of reading that leads to love of neighbor and enemy is a way of reading in step with Jesus.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one way your reading of Scripture could become more life-giving this month?

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 500 — Passover](/guides/500)** (56:23): BEMA marks its 500th episode with Rabbi Dr. David Rudolph, a Messianic Jewish New Testament scholar, who traces how the early church dated *Pascha* (Passover), its central yearly celebration. The story moves from Gentile believers depending on the Jewish community to know when Passover fell, to the Council of Nicaea in 325 deliberately cutting the Christian calendar loose from Jewish reckoning in openly anti-Jewish language. It gives a concrete window into the "parting of the ways" that Marty described in week 3, when he said the church lost something important in splitting from Judaism.
