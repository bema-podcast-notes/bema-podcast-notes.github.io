### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We reviewed the whole biblical story as one of partnership, ending with the early church applying "text to context," so we could carry it into Session 5's question: "Where are we today and how did we get here?"[^1]

**This week:** Before the history begins, Marty explains how he will tell it: honestly, selectively, and always for the sake of reading the text better today.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Marty opens Session 5 with three disclaimers: he is not a professional historian, all historians tell a story with bias, and the session will not be a comprehensive church history.[^3] He points out that Jewish tradition places books like Samuel and Kings among the Prophets, which suggests that telling history is itself a prophetic act.[^4] He also introduces a discipleship framework from Bill Westfall: Message, Mode and Milieu.[^5]

The episode then turns to eschatology. Jesus taught an overlap between "this age" and "the age to come," and 2 Peter 3:11–13 speaks of believers speeding the coming of the day of God. Marty proposes that the date of Christ's return may not be fixed, which raises the question that drives the whole session: if Christ seemed "right at the doorstep" in the first centuries, what happened?[^6]

### Three Disclaimers: History as Storytelling <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#three-disclaimers-history-as-storytelling}

*Marty begins by naming his limits and his bias out loud, before he tells a single story.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Marty gives three disclaimers, which the notes summarize as "He is not a professional historian," "All historians tell a story with bias," and "The goal is not comprehensive church history."[^3] Which of the three is most reassuring to you, and which one makes you want to listen more carefully?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes explain that in the Hebrew Bible, Samuel and Kings sit in the Prophets rather than the Writings, which suggests historians are "not merely recording facts but acting as mouthpieces for God's message."[^4] What happens to the way you read a history book (or a history lesson in church) if you think of it as a kind of prophecy?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Marty says he will "intentionally select the parts of history he believes tell the story in the most useful manner for contemporary culture," and that this bias "should be acknowledged rather than hidden."[^3] What are the strengths and the risks of a teacher who openly chooses his story this way? How might we, as listeners, stay both open and discerning over the next twelve weeks?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes warn against Christians getting "lost" in a favorite period of church history (the Reformation, the early church fathers, the Anabaptists) the way people get lost in politics or end-times speculation.[^7] Every tradition in our room has a story it tells about where it came from. What story about church history did you grow up hearing, and what did that story help you believe about yourself?

</div>

### Message, Mode, and Milieu <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#message-mode-and-milieu}

*Bill Westfall's framework describes discipleship as understanding the story, engaging the whole self, and living among a diverse people.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** In the framework, Message is understanding the whole story of God and the invitation to join it, Mode is engaging body, heart and mind together, and Milieu is an intentional network of diverse relationships.[^5] Which of the three has been strongest in your own formation so far, and which has been thinnest?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty uses the Israel trip to illustrate Mode: walking the land (body), being moved (heart), and learning facts (mind), all at the same time.[^8] Where have you learned something about God with all three engaged at once, and what made it stick?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Milieu means relationships "across different brands, tribes, denominations, age demographics, and backgrounds."[^5] Our group already holds several traditions. What could this room help you see over the coming weeks that your own tradition alone might not, and what would it take for you to really listen?

</div>

### Speeding the Coming <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#speeding-the-coming}

*Peter's letter suggests that the way believers live is connected to the day God is bringing.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read 2 Peter 3:11–13 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes say Peter instructs believers to "speed the coming" of the day of God through holy and godly lives.[^6] As you listen to the passage, what does Peter ask his readers to do, and what words connect their everyday living to the day that is coming? Report one phrase.

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes contrast a two-part eschatology (a hard break between "this age" and "the age to come") with Jesus's three-part view, which includes an overlap where the age to come "slowly fades in."[^9] How does the overlap idea help explain why the early Christians expected Christ's return so soon, without calling them naive?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** Marty connects this to *tikkun olam*, "repairing the world," the Jewish idea that humans partner with God in restoring creation, and proposes that our obedience may actually affect the timeline of Christ's return.[^6] What stirs in you when you hear that idea, whether hope, hesitation or something else? If ordinary obedience really matters to the story's ending, what would you do differently this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> This week we heard a teacher say plainly that he is not a historian, that every history is a story told with bias, and that the text, not history for its own sake, stays at the center. We met a way of describing discipleship as a whole story, a whole self, and a whole people. And we heard Peter suggest that the way God's people live is tied to the coming of the day of God. That leaves us holding the question Session 5 will chase: if the early church seemed so close, what happened?
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one bias you suspect you will bring to the story of church history?

</div>
