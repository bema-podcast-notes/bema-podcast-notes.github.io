### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We pulled up chairs at two prophetic tables, one Babylonian and one Persian, and heard the prophets argue about how to live faithfully under empire. The episode called Scripture a living dialogue and asked what partnership with God demands in our own context.[^1]

**This week:** Our final week of Session 2. Marty and Brent trace the whole story of the Hebrew Scriptures, from creation through exile and return, and look ahead to Session 3.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The capstone reads the Hebrew Scriptures as one coherent story about God seeking partners to restore creation, with a central tension running through it: Empire, a self-centered way that pushes others down, and Shalom, a self-sacrificial way that "puts the world back together."[^3] It retells the story from the goodness of creation and the wedding at Sinai to the kings, highlighting Solomon, who likely had good intentions but used imperial methods and became "the new Pharaoh."[^4]

The episode also names the two perspectives that tell Israel's history (Samuel and Kings, focused on idolatry and immorality, and Chronicles, focused on empire and injustice), summarizes the prophets' message as Warning, Woe and Hope, and walks through the prophets in historical order.[^5] It ends by noting that the Hebrew Scriptures close not with resolution but with tension about how to live under empire, a tension that sets the stage for Jesus.[^6] Because this is our last meeting, we will spend more time at the end looking back together and sending one another out.

### Empire and Shalom, Sinai to Solomon <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#empire-and-shalom-sinai-to-solomon}

*The capstone frames the whole story as a choice between two ways of being in the world.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Exodus 19:3–6, Deuteronomy 17:14–17 and 1 Kings 10:26–29 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes describe Sinai as a wedding where God proposes, "If you say yes, you will be for me a kingdom of priests."[^7] Earlier this session we heard that Chronicles emphasizes Solomon's violation of Deuteronomy 17's laws for kings.[^8] Compare the king Deuteronomy describes with what Solomon gathers in 1 Kings 10. What do you notice about what he accumulates and where it comes from?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes define Empire as a self-centered worldview ("I am most important") that operates from scarcity and achieves its goals by pushing others down, and Shalom as an other-centered, self-sacrificial way that operates from abundance and makes sure those around us have what they need.[^9] They say Solomon likely had good intentions but used "Imperial methods," built a kingdom that required the very oppression God rescued Israel from, and brought the story full circle back to Egypt. Their lesson: "using Empire methods ultimately recreates Empire."[^10] Why is it so easy for good intentions to reach for imperial methods, and where have you watched that happen?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes apply the two sources to our own failures. In the moment, like Samuel and Kings, we tend to see moral failures; looking back, like Chronicles, we may recognize, "We were right about the symptoms, but we missed the disease."[^11] They suggest that a church might first address sexual immorality or substance abuse and later recognize deeper patterns of empire-building or failing to see the vulnerable, and that both perspectives are valuable.[^12] This is not a verdict on any of our churches. Looking back on your own life, what has hindsight shown you that you could not see in the moment?

</div>

### Walking Back Through Session 2 <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#walking-back-through-session-2}

*Before we close, we retrace the road we have walked since Week 1.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Here is Session 2 in ten movements:

- **Joshua and Judges:** *chazak va'amatz* ("be strong and courageous"), a land placed at the "crossroads of the earth," and a "redemption cycle" that shows God's patience with a people trying to follow him.[^13]
- **Ruth:** a Moabite widow and a Boaz who leaves the corners of his field, *hesed* (covenant loyalty) flourishing in the dark days of the judges.[^14]
- **Samuel and the kings:** a donkey herder the people wanted and a shepherd God chose, with *kiddush HaShem* (hallowing God's name) marking the difference.[^15]
- **Kings and Chronicles:** one story, two sources, "agenda-driven headlines from Israel" and a "documentary perspective from Judah."[^16]
- **Elijah and Elisha:** fire on Carmel, God's "still small voice," the 7,000 who had not bowed to Baal, and Elisha's words to Naaman, "Go in peace."[^17]
- **Psalms and wisdom:** "there's a Psalm for that," including lament; proverbs as "wise sayings that are generally true"; and Ecclesiastes finding meaning in legacy and self-sacrifice.[^18]
- **Song of Songs:** *rayah* (poetic infatuation), *ahava* (committed love) and *dowd* (erotic love), meant to work together.[^19]
- **The prophets in historical order:** God's messengers to their own day, from Amos, Hosea, Micah and First Isaiah before Assyria, through Jonah and Nahum, to the voices of the Babylonian crisis and the exile: Zephaniah, Second Isaiah, Jeremiah, Lamentations, Habakkuk, Obadiah, Joel, Ezekiel, Daniel, Job and Third Isaiah.[^20]
- **Exile and return:** Fourth Isaiah's hope, Ezra and Nehemiah's passionate leadership, Haggai's call to build, Zechariah's visions and Malachi's questions.[^21]
- **Esther and the prophetic table:** God hidden in Persia, Esther casting her lot, and the prophets heard as a conversation about life under empire.[^22]

Which movement most reshaped the way you read the Bible, and which one still feels unfinished for you?

*Read Lamentations 3:31–33 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The capstone summarizes the prophets as Warning (a call to return before judgment), Woe (an announcement of judgment, "Time for repentance has passed," and deep lament) and Hope (the promise that God's people will make it through).[^23] Earlier we learned that Lamentations places these verses of hope at the very center of its structure.[^24] The notes say the prophets model "how to be truth-tellers without becoming cynics, how to grieve without despairing, and how to hope without denying reality."[^25] What do you notice about where hope sits in Lamentations, and which of the three, warning, woe or hope, have you most needed to hear this year?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say the Hebrew Scriptures end with tension rather than resolution: How should God's people respond to foreign empires? What does it mean to be God's partner under Persian, Greek or Roman rule?[^26] What unresolved question from Session 2 are you carrying with you into Session 3?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">35 min</span> {#closing}

> We began with Joshua's call to be strong and courageous at the crossroads of the earth, and watched God keep redeeming a people who kept trying. We met Ruth and Boaz, a donkey herder and a shepherd king, and two sources telling one story. We sang the Psalms, learned wisdom, heard the prophets warn, weep and hope through Assyria, Babylon and Persia, and followed the remnant home to build under an empire that never fully let go. Through it all the question was the same one Sinai asked: will God's partners choose Empire or Shalom? The Hebrew Scriptures leave that question open, and the capstone says it sets the stage for Jesus.[^27]
{: .guide-summary}

*Sit together in silence for 90 seconds before the next question.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Over these 40 weeks, how has this study changed the way you read the Bible, the way you understand it, and the way you relate to it? Take one of the three, or all of them.

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes recall Deuteronomy's call to "Remember your story": you were slaves, aliens, orphans and widows, and when you remember your past oppression, you notice current oppression.[^28] Who are the aliens, orphans and widows around you, and what is one practice of Shalom you want to carry out of Session 2?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** Going around the room, finish this sentence in one sentence each: "As we leave the Prophets and the Writings, I want to keep ____." Let everyone speak before anyone responds.

*To close, one person reads aloud: Go as God's partners, a kingdom of priests. Choose Shalom over Empire. Remember your story, and notice the alien, the orphan and the widow. Tell the truth without becoming cynical, grieve without despairing, and hope without denying reality. Keep your seat at the table, and keep wrestling together.*[^29]

</div>
