**This week:** Dr. Jennifer Rosner introduces supersessionism and the "new Jewish-Christian encounter," opening a short series on the Jewish roots of Christianity.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Dr. Jennifer Rosner is a Jewish follower of Jesus who teaches systematic theology, including at Fuller Theological Seminary and the Messianic Jewish Theological Institute. She heard the gospel for the first time in college, came to faith in Jesus, and for a time "shelved" her Jewish identity because she had no models for being both. During seminary that identity "started clamoring for attention again," and her life's work since has been exploring what it means to be both Jewish and a follower of Jesus.[^3]

The conversation centers on supersessionism (replacement theology), which the notes describe as often unconscious, and on the question Catholic theologian Bruce Marshall poses: how can Christians affirm both God's ongoing covenant with Israel and the universal saving mission of Christ? Dr. Rosner also describes two perspectives within Messianic Judaism and her hope of "unparting the ways." Marty admits he began with skepticism about Messianic Judaism and was transformed by the scholarship she introduced him to.[^4]

### A Jewish Follower of Jesus <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#a-jewish-follower-of-jesus}

*Dr. Rosner's own story shows how deeply Judaism and Christianity have been treated as mutually exclusive.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes say Dr. Rosner initially "shelved" her Jewish identity because she had no models for being both Jewish and a follower of Jesus, and that this reflects a broader historical reality: many Jewish believers in Jesus have felt forced to choose one identity or the other.[^3] What does it tell you about the history of Judaism and Christianity that she felt she had to choose? What might it have taken for a church to help her not have to?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Gerald McDermott, from Reformed circles, gave supersessionist lectures while leading a trip to Israel, until a friend kept pulling him aside at lunch: "Jerry, did you ever think about..." His thinking was revolutionized. Marty likewise began with skepticism and low expectations about Messianic Judaism and found a whole world of rigorous scholarship.[^5] When has a friend's patient question changed your mind about something important? What made you able to hear it?

</div>

### Supersessionism and the Faithfulness of God <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#supersessionism-and-the-faithfulness-of-god}

*The notes argue that replacement theology raises a question about whether God keeps his promises.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Supersessionism is the view that God's covenant through Jesus supersedes or replaces his covenant with Israel, which positions Israel as "old, outdated, done away with." It can be punitive (Israel failed, so God moved on) or developmental (Israel was just the starting point), and the notes say it is often unconscious, rarely examined.[^6] Think diagnostically about the tradition you know best. Where might this assumption have been present in songs, sermons, or everyday words like "old" and "new," without anyone intending harm?

*Read Romans 11:1–2 and Romans 11:28–29 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes name a key problem with supersessionism: "if God abandoned His covenant with Israel, can we trust His covenant commitments?"[^6] The notes do not cite a particular passage for this, so this one is offered for the group to test the question against. What does Paul say here about God's people and about God's gifts and calling? Report what you notice.

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Dr. Rosner says the "new Jewish-Christian encounter" is more than interfaith dialogue: its distinctive mark is that Jews and Christians begin to reassess their own traditions after real engagement with the other. She draws on John Howard Yoder's description of Judaism as "a non non-Christian religion," one that cannot be placed in the same category as other religions because of its inherent connection to Christianity.[^7] Why do you think Judaism cannot simply be filed alongside other world religions in Christian thinking?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Bruce Marshall asks, "How can we affirm both the ongoing significance and endurance of God's covenant with the people of Israel and the universal, ecclesially mediated saving mission of Christ?" Marshall also recognizes that affirming the covenant means affirming the Jewish people's call to Torah observance and Judaism as a set of practices: "It's not an abstract affirmation of Jewish chosenness, but a concrete acknowledgment of Jewish religious practice as part of covenant identity."[^8] Why is the concrete version harder for many Christians than the abstract one? How might holding Marshall's question change the way you speak about Jewish neighbors or Judaism this week?

</div>

### Spirit and Tradition <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#spirit-and-tradition}

*Two perspectives within Messianic Judaism raise a question many Christians also live with.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> In the book *At the Foot of the Mountain*, Joshua Lessard represents a Spirit-centered perspective and Dr. Rosner a tradition-engaged one. Lessard's example: at McDonald's, should someone be thinking about whether to order a cheeseburger (which violates kosher law) or whether God is calling them to sit next to someone and share the gospel?[^9] What do you think each perspective is trying to protect?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Dr. Rosner advocates a both/and approach: it is possible to honor both Spirit-led spontaneity and covenantal commitment to practice.[^9] Many Christian traditions lean one way, toward careful structure and pattern or toward openness to the Spirit's leading in the moment. Which way do you lean, and what would it look like this week to honor the side that comes less naturally to you?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Dr. Jennifer Rosner's story shows how long Judaism and Christianity have been treated as two separate, mutually exclusive worlds, so much so that she once set aside her own Jewish identity to follow Jesus. Supersessionism, often unnoticed, says God has moved on from Israel; the notes ask what that would mean for God's faithfulness to us. Bruce Marshall's question holds two commitments together, God's enduring covenant with Israel and the saving mission of Christ, and asks us not to drop either one. The invitation is to let real encounter reshape how we understand our own faith.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one assumption about Judaism or the Jewish people you want to examine more honestly?

</div>
