### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** 2 John, written to a "chosen lady" who may have been a woman, a church, or both, taught that walking in truth means walking in love, while still guarding the door against teachers who deny that Jesus came in the flesh.

**This week:** 3 John puts names and faces on that theme: Gaius who welcomes, Diotrephes who shuts the door, and Demetrius whom everyone speaks well of. Another short episode means more time in the letter itself.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode reads 3 John as a contrast between people. Gaius is commended for faithful love and hospitality to traveling believers; Diotrephes "loves to be first," refuses to welcome John's people, spreads malicious nonsense, and expels those who want to show hospitality; Demetrius is "well spoken of by everyone, and even by the truth itself." The hosts stress that difficult people and church conflict have been part of the church from the very beginning.

They connect the letter to the Hebrew idea of *kiddush haShem* ("sanctifying the Name"), introduced back in the episode on King David, and hear it in John's phrase "for the sake of the name." Hospitality, they argue, is what set believers apart from pagans. And once again they press John's conviction that love is what makes truth authentic: truth claimed without love is not truth at all.

### Gaius and Established Love <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#gaius-and-established-love}

*John opens by celebrating a friend whose faithfulness shows up at his front door.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read 3 John 1–8 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes say John rejoices that his "children" are "walking in the truth," which "manifests as faithfulness to love and hospitality," and that Gaius's love was "established in the church," a pattern of life rather than a one-time act.[^1] What do you notice about what Gaius is doing, and for whom? What phrases does John use to describe it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The episode explains that *kiddush haShem* means "to hallow or sanctify the name" of God. It was introduced in the episode on King David (Episode 39, Session 2), where what set David apart "was not perfection but his consistent desire to kiddush haShem." The phrase "for the sake of the name" in 3 John "directly echoes this tradition."[^2] How does hearing verse 7 through this lens change what Gaius's hospitality means? Why might hospitality, of all things, be a way to make God's name holy?

*Read Matthew 5:43–47 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say hospitality "becomes the defining characteristic that distinguishes believers from pagans," and point to Jesus' words in the Sermon on the Mount about loving only those who love you.[^3] What does Jesus say makes his followers different? How does that compare with what you have usually heard named as the mark of a Christian?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The episode asks: "Are we known primarily for what we oppose or for whom we welcome?"[^4] Gaius's love was established over time. What is one pattern of welcome (a regular meal, an open seat, a standing invitation) that you could begin to establish in your home or church?

</div>

### Diotrephes, Who Loves to Be First <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#diotrephes-who-loves-to-be-first}

*In the middle of the letter, John names someone inside the church who is working against it.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read 3 John 9–10 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes list Diotrephes's actions: refusing to welcome John and his representatives, spreading "malicious nonsense" about the apostles, refusing to welcome other believers, preventing others from showing hospitality, and expelling those who wanted to welcome travelers.[^5] Look at the passage. Which of these actions builds on the others? What do you think was driving him?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode notes that the name Diotrephes means "loved by Jupiter," the Roman equivalent of Zeus, possibly indicating "a struggle to leave behind pagan ways of thinking about power and status." John may even be using a pseudonym.[^6] What "pagan ways of thinking about power and status" are easiest to carry into church life without noticing?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes put John's framework sharply: if you claim to have truth but show no love, "you do not actually possess truth at all - you possess falsehood masquerading as truth." The hosts add that opposition to the Gospel often comes from within the church, even from people wearing "Jesus t-shirts" and singing worship songs.[^7] Every tradition in our room has its own story of conflict. How does this framework help you understand church conflicts you have seen? Where might it be misused to dismiss people we simply disagree with?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The episode says recognizing the Diotrephes pattern can help us "respond with wisdom rather than surprise or despair."[^8] Without naming anyone, where have you been tempted to love being first: in a meeting, a family, a ministry, an argument? What would choosing Gaius's way look like there?

</div>

### Demetrius and the Faithful Few <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#demetrius-and-the-faithful-few}

*John ends with a commendation and a reminder that the faithful are never as alone as they feel.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read 3 John 11–15 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes call Demetrius someone "well spoken of by everyone, and even by the truth itself," and suggest that truth itself testifies to his character, "not abstract theological correctness, but the lived reality of love and generosity."[^9] What do you notice in these verses about how John describes good and evil, and about the kind of testimony Demetrius receives?

*Read 1 Kings 19:14–18 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode connects Demetrius to Elijah's story, when God reminded him that 7,000 in Israel had not bowed to Baal: "the faithful are never as isolated as they feel." In the notes' words, "those who follow God rarely are alone."[^10] When have you felt like the only one trying to be faithful in a place? Who turned out to be standing with you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q11:** The notes observe that John prefers personal presence to written letters.[^11] Gaius had Demetrius; Elijah had seven thousand. Name one person who is a Demetrius to you, someone whose life testifies to love. How could you thank them, face to face if possible, this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> 3 John is a short letter about three people. Gaius opens his door to strangers, and in doing so hallows God's name, the old Jewish practice of *kiddush haShem* carried into the church. Diotrephes loves to be first, shuts the door, and pushes out those who would open it, showing that some of the hardest opposition comes from inside. Demetrius stands beside Gaius as a reminder that the faithful are rarely alone. For John, love is what makes truth real, and hospitality is where that love becomes visible.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each: whose name, or what name, could you hallow this week by welcoming someone?

</div>
