### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Isaiah's maternal images showed a God whose *racham* (womb-like compassion) nurses, carries, and plays with us, and invited us to be comforted by God rather than only to think about God.[^1]

**This week:** Our last week in Isaiah. Chapters 56–66 picture a "new world of hope" that renews what God wanted from the beginning, and Marty closes with a review of the whole book.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Isaiah's closing chapters are prophetic visions for a people coming out of exile. Marty and Brent show that this world is not really new: it renews God's intention from Eden onward, with justice, righteousness, a welcome for outsiders, and shalom. These chapters are not all comfort, though. They also warn about idolatry, empty religious ritual, and the failures of Israel's own leaders. The hosts' main text is Isaiah 58's picture of "true fasting," and they move on to guilt and shame in Isaiah 64 and the vision of a new heavens and a new earth.[^3]

Brent suggests a mirror-image (chiastic) structure for these chapters with Isaiah 61 at the center, and Marty reviews all of Isaiah in two parts: "As a Father Disciplines His Son" and "Partnering with Dad." Tonight is a chance to look back over our weeks in Isaiah as well as to look ahead.[^4]

### A House of Prayer for All Nations <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#a-house-of-prayer-for-all-nations}

*The new world of hope moves from exclusion to inclusion.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Deuteronomy 23:1 aloud. Then read Isaiah 56:1–8 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Deuteronomy 23:1 barred eunuchs from the assembly. Isaiah promises them "a memorial and a name better than sons and daughters" within God's temple, and says God's house "will be called a house of prayer for all nations," a line Jesus later quotes when he cleanses the temple. The notes say the vision "moves from *exclusion*... to *inclusion* in God's mission, covenant, and people."[^5] What do you notice when you hear these two passages side by side? How do you make sense of Scripture developing its picture of who belongs?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say "God's people are often their own worst enemy": the obstacle to hope is Israel's own "watchmen," leaders Isaiah calls blind, "mute dogs" that cannot bark, and shepherds who seek their own gain. Marty suggests Isaiah may be criticizing exiles who stayed in Babylon or Persia "because they liked the comforts and compromises of empire."[^6] Why do you think the prophets so often aim their sharpest words at God's own people rather than at outsiders?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes invite churches and communities to ask "who today plays the role of the 'eunuch' or 'foreigner': people who feel structurally excluded and say, 'I am only a dry tree.'"[^7] Every church draws lines in some way. In the communities you know, who might feel like a "dry tree"? What would welcoming them in the spirit of Isaiah 56 look like?

</div>

### The Fast God Chooses <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-fast-god-chooses}

*Isaiah 58 defines worship that pleases God in terms of justice.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 58:1–12 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The people complain, "Why have we fasted, and you have not seen it?" God answers that on the same day they fast, they exploit their workers, quarrel, and fight. The fast God chooses is to loose the chains of injustice, share food with the hungry, shelter the poor wanderer, clothe the naked, not turn away from your own flesh and blood, and do away with the pointing finger. The result: "Your light will break forth like the dawn," and the people become "Repairer of Broken Walls."[^8] What do you notice about the connection between worship and how people are treated in this passage?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Marty suggests that the people's frustration at not seeing God act "may help explain why they turned to idols that *seemed* to work." He insists that justice "as described by the Bible" is "not partisan politics but a plain reading of the text," and Brent adds that this is a *community* calling: "No single person has to carry the whole burden of feeding every hungry person." Brent also warns that everyone has their own idea of what is loving, and that Isaiah 58 gives concrete content: food, shelter, clothing, and freedom.[^9] Why might it matter that Isaiah 58 makes justice so concrete? How can a group share this calling without turning it into a guilt trip?

*Read Isaiah 58:13–14 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty summarizes Sabbath as "we rest, we play, no work, God loves us," but warns it can be twisted into self-indulgence. "Real Sabbath means taking part in *God's* delight, which echoes Eden, whose name means 'delight.'"[^10] What is the difference between a day of rest that serves only yourself and one that joins God's delight?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes suggest one test for any spiritual practice: "does your spiritual discipline cost you anything on behalf of someone else?" They give modern parallels, like "a business owner who is devout on Sunday but underpays staff, or a consumer who never asks how the things they buy were made."[^11] Take the practice you thought about this week. How would Isaiah 58 test it, and what is one change it might ask of you?

</div>

### Guilt Without Shame <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#guilt-without-shame}

*Isaiah admits real guilt but refuses to make sin the people's identity.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 64:5–9 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The prophet admits, "All our righteous acts are like filthy rags," then turns: "Yet you, Lord, are our Father. We are the clay, you are the potter." The notes distinguish *guilt* ("I have lied," naming an action), *shame* ("I am a liar," making the action an identity), and *conviction*, which admits guilt "while staying secure in who you are, which leads to repentance."[^12] What do you notice about how the prayer moves from verse 6 to verse 8? Why might shame make repentance harder rather than easier?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Isaiah 65:8 says, "As when juice is still found in a cluster of grapes and people say, 'Don't destroy it, there is still a blessing in it'…" The notes read the new heavens and new earth that follow (65:17–25) as a picture of the *quality* of the age to come, *Olam Haba*, "not a literal, mechanical forecast."[^13] Where have you seen God, or a person, refuse to give up on someone because there was "still a blessing in it"?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** Marty reviews all of Isaiah in two parts. Part One, "As a Father Disciplines His Son," has four steps: "You're in a mess," "Trust the story; God will save you," "Well, that didn't work," and "Consequences." Part Two, "Partnering with Dad," has three: "Suffering is the way to restoration," "Perseverance produces fruit," and "Say yes to building a new world with Dad." He adds that healthy discipline "is not driven by revenge."[^14] Looking back over our weeks in Isaiah, which step of that review describes where you are right now? What would saying yes to "building a new world" look like for you?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Isaiah ends with hope grounded in reality, not easy triumph. The new world God promises is the world he wanted from the beginning: eunuchs and foreigners welcomed, the hungry fed, the oppressed set free, and God's people becoming repairers of broken walls. The main obstacle is often God's own people, yet God keeps saying "Here am I," and there is still juice in the cluster. Our part is to let our practices produce justice, to keep Sabbath as a way into God's delight, to own our guilt without taking on shame, and to keep partnering with God in building what comes next.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, name one thing from our weeks in Isaiah that you want to carry with you.

</div>
