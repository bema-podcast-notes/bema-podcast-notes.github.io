### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Jesus walked through six examples (murder, adultery, divorce, oaths, retaliation, and love of enemies) to show that Torah was always meant to change hearts, not just regulate behavior. "Be perfect" turned out to echo Leviticus 19:2: be holy, set apart, different from the world because of how you love, even your enemies.

**This week:** Jesus turns to three practices his listeners already kept (giving, prayer, and fasting) and asks a harder question than "are you doing them?" The question is who you are doing them for.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode begins with a word. The Greek *hupocritos*, usually translated "hypocrite," simply meant "actor," someone performing for an audience. That changes Jesus' critique: it is not about saying one thing and doing another, but about doing the right things for the wrong reasons. Trumpets, street corners, and disfigured faces all turn out to be theater imagery.

At the center of the passage is the Lord's Prayer, which the episode presents as a version of the *Amidah* (the "standing prayer") that Jews already prayed daily. Jesus adds one line that appears in no version of that prayer: "as we forgive our debtors." The episode suggests that this line about forgiveness may sit at the very center of the whole Sermon on the Mount. It closes by asking what makes repeated, liturgical prayer either empty or deeply alive, a question our different traditions will answer in different ways.

### Actors on a Stage <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#actors-on-a-stage}

*Jesus describes religious practice in the language of the theater, and that language reframes what hypocrisy is.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 6:1–6 and 6:16–18 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes point out three images a first-century audience would have recognized from the theater: a trumpet blast announcing a famous actor's entrance, actors positioned on stage where they could be "maximally visible," and exaggerated makeup that let emotions be seen from distant seats.[^1] As you listened to the reading, which words or phrases sounded like a stage to you? What does each image suggest about who the performer is really playing to?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** In modern usage a hypocrite is someone whose actions don't match their words. The episode says the Greek *hupocritos* simply meant "actor." The Pharisees, whom Jesus called hypocrites more than any other group, "were among the most consistent people in terms of aligning their actions with their stated beliefs."[^2] The notes conclude that "we can do exactly what we say we believe, be perfectly consistent in our religious practices, and still be hypocrites if we're doing it all for show."[^3] How does this definition change the way you hear Jesus' warnings? Why might the "actor" version be harder to notice in ourselves than the "inconsistent" version?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode reads the rewards in this passage as mostly present, not only future. Of the performers Jesus says, "They have received their reward in full": the recognition is the whole reward. Authentic practice brings a different reward: "being changed now, experiencing life transformation before death, not just after it."[^4] How have you usually thought about rewards in Jesus' teaching? What would it look like to measure a spiritual practice by how it is changing you rather than by what it earns?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The episode notices a tension. In Matthew 5:14–16 Jesus says to let your light shine so others see your good deeds; in Matthew 6 he says to practice righteousness in secret. Marty talks openly with his students about his fasting, because "most people don't know how to fast, and teaching requires modeling," yet he says he fasts for "far deeper, more meaningful reasons" than being noticed.[^5] Where in your own life is a good practice visible to others? How do you tell the difference, from the inside, between letting your light shine and performing?

</div>

### The Prayer at the Center <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-prayer-at-the-center}

*Jesus does not hand his disciples a new prayer; he hands them one they already know, with one addition.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 6:7–15 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The episode says the Lord's Prayer is a version of the *Amidah*, which first-century Jews prayed daily at noon in groups of ten. The notes record an older Hebrew form that begins, "Our Father, the one who dwells in heaven, may your name be holy."[^6] The episode hears Jesus' point as, "You already know how to pray. Just pray. There's no secret formula, no special magic words."[^7] What changes for you when you picture the disciples hearing words they already prayed every day, rather than something brand new?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** According to the notes, Jesus added one line that appears in no version of the *Amidah*, "ancient, medieval, or modern": "as we forgive our debtors." In Jesus' first-century context, forgiveness was understood as God's work; if someone wronged you, "that was between them and God."[^8] Read verses 14–15 again. What do you notice about where Jesus puts his emphasis right after the prayer ends? Why might inviting ordinary people into the work of forgiveness have felt so new?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe a proposal that the whole Sermon on the Mount is a chiasm, a pattern that moves toward a center and back out again. The first half teaches how to interpret Torah ("you have heard it said"), the second half how to apply it (giving, praying, fasting), and the center is the Lord's Prayer, "specifically the forgiveness line."[^9] Looking back over the weeks we have spent in Matthew 5, what earlier teachings seem to lead toward forgiveness?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes call human participation in forgiveness "one of Jesus's most scandalous and consistently repeated themes," and say this participation "is what transforms our hearts."[^10] Without naming anyone, think of a debt someone owes you that you are still holding. What would it mean for you to pray this line honestly this week?

</div>

### When the Words Repeat <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#when-the-words-repeat}

*Jesus warns against babbling, then gives a prayer meant to be said again and again.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The episode says the warning against babbling "like pagans" is not a condemnation of repeated prayer. Pagan prayers treated words like "magical incantations," while Jesus immediately gives a prayer his disciples said "every single day at noon."[^11] Brent shares that as a Catholic child he missed the word "pagans," decided that repeating the Hail Mary was the babbling Jesus condemned, and only years later realized his error.[^12] Our traditions practice prayer very differently, some with set words and some without. What has your own tradition taught you about repeated or written prayer, and how does this reading sit with what you were taught?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe three stages of repeated prayer: learning the words, a "lullaby effect" where you say them absent-mindedly, and "deep wells," where the prayer begins to speak back. Marty says of the blessing he repeats each morning that it "starts to talk back to me."[^13] The notes suggest that people for whom a tradition became deeply meaningful were often "mentored and discipled in such a way that they were able to push through."[^14] Think of a prayer, song, or verse you have said so often that it either went dead or came alive. What do you think made the difference, and what might help you push through to the third stage?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Jesus is not against visible faith; he is against faith performed as theater. The *hupocritos* is not the inconsistent person but the actor, who does all the right things for the audience and receives the audience's applause as the whole reward. Jesus points his disciples to a prayer they already knew and adds a single line that invites them into the work of forgiveness. If that line really is the center of the sermon, then the question for every practice we keep is the same: is this real, and is it changing how I treat people?
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** Going around the room, finish this sentence in one line: "This week, one thing I want to do for an audience of One is..."

</div>
