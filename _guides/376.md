### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Marty asked us to hear Isaiah 53 at least once as a word to Israel in exile, and Isaiah 54–55 showed that faithful suffering produces a rebuilt city and a free invitation to the thirsty.[^1]

**This week:** With Marty away, Brent, Elle and Reed look at Isaiah's maternal images for God and the invitation to experience God's closeness, comfort, and playfulness.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Elle leads this conversation and wants it to be practical as well as philosophical. It opens with two basic questions: does God have a sex, and does God have a gender? The group then works through three passages: God as a woman crying out in childbirth beside God as a warrior (Isaiah 42), God as a nursing mother who cannot forget her child (Isaiah 49), and God as a mother who nurses, carries, and plays with her child on her knees (Isaiah 66). Along the way they look at the Hebrew word *racham* (womb, compassion), the power of breath and voice, and what these images mean for masculinity and parenting.[^3]

The traditions in our room speak about God in different ways, and the episode itself takes the Bible's masculine pronouns for God seriously while exploring these images. Birth and parenting images may also be painful for some of us, for reasons such as infertility, a hard birth, or difficult parents. Brent encourages working through them with others. Share only what you choose to.[^4]

### The Warrior and the Woman in Labor <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-warrior-and-the-woman-in-labor}

*Isaiah sets fierce power and life-giving nurture side by side.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Elle starts with the catechism: God is "a spirit, and hath not a body like man" (or woman), so gendered language for God is "imagery and metaphor, not anatomy." The hosts then take three angles on gender: Genesis 1:27 ("male and female he created them"), Hebrew grammar (which has no neuter, so God's pronouns are masculine), and culture (Reed's point that "masculine" and "feminine" are largely cultural labels).[^5] Which of these three angles helps you most, and which raises the most questions for you?

*Read Isaiah 42:13–14 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** "The LORD will march out like a champion, like a warrior... But now, like a woman in childbirth, I cry out, I gasp and pant." The notes say Hebrew poetry often pairs images side by side to explain each other. The hosts find common ground: both the warrior and the mother are fully present in the moment, both risk life ("the warrior *risks life to defend life*," "the mother *risks life to bring in new life*"), and both work through voice and breath. Reed stresses that the cry of labor is "closer to a battle cry than a whimper."[^6] What do you notice when you hear these two images together? What does each one show about God that the other might not?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes observe that in Genesis 1 God *speaks* and creation comes into being, and in Genesis 2 God *breathes* life into Adam. In Hebrew poetry breath is often fierce rather than soft, and Reed now pictures a "gale-force" breath at Adam's creation instead of a quiet one.[^7] How does picturing God's breath as fierce change the way you hear Genesis 2:7?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Because God creates by speaking, Elle asks: "are we complaining, gossiping, and forecasting the worst, or are we praying, praising, encouraging, and speaking life?" Reed recalls students still wounded by words from high school coaches, and the notes connect this to James 3.[^8] Think of words spoken to you years ago, kind or cruel, that still echo. What words could you speak this week that would create something good?

</div>

### Racham: The Womb Beneath Mercy <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#racham-the-womb-beneath-mercy}

*The Hebrew word often translated "mercy" comes from the word for womb.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 49:13–16 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Drawing on Rabbi David Fohrman, Elle explains that *racham*, usually translated "mercy," shares a root with *rechem*, "womb." "Mercy" suggests a courtroom, so she prefers "nurturing" or "compassion," because the womb is expansive, is the first provider, and is a safe place. Reed adds that everything the womb does is directed toward the child's life, "and it never stops, even while the mother sleeps." Verse 13 says the Lord "will have compassion on his afflicted ones."[^9] What do you notice in these verses once you hear "compassion" as womb-love? How is that different from a courtroom picture of mercy?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> "Can a mother forget the baby at her breast...? Though she may forget, I will not forget you!" The notes point out that the text admits human mothers *may* forget, and "for people with difficult mothers, God says, 'I am not that.'" They describe a love that "does not depend on performance."[^10] Without needing to share details, what does it mean to you that God's love is described as stronger than the strongest human bond?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Elle's "radical" conclusion is that if God has *racham* and God's pronouns are masculine, "there is nothing unmasculine about nurturing." The notes say this may be freeing for men who never fit a hardened version of masculinity and challenging for those who find toughness easy. In 1 Thessalonians 2:7 and 11, Paul describes his ministry as like a nursing mother *and* an exhorting father.[^11] Our traditions and families teach different things about men, women, and nurture. What were you taught, and how does this passage free or challenge you?

</div>

### Nursed, Carried, and Dandled <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#nursed-carried-and-dandled}

*God's presence is the closeness of a parent who simply loves being with the child.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 66:10–13 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Elle highlights three images: nursing (which calms and creates closeness, not only feeds), being carried on the hip (constant "withness"; the baby "does nothing impressive and doesn't need to"), and being dandled on the knees (play). The notes say God's presence is neither abstract omnipresence nor "a bodyguard who follows us around counting our mistakes."[^12] What do you notice about these three images? Which one is hardest for you to picture God doing?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Reed jokes that we sing about being soldiers in God's army, and asks what would happen if we wrote songs about being "babies in God's nursery." Elle describes comforting her three-year-old first ("Oh no, what happened? Tell me about it.") before talking about how to do better next time.[^13] Where would "comfort before correction" change the way you treat others, or yourself?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** Elle challenges BEMA listeners, who may prefer keeping faith intellectual, to get out of the "brain cage" and go "further up and further in" (C.S. Lewis): to be open to being comforted, carried, and delighted in by God.[^14] What would it look like for you to let yourself be carried or delighted in by God this week, rather than only thinking about God?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Isaiah's most tender images for God sit beside his fiercest. God cries out like a woman in labor next to a warrior's shout, showing that power and nurture are not opposites. God's love outlasts even a nursing mother's bond and engraves us on God's palms. God nurses, carries, and bounces us on the knee, wanting closeness rather than performance. Underneath it all runs *racham*, a womb-like compassion that fits fathers as well as mothers, and an invitation to be held by God, not only to think about God.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, name one way you will let yourself receive God's comfort this week, or offer that comfort to someone else.

</div>
