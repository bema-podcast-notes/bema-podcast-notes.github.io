### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Song of Songs took its place among the wisdom books as the tool for relationships, and three Hebrew words for love (*rayah*, infatuation; *ahava*, commitment; and *dowd*, erotic love) were pictured as flames that become a bonfire when they burn together[^1].

**This week:** We leave the wisdom literature and step back into the story, meeting the prophets as God's messengers to real people in real crises, arranged across five historical periods[^2].

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This short episode works as a map for the weeks ahead. It begins with Brent's realization that he had read Psalms and Proverbs as if they were written for him alone, when they were written for the whole assembly of Israel to practice together[^3]. It then reframes the prophet (Hebrew *navi*) as a messenger with a present-tense word for a present audience, not a fortune-teller speaking in code to people centuries later[^4].

The second half arranges the prophets into five periods: pre-Assyrian, Assyrian, Babylonian, exilic and remnant[^5]. Along the way the episode introduces the idea that Isaiah holds several voices from different periods, and it carries forward the earlier contrast between Source A (Samuel and Kings) and Source B (Chronicles) as two lenses for hearing the prophets[^6]. Because the episode is brief, this guide spends extra time reading short prophetic passages together.

### Written for Us, Not Just for Me <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#written-for-us-not-just-for-me}

*Before we meet the prophets, we notice the lens most of us bring to every page.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Brent described reading the Psalms as "a Psalm for me to understand what I'm feeling" and Proverbs as "Proverbs for me to understand what to do," before realizing these texts were written for all of Israel to practice together[^3]. The notes suggest making "Is this communal or individual?" a default question when reading[^3]. What changes when you picture a whole congregation praying a psalm aloud rather than one person reading it alone?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes apply the same insight to the prophets: when Hosea pictures Israel as an unfaithful wife, he is describing the covenant relationship of a whole people rather than giving marriage counseling to individuals[^7]. Where in your own Bible reading or church life do you tend to hear "me" where the text may be saying "us," and what would it look like to read one passage this week with "us" in mind?

</div>

### A Messenger, Not a Fortune-Teller <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#a-messenger-not-a-fortune-teller}

*The prophets spoke first to the people standing in front of them.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Amos 2:6–8 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes describe Amos addressing economic exploitation in eighth-century Israel during a season when the nation was prosperous but unjust[^8]. Listening to the passage, what details tell you who Amos's audience was and what was happening in their towns and markets? Name the specific people and actions you noticed.

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The episode says Western Christianity has often pictured prophets speaking cryptically about the far future, leaving their first hearers confused. By contrast, the notes call any later fulfillment "icing on the awesome biblical cake," not the primary purpose[^4]. If Amos's first hearers were meant to understand him and respond, what does that suggest about how we should read a prophet before asking what the passage means for us?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Many of us were taught to read the prophets mainly as predictions pointing toward Jesus, and the notes themselves name the question of how to hold the historical meaning and the Christian reading tradition together responsibly[^9]. Without deciding which approach is right, what has the predictive reading given you over the years, and what might a present-tense reading add that you would not want to lose?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> *Read Amos 5:21–24 aloud.* The notes summarize Amos's message as "God cares more about justice than your religious festivals"[^8]. If a prophet were sent to your town this year with a present-tense message, what situation do you imagine that message would address, and how would you want to respond?

</div>

### Five Periods, One Underlying Message <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#five-periods-one-underlying-message}

*Knowing when and where a prophet spoke changes what we hear.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The episode arranges the prophets in five waves: pre-Assyrian (Hosea, Amos, Micah, First Isaiah), Assyrian (Jonah, Nahum, Zephaniah, Second Isaiah), Babylonian (Jeremiah, Lamentations, Habakkuk, Joel, Obadiah), exilic (Ezekiel, Daniel, Third Isaiah, possibly Job) and remnant (Ezra and Nehemiah, Haggai, Zechariah, Fourth Isaiah, Malachi, Esther)[^5].

*Read Ezekiel 1:1–3 aloud, then Haggai 1:2–6.*

Ezekiel sits by the River Chebar in Babylon facing the question "Can we encounter God outside the Promised Land?" Haggai speaks to returned exiles deciding what to rebuild first[^10]. What do you notice about each speaker's location and audience, and how does that change the way each message lands?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes describe two ways of hearing the prophets. Source A hears them as moral correction focused on obedience; Source B hears them calling God's people back to their identity and mission, since the deeper failure was a "lust for empire" and "forgetfulness of God's great project." The notes say both readings are inspired and correct[^6]. Which lens were you trained to listen with, and what might the other one help you hear?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode suggests Isaiah contains several voices from different periods, preserved and arranged by later communities. The notes stress that this "isn't about denying divine inspiration or biblical authority" and that the text is "God-breathed" in its final form, whatever its compositional history[^11]. This may be a new idea for some and a familiar one for others. What questions does it raise for you, and what about Isaiah's message would stay the same either way?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes say the remnant prophets called the people to "walk in the ways of God and recapture the mission of God in the world to remember where they came from"[^12]. What does your family, your church or this group most need to remember about where it came from, and what would remembering look like this month?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The prophets were not puzzle-makers writing to us over the heads of their neighbors. They were God's messengers, sent to real people facing Assyria, Babylon and the long road home, calling them back to who they were. Over the coming weeks we will meet them one at a time, in the order they spoke. The episode's counsel for the journey is simple: "listen for the underlying message."[^13]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one assumption about the prophets that you are ready to set down, or one question you want to carry into the weeks ahead?

</div>
