### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** BEMA puts the text at the center, with the community gathered around it, and asks us to dump out the "file cabinet" of old pictures so we can read the Bible again in its own Jewish context.

**This week:** We start at the very beginning. Marty and Brent read Genesis 1 as a carefully built Hebrew poem whose center points to sacred time and rest, written for people who had been valued only for what they produced.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The notes say plainly, "Everything will build on this lesson." Marty and Brent treat Genesis 1 as a poem rather than a science report. Its six days mirror one another (days one and four, two and five, three and six), and it is full of patterns of three, seven, and ten. At the exact center of that mirror structure sits the Hebrew word *moad* ("sacred times" or "seasons"), one of the words connected to Sabbath.

The hosts then ask who first heard this poem: Hebrew slaves just out of Egypt, who had made bricks for 430 years and had been told their value lay in their output. For them, the first lesson is how to stop. The episode closes with practical, personal pictures of Sabbath from Marty's own home and an invitation to "trust the story": creation, including you, is good.

### A Poem Built Around a Center <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#a-poem-built-around-a-center}

*Genesis 1 is shaped like a mirror, and the shape is part of the message.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 1:1–25 aloud, with different readers taking different days.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Marty points out that plants appear on day three, before the sun is made on day four, and that the creatures of days four through six fill the spaces made on days one through three.[^1] Look back over what was read. What fills the light of day one? What fills the sky and waters of day two? What fills the dry land of day three? What do you notice when you line the days up this way?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes list some of the poem's patterns: seven Hebrew words in the first verse, fourteen in the second, "earth" twenty-one times, and "and God said" ten times.[^2] Marty's conclusion is blunt: "This poem is FULL of patterns... it is NOT a scientific lab report."[^3] Our churches have often been part of public debates about Genesis 1 and science. What questions do you think those debates were trying to protect? What questions might they have kept us from asking?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** In a chiasm (a mirror structure), "there is a treasure buried in the middle." The poem begins with "chaotic nothingness" and ends with God "doing nothing--resting," and the word at its center is *moad*, which can be translated "seasons."[^4] The notes say Sabbath matters so much to the Jewish people "because the Bible begins with a huge poem about Shabbat."[^5] If the Bible's opening chapter is mainly about rest, what changes about how you read everything that follows?

</div>

### Worth That Is Not Measured in Bricks <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#worth-that-is-not-measured-in-bricks}

*The poem's first hearers needed to unlearn a story about their value.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The first audience were Hebrew slaves who had made bricks for 430 years. In Egypt's story their value equaled their productivity; in God's story their value comes from being beloved creation.[^6] The notes put it simply: before anything else, God "needs his people to know how to take a break."[^7] Why do you think rest, rather than a law or a doctrine, is the first lesson?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> God calls creation "very good" after making humans, and stops not because he is tired but because "there is nothing more he can do to make creation any better." Marty adds that "perfection doesn't even exist in the Hebrew mindset"; creation was not perfect, it was good.[^8] What is the difference, for you, between being called good and being called perfect? Which one is easier to believe about yourself?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes name modern versions of Egypt's story: "I am what I do," worth tied to achievement or appearance, and validation measured by performance metrics.[^9] Where do you feel the brick quota in your own life right now, in work, family, church, or anywhere else?

</div>

### We Rest, We Play <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#we-rest-we-play}

*Sabbath is not another performance to master; it is a reminder that we are loved.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Jewish days begin in the evening. Why? "Because the first thing that a Jew does is not production but resting."[^10] In this rhythm, sleep comes before work and identity is set in rest before activity.[^11] What would change in your week if you thought of each day as beginning when you lie down rather than when you get up?

*Read Genesis 2:1–3 and Mark 2:27 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Every day so far has closed with the refrain of evening and morning. The notes observe that this refrain is missing from the seventh day, "as if the author is hanging endlessly forever," because God's invitation is "to trust the story."[^12] The notes also quote Jesus: "Man was not created for Sabbath but Sabbath was created for man."[^13] What do these two observations add up to? What kind of Sabbath is this, and who is it for?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty hates shaving his head and loves making his bed, so on Shabbat he does neither. Leaving the bed unmade makes him cringe, but it reminds him that even when his life looks like that, he is still loved.[^14] What small thing could you deliberately leave undone for one day, and what would you hope it reminds you of?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The Solomon children describe the Sabbath this way: "we rest. we play. no work." and God loves them. Marty puts the test sharply: if you are not doing something that reminds you that you are loved by God, "you're doing it wrong." He adds that this rest is also a posture that shifts us "from a scarcity mindset to one of abundance."[^15] What is one concrete practice you could try this week that would remind you that you are loved apart from your output?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Genesis 1 is a poem, built with care, and at its center is sacred time. It was first heard by people who had been told for generations that their worth was measured in bricks. God's first lesson for them was how to stop, because creation, including them, was already good. The seventh day never closes, which means the invitation is still open to us: to rest, to play, and to trust the story.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one thing you will stop doing, or start doing, this week as a way of trusting the story?

</div>
