### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Esther, written for the Jews who stayed in Persia, never names God yet is full of providence. Esther walked Joseph's path, used shrewd wisdom before the king, and cast her lot before God, teaching that silence in the face of injustice is complicity.[^1]

**This week:** Instead of one more book, Marty and Brent set two tables, one in Babylon and one in Persia, and seat the prophets around them to argue about how God's people should respond to empire.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode claims that the prophetic literature is not a single, uniform voice but an ongoing dialogue among God's people about how to respond to exile.[^3] At the Babylonian table, the question is "What do we do about Babylon?" Isaiah says stand and suffer; Ezekiel, Habakkuk and Job say learn to live faithfully in exile; Jeremiah and Zephaniah say return to God first. At the Persian table, the question becomes "What do we do about Persia?" Ezra, Nehemiah, Haggai and Zechariah say return and rebuild; Esther and Daniel say stay, accommodate and resist; Malachi says obey wherever you are.[^4]

From there the episode makes a larger claim about Scripture itself: that it is a living, God-breathed dialogue that invites wrestling, not a codified list of rules, and that different contexts call for different prophetic voices.[^5] Some of us come from traditions that put great weight on doing exactly what the Bible says, so this may be a conversation where we listen carefully to each other.

### The Babylonian Table <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#the-babylonian-table}

*In exile, the prophets agree on the crisis and disagree on the faithful response.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 53:4–5, Habakkuk 3:17–19 and Jeremiah 3:14 aloud, pausing between them.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes describe three voices at the Babylonian table:[^6]

- **Isaiah:** stand and suffer, standing publicly for God's justice even at great personal cost, as in the Suffering Servant discourse.
- **Ezekiel, Habakkuk and Job:** accept exile as home, recognizing that Babylon's ways are not right while learning to be faithful in this place.
- **Jeremiah and Zephaniah:** repentance and covenant restoration, *t'shuva* (returning to God), because the broken relationship matters more than staying or leaving.

Each passage you just read comes from one of these voices. What do you notice about what each one asks God's people to do, and which words in each passage carry that voice?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes say each voice addresses the same crisis but sees different priorities and solutions, and the comprehension questions ask what the strengths and weaknesses of each might be.[^7] Pick one of the three voices. What does it see clearly, and what might it miss if it were the only voice at the table?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> When life feels like exile to you, which of these three voices do you instinctively reach for first, and which one do you tend to resist?

</div>

### The Persian Table <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-persian-table}

*Under a gentler empire, the question changes, and so do the answers.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Nehemiah 2:1–5, Daniel 6:10 and Malachi 1:6–8 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes say Persia treated the Jewish people very differently from Babylon, with relative freedom and permission to go home.[^8] They describe three voices at this table:[^9]

- **Ezra, Nehemiah, Haggai and Zechariah:** return and rebuild. It will be difficult, but it is worth the effort.
- **Esther and Daniel:** accommodation and resistance, staying in Persia "with shrewd wisdom, not assimilation," honoring the king while shrewdly subverting empire's ways.
- **Malachi:** obedience regardless of location. Do not rob God, dishonor God or bring blemished offerings.

Match each passage you just read to a voice. What do you notice about Nehemiah's request, Daniel's window and Malachi's complaint, and how does each show what faithfulness looks like to that speaker?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The episode applies the tables to different settings today. It suggests that for 21st-century Western believers, living under a relatively benign empire more like Persia than Babylon, the Esther and Daniel model may fit best; that for persecuted believers whose churches have been burned, the return-and-rebuild message and Zechariah's hope may matter most; and that in other places, Malachi's call to obedience may be most fitting.[^10] Where do you agree or disagree with how the episode reads our own setting, and what would "accommodation and resistance" look like for you this year?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes say Scripture should not be understood as "a codified list of things to do," a "read-the-Bible-do-what-it-says" instruction manual, but as a living dialogue that invites wrestling.[^11] They contrast a common Christian approach, "God said it, I believe it, that settles it," with the Jewish perspective: "God said it, and now we all have to wrestle with it."[^12] Each of our traditions has its own way of honoring the authority of Scripture, and that is worth respecting. What does your tradition do well in how it reads the Bible, and what might the idea of a prophetic dialogue add to it or challenge in it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes offer an example: a church torn between social justice and personal piety. They call it a false binary, since Isaiah's voice calls for standing against injustice, Jeremiah and Zephaniah emphasize covenant relationship and repentance, and Malachi stresses obedience and worship, with all three "needed in different proportions depending on context."[^13] Which voice do you think your church or community most needs to hear right now, and why?

</div>

### Wrestling Together <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#wrestling-together}

*The episode closes with a picture of what it means to argue faithfully, as partners with God.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes recount a parable, attributed to Peter Rollins and possibly from the Talmud, about two rabbis who argue every day. When God comes down to settle the argument, both tell God to leave so they can keep arguing. The point is that "the power is in the ongoing wrestling, not in arriving at a final answer."[^14] What do you make of this story, and what might the rabbis know that we sometimes forget?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes connect the tables to an earlier idea from this session: Samuel and Kings as "agenda-driven headlines" and Chronicles as a "documentary perspective." Neither is wrong; both are accurate but represent different perspectives.[^15] Looking back over these 39 weeks, where else have we seen Scripture hold more than one faithful view in tension?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The episode frames the whole prophetic dialogue as partnership, *sh'tuf*, with God, and asks: "As partners with God in His mission, what does that mission demand in our specific context?"[^16] Think about your own setting: your household, work, church and town. Which prophetic voice does God's mission most demand of you right now?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The prophets do not speak with one voice. Around the Babylonian table they argued about whether to stand and suffer, learn to live in exile, or return to God first, and around the Persian table they argued about whether to rebuild, to stay and resist shrewdly, or to obey above all. The episode says none of these voices is simply right or wrong; each is a faithful response to its context, and Scripture keeps the whole conversation. That makes Bible reading less like looking up the answer and more like taking a seat at the table, in community, with open hands and a commitment to God's mission.[^17]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, name the prophetic voice you most need to keep at your table this year.

</div>
