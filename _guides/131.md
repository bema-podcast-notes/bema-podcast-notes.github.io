### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We asked who killed Jesus and heard a clear answer: not the Jewish people, who loved him, but a small, corrupt group of chief priests who tried him at night and pushed Pilate into a corner. Misreading this story has cost millions of Jewish lives.[^1]

**This week:** We turn to the two disciples who failed Jesus on his last night, Judas and Peter, and ask what really separates their stories.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode speaks of the betrayals of Jesus, plural. It explores Judas Iscariot's name as "man from Keriot," a place the notes connect to the Zealots, and suggests Judas may have believed he was forcing Jesus' hand to start a revolution rather than betraying him. It also traces how closely Judas's actions follow Zechariah 11.[^3] Then it makes a surprising claim: from a rabbinical perspective, Peter's public denial of his rabbi was the more serious betrayal.[^4]

The heart of the episode is its ending. The difference between Judas and Peter "is not the severity of their mistakes, but whether they believed there could be a tomorrow after their failure."[^5] This story includes Judas's death by suicide. Some of us carry losses or struggles that make this hard to hear, and the notes themselves ask how the church can teach it with "pastoral sensitivity."[^6] Please be gentle with one another, and feel free to pass on any question.

### The Man From Keriot <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#the-man-from-keriot}

*Judas may have heard what he wanted to hear.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 26:14–16 and Matthew 26:47–50 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes explain that "Iscariot" comes from the Hebrew *ish Keriot*, "man from Keriot," and that Keriot may have been a Zealot compound. Seen this way, Judas's concern for the poor, his role as treasurer, and even his taking from the money bag with a "Robin Hood mentality" fit a Zealot outlook.[^7] After a week of Jesus confronting the chief priests, the notes picture Judas "champing at the bit," likely believing "he was forcing Jesus's hand to kickstart the revolution, not betraying him."[^8] As you hear these verses, what details fit that picture, and what details resist it?

*Read Zechariah 11:12–13 aloud, then read Matthew 27:3–10 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes show how closely Judas's actions mirror Zechariah 11: a prophecy against corrupt shepherds, thirty pieces of silver, the silver thrown "to the potter at the house of the Lord," and a closing word, "Woe to the worthless shepherd, who deserts the flock." They suggest Judas may have taken that last condemnation onto himself.[^9] What parallels do you notice between the two passages? What does it suggest that Judas may have known Scripture this well and still misread his teacher?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes stress that all the disciples, not just Judas, seem to have misread Jesus as leading a Zealot-style revolution; even after the resurrection they asked, "Are you going to restore the Kingdom now?"[^10] They add that "People can know a teacher well, hear their teachings repeatedly, and still completely misunderstand their intent."[^11] Where might you be filtering Jesus' words through an agenda of your own?

</div>

### The Other Betrayal <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-other-betrayal}

*A rabbinical reader would have pointed to a different disciple.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 26:69–75 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Follow Peter through the three denials. What changes from the first to the third, in who is asking, where he is, and what he says?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes say that a disciple trying to do the right thing and making a terrible mistake is part of the learning process, but that publicly disassociating from one's rabbi was "unheard of in rabbinical discipleship, the truest form of a slap to the face of his teacher." Peter understood this; he went back to fishing "because he knew his time as a disciple was over."[^12] The notes add that the Gospel writers call Judas "the betrayer" so often, "awkwardly often," precisely because first-century Jewish readers would naturally have pointed to Peter.[^13] How does this change the way you see Peter's tears in verse 75?

*Read Mark 16:7 aloud, then read John 21:15–19 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes point out that the message at the empty tomb is to "tell his disciples and Peter," because Peter would no longer have been counted a disciple, and that without John's Gospel we would have almost no record of Peter's reinstatement on the beach. That reinstatement was "necessary for Peter to later become the leader seen in Acts."[^14] What do you notice in the way Jesus restores Peter? Why might it matter that the restoration is named, specific, and public?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Peter heard his name singled out after the worst failure of his life. Who in your life might need to hear their name spoken, the way "and Peter" was spoken, to know they are still wanted?

</div>

### Is There a Tomorrow? <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#is-there-a-tomorrow}

*The two stories end differently, and the episode says that is the whole point.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes put it plainly: "Both made huge mistakes - one misjudged his teacher's mission, the other publicly denied any connection." Judas "decided his mistakes could not be overcome," while Peter "was willing to face his greatest fears and insecurities, trusting Jesus to give him a new tomorrow."[^15] The episode concludes that "The only true betrayal of Jesus is believing there's no coming back, no second chance, no hope."[^16] What do you make of that claim? What does it say about what Jesus most wants from people who have failed him?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode links this story to Genesis. Peter's question about forgiving "70 times 7" echoes Lamech in Cain's line, and God's word to Cain, "If you do what's right, you'll be accepted," parallels Jesus' message to Peter. The notes say "The pattern of not being defined by one's biggest mistakes runs throughout Scripture."[^17] Where else in our study have you seen God refuse to let someone be defined by their worst moment?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes describe this as a story of "Jesus's ministry of second chances, bottomless grace, and wasteful love."[^18] Think of a failure, old or recent, that you still carry. Without needing to name it aloud, what would it look like to let Jesus give you a tomorrow after it? Share as much or as little as you wish.

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> On the same night, two disciples failed their rabbi. Judas, perhaps a Zealot who thought he was forcing a revolution, handed Jesus over for thirty pieces of silver. Peter, the bold one, swore three times that he did not know him, a deeper break by the standards of his day. Both stories could have ended there. One did. The other heard "and Peter," met Jesus on a beach, and was given a new tomorrow. The episode leaves us with this: the only true betrayal is believing there is no way back.[^19]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one word you want to say to someone (perhaps yourself) about second chances this week?

</div>
