### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** At Babel, people moving east tried to make a name for themselves before they could be scattered. God's scattering read less like a punishment than a new project: to do anything great, humanity would have to learn one another's languages.

**This week:** A short episode that steps back to see Genesis 1–11 as one designed whole. The eight stories we have studied form a mirrored structure, and at its very center sits a name that points to rest.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This week is a review with a twist. The episode describes Genesis 1–11 as a "preface" to the whole biblical story: world-building that comes before the introduction of the main characters in Genesis 12–50 and the main narrative from Exodus onward.[^1] The authors take stories their first audience already knew and retell them so that readers will see God, humanity, and creation in a new way.[^2]

The headline discovery is that the eight sections of Genesis 1–11 mirror one another in a large chiasm (a mirrored structure). Creation pairs with the flood, Adam and Eve with Noah's curse, Cain and Abel with Babel, and the two genealogies with each other.[^3] At the center is Genesis 5:28–29, where Noah is named, and the message the hosts draw from the whole preface is short: "TRUST THE STORY."[^4] Because the episode is brief, we have extra time to look back over the past seven weeks together.

### Reading a Preface <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#reading-a-preface}

*Genesis 1–11 sets up a way of seeing before the main story begins.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The episode compares Genesis 1–11 to Tolkien's preface to *The Lord of the Rings*, which introduces Middle Earth's hobbits, elves, and orcs before the story starts.[^5] The notes divide Scripture into three parts: the preface (Genesis 1–11), the introduction to Abraham's family (Genesis 12–50), and the main narrative (Exodus through Revelation). They add that the type of literature and language shifts as you move from Genesis 11 into Genesis 12.[^6] What changes for you if Genesis 1–11 is mainly meant to shape how you see the world, rather than mainly to tell you what happened first?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say these chapters challenge the idea of "the angry Old Testament God of popular misconception," and that God is consistently loving and patient throughout them. Even the flood, read closely, is about God saving the world and knowing when to stop destroying.[^7] Which of these early stories most shaped your picture of God when you first heard it? How has your picture shifted, if at all, over the past seven weeks?

</div>

### Eight Stories, One Shape <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#eight-stories-one-shape}

*The stories we have studied mirror each other in pairs.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Here is the structure the episode lays out, with the pattern the notes give for each story:[^8]

- A: Creation (Genesis 1) and the flood (Genesis 6–9a): goodness, God knows when to stop, rest
- B: Adam and Eve (Genesis 2–3) and Noah's curse (Genesis 9b–10): tragedy, obsession, mistrust
- C: Cain and Abel (Genesis 4) and the Tower of Babel (Genesis 11a): tragedy, obsession, mistrust
- D: Genealogy (Genesis 5) and genealogy (Genesis 11b)

Use your skim of Genesis 1–11 and what we have discussed in earlier weeks. Choose one pair (A, B, or C) and name one specific detail that the two stories share. What do you notice when you hold them side by side?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes name a different obsession in each of the tragic stories: creativity (Adam and Eve), acquisition (Cain and Abel), destruction (Noah's curse), and fame (Babel).[^9] In every one, someone fails to stop. The notes put the lesson this way: "G-d knows when to say enough and we can follow in his footsteps and we know what happens when we don't."[^10] Why do you think the preface keeps telling the same kind of story in so many forms? What does the repetition teach that one story alone could not?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes connect each obsession to modern life: constant consumer desire, competitive career rivalry, cycles of revenge, and social media self-promotion.[^11] Which of the four obsessions do you find easiest to recognize in the world around you, and which is hardest to admit in yourself?

</div>

### A Name at the Center <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#a-name-at-the-center}

*The literal center of the grand structure is a father naming his son.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 5:28–29 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes place the center of the whole preface in these two verses, where Lamech names his son Noah. One set of notes says the name means "comfort"; the original notes say it means "he rests," a man sent to save creation.[^12] Listen to the verses again. What is Lamech hoping for? What words in his hope remind you of earlier stories, and why might it matter that this center is tucked inside a genealogy rather than a dramatic scene?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes sum up what we learn from the preface in four words: "The story is good; **TRUST THE STORY.**"[^13] Another set of notes fills this out as trusting that creation is good, that God loves creation, and that there is enough.[^14] Of those three, which do you find easiest to believe, and which is hardest to live as if it were true?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The structure was first noticed in a classroom: Marty's student Paris Shewey saw that the individual chiasms formed a larger one.[^15] The episode quotes Rob Bell: "It's almost as if people had help." The notes add, "If there are multiple authors to this story, it's even more impressive."[^16] Our traditions hold a range of views about how Scripture was written. When you hear that a structure like this might have come through several authors, what is your first reaction, and what does that reaction tell you about what you believe inspiration needs to look like?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes describe the rest principle in practice: learning when to stop working, knowing when enough is enough in relationships, career, and possessions, and trusting God's timing rather than forcing outcomes.[^17] Where in your week is God inviting you to stop, and what would it look like to trust the story there?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> For seven weeks we have read one story at a time. This week we saw that the stories were built to be read together: two stories of goodness, rest, and a God who knows when to stop, surrounded by stories of obsession and mistrust in which people do not stop. At the center is a child named for comfort and rest, a man sent to save creation. The preface does not end with an answer so much as an invitation. Before we meet Abraham, the question is already set: will we trust the story, or will we keep reaching for more?
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, which story from Genesis 1–11 has stayed with you most, and why?

</div>
