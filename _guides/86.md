### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Mark gave Rome a fast, vivid Gospel and then told the crucifixion as a coronation, ending with frightened women at the tomb and a question about what real power looks like.

**This week:** We meet the third voice. Marty questions the familiar picture of Luke as a Gentile writing an orderly history for Gentiles, and proposes a very Jewish Luke.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The common view, the notes explain, is that Luke was the only Gentile author in the Bible, a physician writing a more chronological account for Gentile readers.[^1] Marty argues instead that Luke was a proselyte, a convert to Judaism, who would have been considered "as Jewish as if born Jewish," and that his Gospel shows deep knowledge of Jewish liturgy and literary forms such as chiasm and parallelism.[^2] He also presents Luke as portraying Jesus as a second Moses.[^3]

The episode's central idea comes from scholar M. D. Goulder's book *The Evangelists' Calendar*: Luke wrote an "ordered" (sequenced) account, not merely an "orderly" one, arranged to accompany the weekly synagogue readings from the Torah and the Prophets.[^4] Marty holds this theory with open hands, saying Goulder does not get every section to line up perfectly but that it "mostly lines up brilliantly."[^5] Because the episode is short, this guide gives extra time to reading the texts themselves.

### Who Was Luke? <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#who-was-luke}

*The episode starts by questioning what many of us were taught about Luke's identity and audience.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes give several reasons Marty thinks Luke was a Jewish proselyte rather than a Gentile outsider: Luke traveled with the disciples before the Jerusalem Council (Acts 15), when Gentile inclusion was still controversial, and no one questioned his presence; as a physician he may once have been an Asclepian priest; and in Jewish understanding a circumcised proselyte becomes "as Jewish as if born Jewish."[^6] What were you taught about Luke, and which of these points strikes you most? How would a Jewish Luke change the way you hear his Gospel?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The name Theophilus means "friend of God." The original notes suggest it could be a real proselyte, or "a euphemism for anyone reading Luke's account who considers themself to be a friend of God."[^7] Read Luke 1:3 aloud and hear it addressed to you by that name. What changes when you hear Luke's Gospel written for you as a friend of God?

</div>

### An Ordered Account <span class="guide-time">35 min</span> {#an-ordered-account}

*A single word in Luke's opening may reveal how his whole Gospel is put together.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Luke 1:1–4 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Look closely at these four verses. What does Luke say others have already done? From whom did the accounts come? What does Luke say he is doing, and why? Report what you notice before discussing what it means.

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes explain that "servants of the word" in verse 2 uses the same Greek term the Septuagint uses for the *hazzanim*, synagogue custodians who looked after the scrolls. Since most synagogues owned only one or two scrolls, the *hazzanim* memorized large portions of Scripture and served as "walking concordances."[^8] The notes also say the word translated "orderly" in verse 3 is better rendered "ordered" or "sequenced."[^9] How does knowing who the "servants of the word" were affect your confidence in what Luke handed on? What difference does it make if "ordered" means arranged for a purpose rather than strictly chronological?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> *Read Luke 4:1–13 aloud.* The notes pose a thought experiment: imagine teaching this passage if you knew it was meant to be read alongside a Torah portion about Israel's wilderness wandering.[^10] Without assuming which portion Goulder assigns, what echoes of Israel in the wilderness do you notice in Jesus' temptation? How would reading the two together deepen the story?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Goulder's theory, as the notes describe it, is that each section of Luke corresponds to the weekly *parashah* (Torah portion) and *haftarah* (Prophets portion), creating "a three-dimensional reading experience: Torah → Prophets → Gospel."[^11] The original notes add, "Marty doesn't always think that Goulder got every section to line up perfectly but he has found that it mostly lines up brilliantly."[^12] A BEMA study group in Albany, New York, read each passage of Luke this way and found "layers of meaning and connection previously invisible."[^13] What do you find compelling in this idea, and what makes you cautious? What would it look like for you, or for this group, to try reading a Gospel passage with a Torah and a Prophets reading beside it this month?

</div>

### Jesus, a Second Moses <span class="guide-time">10 min</span> {#jesus-a-second-moses}

*Luke's Jewish themes include one of Second Temple Judaism's central hopes.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Deuteronomy 18:15–18 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes say Luke presents Jesus as the "second Moses," the prophet promised in Deuteronomy whom God would raise up "like Moses," a primary messianic expectation of the time.[^14] What does this promise say the coming prophet will do? What would it mean for a Jewish listener, sitting in synagogue, to hear Jesus presented as this prophet?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes observe that Marty presents Goulder's theory "not as absolute truth but as a compelling framework that has shaped his own understanding," and that he disagrees with parts of Goulder's wider work.[^15] Where in your own faith are you learning to hold an idea with that kind of humility, firmly enough to learn from it and loosely enough to revise it?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Luke tells us at the start that he wrote an ordered account, drawing on eyewitnesses and on the servants of the word who carried Scripture in their memories. If Marty and Goulder are right, Luke is not a Gentile outsider explaining Jesus to strangers, but a convert steeped in synagogue life, arranging the story of Jesus to sit beside the Torah and the Prophets week by week. Whether or not every section lines up, the invitation is the same: to read Jesus in the world he inhabited, a world saturated with Scripture and expecting God to act again as God had acted before.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one thing you want to read differently because of Luke's opening four verses?

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 87 — John — Grafted](/guides/87)** (40:35): The fourth Gospel voice. John, the pastor to Asia, writes for a "grafted" community of Hellenistic Jews and Gentiles in cities like Ephesus. The episode shows how John's opening, "In the beginning was the Word," speaks to Greek philosophers and to Torah-loving Jews at the same time, and how the seven "I am" statements answer both pagan gods and Jewish hopes.

**What to bring next week:** Read John 1:1–5 twice: once imagining you are a Greek philosopher who has long discussed the *logos*, and once imagining you are a Jew who loves Torah. Note which words would catch each reader off guard.
