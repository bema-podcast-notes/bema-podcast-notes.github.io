### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Session 4 opened by retracing the story from Torah to the Gospels and laying out the road ahead. Its guiding question is how God's people live out Christ's reality in their own cultural setting.

**This week:** Acts begins. Jesus ascends, the Spirit comes at Shavuot, and the first community in Jerusalem shows what a true celebration of the festival looks like.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode starts in Rome, not Jerusalem. After Julius Caesar's assassination, a bright comet appeared, and his adopted heir Octavian announced that Caesar had ascended to become a god, which made Octavian a "son of god." Marty argues that Luke tells Jesus' ascension in a way that deliberately mirrors and subverts that story, a tale of two kingdoms.

The second half moves to Pentecost, which is the Jewish festival of Shavuot. Shavuot remembers the giving of Torah at Sinai and celebrates the wheat harvest, and the episode traces parallels between Sinai and Acts 2, including the 3,000 who died after the golden calf and the 3,000 added at Pentecost. It closes with Leviticus 23:22, which tells farmers to leave the corners of their fields for the poor, and a sharp line from Marty's teacher Ray about what makes a celebration of Jesus truly Pentecostal.

### A Tale of Two Ascensions <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#a-tale-of-two-ascensions}

*Luke's first readers already knew a story about a ruler ascending to heaven.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Around 44 BCE a comet as bright as Venus appeared after Julius Caesar's death. Octavian proclaimed it was Caesar ascending to godhood, which made Octavian "son of god," and the Senate presented itself as Caesar's ambassadors, spreading Rome "to the very ends of the earth." This story predates the Gospels by 60 to 90 years.[^1]

*Read Acts 1:6–11 aloud.*

What details in Luke's account might have sounded familiar to someone who knew the Caesar story? What is different?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** Marty calls this a "tale of two kingdoms," Empire versus Shalom, and notes that the Gospel writers apply imperial titles such as "Son of God," "Lord of Lords," and "Prince of Peace" to Jesus.[^2] When you hear the ascension as a claim about who really rules the world, how does that change what it means to call Jesus "Lord"?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Among the topics the notes suggest for further study is the ascension's "neglect in modern church practice."[^3] How much has the ascension figured in the teaching of your church or your own faith? What might it add to the way you live this week if Jesus, not Caesar or any modern power, is the one enthroned?

</div>

### From Sinai to Zion <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#from-sinai-to-zion}

*Pentecost is the Jewish festival of Shavuot, and Luke tells it as an echo of Mount Sinai.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Shavuot (the Festival of Weeks) celebrates both the barley and wheat harvests and the giving of Torah at Sinai, which Jewish tradition places fifty days after Passover.[^4]

*Read Exodus 19:16–19, then Acts 2:1–4, aloud.*

What echoes do you notice between the two scenes? After you have made your own list, compare it with the episode's: two mountains of the Lord, thunder and tongues (the episode notes that Greek uses *glossa* for both), fire, and a message from God brought down to the people.[^5]

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode adds a numerical parallel: about 3,000 died after the golden calf, and 3,000 were added at Pentecost. The notes conclude that this presents "Pentecost as the redemption of the golden calf incident."[^6]

*Read Exodus 32:25–28 and Acts 2:41 aloud.*

What does setting these two scenes side by side suggest about what God is doing at Pentecost?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The episode insists that Pentecost "is not about starting a new religion but calling Jewish people back to their original moorings," and describes Acts as "a Jewish movement of repentance (*t'shuvah*), not conversion to a new religion." *T'shuvah* is the Hebrew word for repentance as returning.[^7] If the people in Acts 2 are Jews returning to their own covenant rather than leaving it, how does that change the way you hear Peter's sermon? How might it change the way Christians speak about Judaism?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes connect the timing to the harvest: Jesus is crucified at Passover, raised at First Fruits (the barley harvest, "poor man's grain"), and the church is born at Shavuot (the wheat harvest, "fine flour").[^8] What does it mean to you that the church's birthday falls on a harvest festival? What kind of harvest is God looking for in your life right now?

</div>

### Uncut Corners <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#uncut-corners}

*The festival comes with an instruction about the edges of the field.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The law told farmers to leave the corners of their fields unharvested for "the alien, the orphan, and the widow."[^9]

*Read Leviticus 23:15–22 aloud.*

Notice where this instruction about corners appears. What festival is it attached to, and what does its placement suggest?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The book of Ruth is traditionally read during Shavuot. Ruth gleans from the barley harvest through the wheat harvest, and Boaz's generosity models uncut corners.[^10]

*Read Ruth 2:1–3 aloud.*

What do you notice about how Ruth's survival depends on someone else's obedience?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** Marty's teacher Ray put it bluntly: "If there are no uncut corners in your celebration of Jesus, you are not Pentecostal. I don't care how many of you are speaking in tongues."[^11] Our traditions understand spiritual gifts differently, and Ray's line is not settling that question. What does it say counts as evidence of the Spirit? How does that land in your own tradition?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q11:** The notes describe Acts 2:42–47 as "the ideal Pentecostal community": shared possessions, property sold for those in need, daily meals in homes.[^12] Look at your own calendar and budget for the coming month. What is one "corner" you could deliberately leave uncut for someone else?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Luke tells the ascension against the backdrop of Caesar's comet, so that Jesus, not the emperor, is the Son of God who sends ambassadors to the ends of the earth. Pentecost then arrives at Shavuot as a second Sinai: fire, sound, God's word, and 3,000 lives redeemed rather than lost. The people who receive the Spirit are Jews returning to their covenant, not leaving it. And the festival itself carries a test: a celebration of Jesus that leaves no corners for the alien, the orphan and the widow has missed the point.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each: where will you leave an uncut corner this week?

</div>
