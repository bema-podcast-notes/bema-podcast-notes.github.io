### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We met Hebrews as a sermon for Jewish believers after the temple's destruction, bracketed by the line "He sat down at the right hand of God" (1:3 and 10:12). Inside that bracket, Jesus is the merciful and faithful high priest who shows us how to persevere through suffering.

**This week:** We stay in Hebrews and ask the question it answers more directly than any other New Testament book: what did Jesus' death accomplish, and for whom?

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode surveys the major ways Christians have explained the atonement over the centuries: the ransom theory, Christus Victor, penal substitution, the moral exemplar view, solidarity, and René Girard's scapegoat theory. Marty argues that each one grew out of the world its teachers lived in (persecution, war, courtrooms, modern skepticism), and that each is useful in its place, like the clubs in a golf bag. He is especially critical of leaning on penal substitution alone.

The heart of the episode is Hebrews' repeated claim that the old sacrifices could not clear the worshiper's conscience, while Jesus' sacrifice does. In Marty's reading, God never needed sacrifice; we did. Along the way he offers an iPhone analogy for old and new covenant and shows Jesus serving in Hebrews as temple, priest, and sacrifice all at once. People in our room were taught different things about the cross, so come ready to listen as much as to speak.

### Clubs in the Bag <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#clubs-in-the-bag}

*Christians have told the story of the cross in several ways, and the episode argues that each telling carries the fingerprints of its age.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The episode walks through six theories and the settings they came from: a persecuted early church that heard Jesus as a ransom paid for captives, a medieval world of plague and crusade that celebrated Christ as victor over death, Reformation-era lawyer-theologians like John Calvin who framed the cross as a legal substitution, twentieth-century liberal theology's moral example, Tony Jones's solidarity theory, and René Girard's scapegoat.[^1] Which of these had you heard of before, and which were new? What do you notice about how each one fits the world it came from?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes point to C.S. Lewis's *The Lion, the Witch, and the Wardrobe* as a picture of the ransom theory: Aslan's deal with the White Witch, the "deep magic," and a resurrection that leaves her empty-handed. The episode also names that theory's problems: it puts power in Satan's hands and makes God look deceptive.[^2] What other stories, songs, or sermon illustrations carry one of these theories without naming it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Marty borrows Scot McKnight's image: each atonement theory is like a golf club, and you would not use a putter to drive off the tee. Forcing penal substitution to explain everything is like playing a whole round with one club.[^3] The episode's sharpest concern is a popular version that pictures an "Angry Father, nice Jesus, helper Holy Spirit," which Marty says "destroys Trinity doctrine."[^4] Several of our traditions have taught penal substitution as central. What has that way of telling the story done well for you or your church? Where, if anywhere, have you seen it slide into the picture Marty warns about?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Think of different moments in a person's life: grief, addiction, shame, injustice, doubt. Which "club" has helped you most in a particular season, and which might help someone you love who is struggling right now?

</div>

### What the Blood Is For <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#what-the-blood-is-for}

*Hebrews keeps returning to one word that changes the whole conversation: conscience.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Hebrews 9:11–14 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes count roughly five times that Hebrews says the old sacrifices could not "clear the conscience of the worshiper." Animal blood gave outward cleansing but not inner peace, while Jesus' sacrifice "cleanses our consciences from acts that lead to death."[^5] Read the passage again quietly. What words describe what the old sacrifices did, and what words describe what Christ's blood does? Report what you find.

*Read Hebrews 10:1–10 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The episode draws a key insight from Hebrews 10: God commanded sacrifices but does not need or want them, as the Psalms and Prophets also say. In the notes' words, "God doesn't need sacrifice - we do." God could simply forgive, but people would keep their guilty consciences, so the sacrificial system was God's provision for us rather than a payment to satisfy him.[^6] What in this passage supports that reading? How does it sit with what you were taught, and what questions does it raise for you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say the Levitical system "actually reminded people of sin annually rather than removing guilt."[^7] Where have you seen religious practices that keep reminding people of their failures without ever letting them feel clean?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** If the point of the cross is that your conscience can actually be cleansed, what is one thing you still carry as though it had never been dealt with? You do not have to name it aloud. What would it look like to live this week as someone whose conscience has been made clean?

</div>

### Temple, Priest, and Sacrifice <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#temple-priest-and-sacrifice}

*Hebrews pictures Jesus filling every role in the old system at once, and the episode offers two images for how old and new relate.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes say Jesus serves in Hebrews as temple (where God meets people), high priest (the one who offers), and sacrifice (the offering that cleanses), and that this combination is why his work is "once for all" rather than endlessly repeated.[^8] The episode also expects Girard's scapegoat theory, which draws on the Levitical scapegoat, to shape evangelical theology in coming decades.[^9]

*Read Leviticus 16:20–22 aloud.*

What do you notice about what happens to the scapegoat and to the people watching? How does that picture connect to a cleansed conscience?

*Read Hebrews 8:13 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty compares the old covenant to an iPhone 5 and the new to an iPhone 11. The older phone is not junk; it holds technology the newer one depends on, though nobody goes back once they have the new one. The notes add that "obsolete" would be better translated "old": the covenant aged, it was not discarded.[^10] Last week reminded us to read Hebrews' "better" language without contempt for Judaism. How does "old" rather than "obsolete" change the way this verse sounds to you? Where does the analogy help, and where might it fall short?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The church has told the story of the cross in many ways, and each telling grew up in a particular world. Hebrews holds several of those threads together, presenting Jesus as temple, priest, and sacrifice. Its steady concern is not a God who needs appeasing but people whose consciences need cleansing. God gave sacrifice for our sake, and in Jesus that gift is complete. We can hold our favorite "club" with gratitude while staying humble before a mystery larger than any one theory.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each: which picture of the cross do you want to carry with you this week, and why?

</div>
