### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Genesis closed with Judah offering his own life for Benjamin and Joseph choosing forgiveness over vengeance. The notes call forgiveness "one of, if not THE, ultimate expression of trust": laying down the right to revenge and trusting God to own the story.[^1]

**This week:** We cross from Genesis into Exodus, but the episode steps back from the narrative to ask two questions. Who does Moses think he is, and what kind of God stands behind the stories we usually call "wrath"?[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode begins with a strange little story. In Exodus 4:24–26, God seems ready to kill Moses, and Zipporah circumcises their son and calls Moses a "bridegroom of blood." Marty treats it as a *hagah* project, a chance to "growl" over a text like a lion over its prey until it gives up its meaning. The thread he follows is Moses' identity: a Hebrew who bears the sign of the covenant but was raised in Pharaoh's house, who carries survivor's guilt, and whose excuse of being "slow of speech" turns into being "uncircumcised of lips." God never argues with those insecurities. In the notes' words, God is interested in availability and trust, not qualifications.[^3]

The second half traces four Hebrew words that appear in both the story of Sodom and Gomorrah and the Exodus. Together they suggest that these "wrath" stories are really stories of God hearing the cry of the oppressed and acting to put things right. The notes contrast *mishpat* (restorative justice) with *diyn* (retributive justice).[^4] Our traditions have taught God's judgment in different ways, so this guide asks each of us to describe what we were taught and notice what this reading adds, rather than to win an argument.

### A Prince Who Was Supposed to Die <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#a-prince-who-was-supposed-to-die}

*Moses' reluctance makes more sense once we see the identity he was carrying.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Exodus 4:24–26 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes describe the Hebrew idea of *hagah*, a word that imitates a lion's low growl over its prey, as a way of staying with a text and asking better and better questions instead of accepting a surface reading.[^5] Marty's *hagah* questions for this passage include: Why does God show up to kill Moses? How does Zipporah know exactly what to do? Why is Moses' son not carrying the sign of the covenant? Brent adds that it is simply "weird" that Zipporah touches "his feet" with her son's foreskin.[^6] What stands out to you in these three verses, and what question would you add to the list?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes sketch Moses' experience: born to Hebrew parents, circumcised on the eighth day, raised in Pharaoh's household, living with survivor's guilt because he should have died with the other babies, and watching his people suffer while he enjoyed Egyptian privilege. Marty doubts that a real speech impediment would have survived an upbringing in the most powerful house in the world. The notes read the uncircumcised son as another sign that Moses does not see himself as a member of God's people.[^7] If that reading is right, what does this odd story reveal about what was really holding Moses back?

*Read Exodus 6:10–13 and 6:28–30 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The phrase translated "faltering lips" in 6:12 reads in Hebrew as "uncircumcised of lips," and the same language returns in 6:30. Between the two, the text inserts a genealogy. The notes see a small chiasm here: the genealogy sits in the middle, proving that Moses is the Hebrew he struggles to believe he is.[^8] Why might the writer answer Moses' insecurity with a family tree rather than a pep talk?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes observe that God never argues with Moses about his insecurities but meets him where he is, and then summarize: "God’s not interested in your qualifications. God is interested in your availability. God is interested in your trust."[^9] The notes also point out that many people today feel caught between worlds (cultural, religious or social) in ways that make them feel disqualified.[^10] Where do you feel most "caught between worlds" right now, and what would it look like to offer God your availability there this week?

</div>

### Four Words and a Cry <span class="guide-time">35 min</span> {#four-words-and-a-cry}

*Two stories usually filed under "wrath" turn out to share a vocabulary of oppression and rescue.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 18:20–21 and Exodus 3:7–9 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes list *tsa'aqah*, the "cry" of the oppressed, as appearing at Genesis 18:21 and 19:13 and at Exodus 3:7 and 3:9.[^11] Listen to both passages side by side. What do you notice about what God is responding to in each one, and about what God says he will do?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes name three more words that tie the two stories together: *chathan* ("bridegroom" or "son-in-law," found only four times in the Torah, twice in each story), *rasha* ("wicked," the wickedness that causes oppression), and *shaphat* ("to judge," in a restorative sense).[^12] Zipporah's "bridegroom of blood" turns out to be one of those four occurrences. What happens to your picture of Sodom and Gomorrah when you read it as a companion to the Exodus rather than as a separate story about punishment?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes contrast *mishpat*, restorative justice that aims to make things right and restore *shalom* (peace, wholeness), with *diyn*, retributive justice focused on punishment. They illustrate it with a parent who steps in when children are fighting, mainly to protect the one being hurt and restore the household's peace.[^13] They conclude that God's "wrath" stories are "stories of rescue, not punishment."[^14] Our traditions have taught God's judgment in different ways, and some of us may feel this reading challenges something important. Without trying to settle it tonight, how were you taught to understand God's judgment in these stories, and what does the restorative lens add, clarify or unsettle for you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes suggest that understanding God as the one who "hears the cry" invites us into justice work as a way of sharing God's character.[^15] Whose cry is within earshot of your ordinary week (at work, in your neighborhood, in your own family), and what would it take for you to actually hear it?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Exodus opens with a reluctant prince. Moses has every qualification for the job and still feels disqualified, because he is not sure he belongs to the people he is being sent to save. God does not argue with him; God waits for his availability. Behind the story stands a God whose "wrath" looks different once we listen closely: in Sodom and in Egypt alike, God hears the cry of the oppressed and moves to put things right.[^16]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, where is God asking for your availability rather than your qualifications?

</div>
