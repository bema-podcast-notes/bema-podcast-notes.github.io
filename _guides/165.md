### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Paul asked Philemon to receive Onesimus as a brother, appealing to "faith works" (doing right because it is good) rather than "law works" (doing right from fear of consequences).[^1]

**This week:** We leave Paul's letters and open Hebrews, a sermon for Jewish believers trying to remain faithful after the temple was gone.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode introduces Hebrews as a synagogue homily, likely written after the temple's destruction in AD 70 "to address Judaism without the temple from a Jesus perspective." The author is unknown, but the notes describe a probable "Alexandrian-trained Jewish believer, possibly Priscilla, writing to other Jews."[^2] The book's argument is framed by an inclusio, a pair of literary brackets: the phrase "He sat down at the right hand of God" appears at 1:3 and again at 10:12, and between them the author builds the case that Jesus is a merciful and faithful high priest.[^3]

The episode is careful about how to hear the book's repeated word "better." Jesus is presented not as a replacement for Jewish faith but as "a 'better version' of everything in the Levitical system," and the notes describe Hebrews as Jewish believers helping other Jewish believers through a crisis, "not as replacement theology."[^4] The book's main exhortation is perseverance: Jesus learned through suffering, and his people are called to run their race with endurance and to love those on the fringes.[^5]

**Opening Question:** Think of a time you lost something that had been central to your faith or your community: a church building, a congregation, a leader, a practice. What helped you keep going afterward?

### A Sermon After the Temple <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#a-sermon-after-the-temple}

*Hebrews was written to people whose center of worship had been destroyed.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes say that after AD 70 "both Judaism and Jewish believers had to reimagine their faith without the central temple structure." They describe a parallel: rabbinic Judaism "replaced temple worship with Torah obedience and righteous living," while Jewish believers "found the same replacement in Jesus as the ultimate sacrifice and high priest."[^6] Imagine you are a Jewish believer in a synagogue the year after the temple fell. What questions would you be carrying into that gathering?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> *Read Hebrews 1:1–4 aloud.* The notes say "recent scholarship" places Hebrews after the temple's destruction and explains the present-tense language about the temple and priesthood as reflecting "the ongoing reality of Jewish worship patterns rather than indicating the temple still stood."[^7] How does hearing these opening verses as words to a grieving community change their tone for you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes say Hebrews shows how Jesus "provides a better version of everything they held dear" while calling them "to persevere through suffering just as Jesus did."[^8] When you think back to the loss you named at the opening, what did you need someone to tell you in that season?

</div>

### Inside the Brackets: A Better High Priest <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#inside-the-brackets-a-better-high-priest}

*The author marks off the main argument with a repeated phrase, and at its center is a high priest.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Hebrews 1:3 and 10:11–14 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** What phrase appears in both passages? The notes explain that an inclusio is a "literary bracketing tool that identifies an argument within literature," and that the identical phrase at 1:3 and 10:12 "signals readers to pay careful attention to the theological argument contained within these brackets."[^9] The notes also trace a chain of "therefore" statements inside: a better messenger, better humanity, a better Moses, a better Sabbath, and a better high priest.[^10] Why might a preacher bracket an argument this way for listeners who could not flip back through a book?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** *Read Hebrews 4:14–5:10 aloud.* What qualities of Jesus as high priest does this passage name? The notes summarize them as "merciful," able to "deal gently, sympathize with weakness," and "faithful," "made perfect through suffering," and they explain that the order of Melchizedek solves "the problem of Jesus not being from the tribe of Levi."[^11] Which of those qualities would have mattered most to a community that had just lost its priesthood?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes offer examples of "better version, not replacement": Moses was an amazing leader, but Jesus was everything Moses was "and then some"; Jesus is "the best high priest you could ever imagine"; Torah was a wonderful gift, and Jesus makes it "even better and more clear."[^12] They also call for "careful theological work on how to read Hebrews' 'better' language without falling into anti-Semitic interpretations."[^13] How have you heard Hebrews used to talk about Jews and Judaism? What changes when "better" is heard as Jewish believers speaking to other Jewish believers rather than as Gentiles speaking about them?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes say Jesus is "able to empathize with human weakness because he suffered."[^14] What weakness or struggle are you carrying this week that you would bring to a high priest who has been there too?

</div>

### Running the Race, Outside the Gates <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#running-the-race-outside-the-gates}

*After the argument comes the exhortation: endure, together, and go out.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Hebrews 10:23–25, 10:32–36 and 12:1–3 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** What resources for endurance do you find in these passages? The notes name three: memory of past faithfulness ("Remember those earlier days...when you endured in a great conflict full of suffering"), community support ("Not giving up meeting together...but encouraging one another"), and confidence in better promises ("You knew that you yourselves had better and lasting possessions").[^15] Which of these has carried you through a hard stretch, and how?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> *Read Hebrews 13:11–16 aloud.* The notes say the audience "likely still kept kosher and wore tassels," but that "true faithfulness goes beyond following rules to loving others, especially outsiders and those on the fringes," summed up in the call, "Let us go out beyond the city gates."[^16] What do you think it meant for these believers to go out to where Jesus suffered, and what would it mean for a church today?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes describe the call to go outside the city gates as one that "challenges comfortable religion, pushing us toward the margins where love meets real need."[^17] Who is outside the gates in your town or your circle, and what is one way you could go out to them this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Hebrews is a sermon for people who lost the center of their world when the temple fell. Its author, a Jewish believer writing to other Jewish believers, does not tell them to abandon what they loved. The sermon shows them Jesus as a better version of it: a better Moses, a better Sabbath, and a merciful and faithful high priest who learned through suffering. Between the brackets of "he sat down at the right hand of God" stands that high priest, and after them comes the call to run with endurance, to keep meeting together, and to go out beyond the city gates.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: who is one person, living or from Scripture, in the cloud of witnesses that helps you keep running?

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 166 — Hebrews — Atonement 101](/guides/166)** (45:47): The episode surveys theories of atonement that have developed through Christian history, including ransom, Christus Victor, penal substitution, moral exemplar, solidarity and scapegoat, and shows how each reflects its historical setting. It argues that Hebrews is the New Testament book that most directly addresses atonement, and that its focus is the cleansing of the conscience, with Jesus as temple, priest and sacrifice at once.[^18] Our traditions explain the cross in different ways, so this will be a week for listening carefully.

**What to bring next week:** Write down, in two sentences, how you were taught to explain why Jesus died. Then read Hebrews 9:11–14 and 10:1–4 and mark every time the word "conscience" appears.
