### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** In Matthew 18, Jesus called us to keep conflict circles small and to "out-forgive evil," and the parable of the unforgiving servant suggested that a person who refuses to forgive may be holding the key to their own prison.

**This week:** Matthew 19 takes up three subjects that touch people's lives directly: divorce, children, and money. The thread running through all three is Jesus' care for the people his culture pushed to the margins.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode opens with one of the major rabbinic debates of Jesus' day. The schools of Shammai and Hillel disagreed about when divorce was permitted, and the disagreement hinged on the order of two Hebrew words in Deuteronomy 24:1. The episode argues that Jesus took Shammai's strict position for Hillel's reason, love, and that his real concern was protecting women who could be discarded and left without provision.

The chapter then turns to children, whom the disciples treat as interruptions, and to a rich young man whose list of commandments contains a revealing gap. It closes with Jesus' promise that the disciples will "judge" the twelve tribes, which the episode reads as restoring rather than condemning. Divorce is a tender subject, and some in any group carry it personally. Please speak with care, and remember that no one needs to share more than they choose.

### Two Schools and a Naked Thing <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#two-schools-and-a-naked-thing}

*A debate about two words becomes a question about who gets protected.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes describe two "yokes," or interpretive lenses, from the rabbis Shammai and Hillel. Shammai's was a yoke of obedience: what makes God's heart happy is obedience. Hillel's was a yoke of love, with love of neighbor as the second greatest commandment.[^1] Hillel's school even required divorcing couples to use the court on the opposite side of town, so they had to walk through a community that would try to talk them out of it.[^2] Which of these two lenses do you tend to reach for first when you read a hard command? Where did you learn it?

*Read Deuteronomy 24:1–4 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode explains that the debate turned on two Hebrew words, *ervat* ("nakedness") and *davar* ("thing"). Read as *davar ervat*, "a thing of nakedness," the verse allowed divorce only for sexual immorality (Shammai). Read as *ervat davar*, "a naked thing," it meant anything out of place (Hillel), as in his famous line about a wife who burns the biscuits.[^3] The notes say different manuscript traditions had the words in different orders, making this "a legitimate rabbinical debate."[^4] What does it do for you to learn that faithful teachers in Jesus' day disagreed over the wording of the text itself?

*Read Matthew 19:3–9 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes observe that the Pharisees' question, "Can we divorce our wives for any and every reason?", reveals their perspective: "The woman was merely a prop in their question." Jesus reframes the conversation around the woman, who in that culture "would be left without provision, belonging, or care."[^5] As you listen to the passage, whose voice is present and whose is absent? What changes when Jesus places the responsibility on the man?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The episode argues that Jesus sided with Shammai's strict position "but for Hillel's reason: love and protection of vulnerable people."[^6] It also names how the church has often used this teaching to "heap shame and guilt on divorced individuals, especially women," committing the very sin Jesus fought against, and calls instead for helping divorced people find healing while recognizing that "reconciliation requires two willing parties."[^7] Our traditions have taught and practiced this passage in different ways. Without judging anyone's church, how has this text been used in communities you have known, and what would it look like for your community to use it as protection rather than condemnation?

</div>

### Children, a Rich Young Man, and a Missing Commandment <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#children-a-rich-young-man-and-a-missing-commandment}

*Jesus keeps making room for the people others overlook, and keeps unsettling the people others envy.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 19:13–15 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The disciples treat the children as interruptions to important "rabbi business." The notes suggest a possible connection to Matthew 18: the disciples may have seen the child Jesus placed among them as "merely a teaching prop," and here Jesus shows that the Kingdom is made up of real people like these children.[^8] Who are the people in your world who tend to get treated as interruptions or as props for someone else's point?

*Read Matthew 19:16–22 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Listen to which commandments Jesus lists. The notes point out that he names the sixth, seventh, eighth, and ninth, then the fifth (honor your father and mother), and leaves out the tenth (do not covet). Drawing on Rabbi David Fohrman, the episode explains that the two tablets parallel each other, so the fifth pairs with the tenth. The fifth is "essentially about being satisfied with the life you have been given," and the tenth is about not wanting someone else's life.[^9] What do you notice about the list once you see the gap? Why might the fifth commandment, and not the tenth, be the one this young man needed to hear?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes say the camel and the eye of the needle is "Not a reference to a small gate in Jerusalem," a popular but historically unfounded idea, and means what it says.[^10] Jesus' call to sell and give echoes Abraham and Isaac, wealthy families who practiced radical hospitality and generosity.[^11] Many of us have heard the gate explanation. What do you think made that explanation so appealing? What would radical generosity look like in your own household this year?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The rich young man had everything others coveted. The notes frame his real challenge as "whether he could be satisfied with the life he had."[^12] Where in your life is it hardest to be satisfied with what you have been given?

</div>

### Thrones and Trust <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#thrones-and-trust}

*Judging means putting things right, and none of this is possible unless we trust the story.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 19:27–30 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The promise that the disciples will sit on twelve thrones "judging" the tribes uses the idea of *shaphat*, "to judge," which the notes define as "to put things right, restore, bring justice." The same root gives *shofetim* (the Judges who delivered Israel) and *mishpat*, "justice, restoration, putting the world back together."[^13] The "hundred times as much" may also hint at Isaac, who received a hundredfold harvest.[^14] How does hearing "judge" as "put things right" change your picture of what the disciples were promised?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes gather the whole chapter under one principle with a series of questions: "How can you forgive without trusting the story? How can you protect the vulnerable in divorce without trusting the story? How can you sell everything without trusting the story?" They add that trust is cultivated through practices like Shabbat, which "repeatedly tells us the truth."[^15] Which of those questions feels most personal to you right now? What practice helps you trust the story enough to let go of self-preservation?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Matthew 19 is about people. In a debate that treated a wife as a prop, Jesus took the strict position for a loving reason, so that women would not be discarded and left without care. He welcomed children the disciples saw as interruptions, and he challenged a wealthy man to be satisfied with his life and generous with it. The disciples' future work is to put the world back together. Underneath every one of these teachings is the same question: do we trust the story enough to let go of protecting ourselves?
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: who is one person on the margins you want to see more clearly this week?

</div>
