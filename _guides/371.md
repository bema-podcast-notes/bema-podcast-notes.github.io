### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Isaiah 28–33 asked God's own people where their trust lies, setting Egypt's horses and chariots against a kingdom that grows as God's people become "a shelter from the wind" for others.[^1]

**This week:** Turn one page and Isaiah is back to the hard reality that people keep saying no; Marty and Brent take up God's anger and its "flip side" in Isaiah 34–35.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Using the Hulk's line "You won't like me when I'm angry" as a hook, Marty and Brent look at the reality of God's anger. Isaiah 34 summons every nation and its armies and describes devastating judgment focused on Edom, using language that undoes creation. Isaiah 35 then shows the other side of the same coin: the desert blooms, the weak are encouraged, and the redeemed return to Zion singing. The hosts argue that God's anger is not blind rage. It comes from his love for shalom, it is aroused by injustice, and it aims at restoration.[^3]

The hosts also notice how heavily these chapters echo Genesis and Exodus, and Marty admits that a comfortable life once gave him a one-dimensional view of God's wrath. The episode ends with an unplanned review of Isaiah 1–39, summed up as "a father disciplining his sons." Some questions tonight touch on injustice and harm; share only what you choose to.[^4]

**Opening Question:** Think of a time you saw someone get angry on behalf of another person: a parent, a teacher, a friend. What made that anger feel protective rather than selfish?

### Where God's Anger Comes From <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#where-gods-anger-comes-from}

*In the Torah, God's anger rises when the powerless cry out.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Exodus 22:21–27 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Marty roots the discussion in this passage, where God warns Israel not to mistreat the widow, the foreigner, or the poor, and not to keep a poor person's cloak overnight, because "when they cry out to me, I will hear."[^5] What do you notice about what sets off God's anger here, and on whose behalf?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe God's anger as "relational and protective." It "starts from **shalom**," and it is "**not** set off simply by people breaking rules. It responds to the harm that disobedience does to people and to creation."[^6] How is that different from the picture of God's anger you grew up with? What difference does the distinction make?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Marty admits that a comfortable life led him to see God's anger in a one-dimensional way. People who had been on the receiving end of abuse showed him "that a God who gets angry is a God who sees them." He adds that deconstructing distorted views of wrath is good, but throwing out God's anger altogether "would be a serious overcorrection."[^7] Whose voices have helped you see a blind spot in how you picture God? What might change if you listened to people whose social position is different from yours?

</div>

### All Nations, All Armies, and Creation Undone <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#all-nations-all-armies-and-creation-undone}

*Isaiah 34 condemns the whole world's commitment to violence, not one side of a conflict.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 34:1–4 aloud. Then read Isaiah 34:8–17 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The passage summons "all nations" and says, "His wrath is on all their armies." Brent notes that this narrows the focus: "The guilt lies with the machinery of violence, not with every individual." The notes also observe that God "gives them over to slaughter," which can be read as nations destroying one another through their own violence.[^8] What do you notice about who is addressed, and what is named as the target?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** In verse 11 Isaiah uses *tohu va-vohu* ("formless and void"), the phrase from Genesis 1:2. The NIV renders it "the measuring line of chaos and the plumb line of desolation." The notes observe that measuring lines and plumb lines are building tools, here used to **build destruction**: "the world's way of solving problems does not move toward Eden. It moves back toward chaos."[^9] Where do you see "building tools" used to build destruction, in the world or closer to home?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty returns to Heschel's principle, "Few Are Guilty, All Are Responsible": guilt belongs to those who commit the violence, but responsibility belongs to everyone, since "each of us has to decide which story we will give ourselves to."[^10] What does it look like to take responsibility for violence you did not commit?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The hosts recorded during the conflicts in Israel and Gaza and in Ukraine and Russia, and they say the passage "challenges the assumption that God has favorite nations he backs in war." The notes invite us "to stop casting God as a supporter of 'our side' in any conflict and to grieve all violence carried out by 'all their armies.'"[^11] Without debating any specific conflict, where do you feel the pull to put God on "our side," whether in politics, in church, or in a family dispute? What would grieving all the violence ask of you?

</div>

### The Flip Side of Anger <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#the-flip-side-of-anger}

*Isaiah 35 shows where God's anger is aimed: home, healing, and new life.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 35 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes list the chapter's movements: the desert blooms, the feeble hands are strengthened, the blind see and the lame leap, water breaks out in the wilderness, and a highway called the Way of Holiness carries the ransomed home. In verse 4, "vengeance and salvation come in the same breath." The JPS notes see Exodus echoes here, just as Isaiah 34 echoed Genesis.[^12] Which Exodus echoes did you hear? Why might God's rescue be described in the language of the first exodus?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Robert Alter's note on 35:3 says the call to take heart "speaks to the condition of exiles." The notes conclude, "For exiles, 'God is coming with vengeance' is comfort, not a threat."[^13] Who in our world might hear Isaiah 35:4 as good news, and why might comfortable readers hear it differently?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** Marty sums up Isaiah 1–39 as "a father disciplining his sons": loving and merciful, but out of patience "because his children are hurting other people and his creation." His road-trip image is a parent driving to Disneyland who warns, "I'm going to pull this van over." The destination doesn't change, "but behavior decides how the trip goes."[^14] Where in your life right now do you need to hear that God has not given up on the destination, but that the way there matters?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Isaiah 34 is hard to read, but it is not a picture of an irritable God losing his temper. It is a verdict on the world's addiction to violence, with every nation and every army included and creation coming undone as a result. Isaiah 35 shows where God's anger is aimed: deserts in bloom, the weak strengthened, and a highway home. For people crying out under injustice, a God who does not get angry would be no comfort at all. Anger and restoration belong to the same story.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, name one cry of the vulnerable that you want to listen for more carefully this week.

</div>
