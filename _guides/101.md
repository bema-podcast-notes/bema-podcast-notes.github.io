### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Coming down from the mountain, Jesus touched a leper, honored a Roman centurion, and healed until evening. Being unclean was never the same as being sinful, and following Jesus turned out to cost more than a couple of hours a day.

**This week:** Jesus tells his disciples to get in the boat and cross to the pagan side of the lake. The storm, the demon named Legion, and the healed man's commission all push the same question: where will we follow Jesus?

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The Sea of Galilee divided the Jewish towns of Capernaum, Bethsaida, and Chorazin from the Decapolis, pagan territory on the eastern shore. The episode argues that crossing over was "a stunning, shocking move" and that the disciples resisted it. It explains why a large body of water represented chaos and the abyss, how Jesus sleeping on a cushion echoes Jonah (another reluctant mission to Gentiles), and how the disciples answer the calmed storm with words from the Psalms.

On the other side, a man rejected by everyone meets Jesus. The demon's name, Legion, carries political weight, and the townspeople ask Jesus to leave. Then Jesus refuses the healed man's request to follow him and sends him home with only his story. The episode traces that story to a later Christian community in Hippos. Come ready to think about your own story.

### Crossing into Chaos <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#crossing-into-chaos}

*Getting in the boat meant crossing a cultural line, and the water itself meant chaos.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Mark 4:35–41 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes explain that the Sea of Galilee separated the Jewish "triangle" of towns from the Decapolis, and that later rabbinic tradition said even uttering the word "Decapolis" made a person unclean for seven days.[^1] The original notes add that Jesus' choice to take his disciples across was "a stunning, shocking move" and that "Most Jews wouldn't be caught dead there."[^2] What details in the reading hint at the disciples' reluctance? How might they have felt about this trip before the storm even began?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** In Jewish thought, the notes say, flowing water from springs or rain (*maim chaim*, "living water") represented life, but large bodies of water like the Sea of Galilee represented chaos, the abyss, or the underworld, going back to Genesis 1.[^3] The episode also hears a deliberate echo of Jonah: Jonah slept on a cushion on his way to pagan Nineveh, and Jesus sleeps on a cushion on his way to the pagan Decapolis.[^4]

*Read Jonah 1:1–6 aloud.*

Lay the two stories side by side. What parallels do you notice, and what do they suggest Jesus was trying to teach his disciples about where they were going?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** When Jesus says "Quiet, be still," the notes say he is using language from Psalm 107:23–32 and Psalm 89:9, and the disciples' response, "Who is this? Even the wind and waves obey him," echoes Psalm 89's refrain, "Who is like the Lord?" The episode argues the disciples "are not bumbling idiots but well-trained Jewish students."[^5]

*Read Psalm 107:23–32 and Psalm 89:5–9 aloud.*

What changes in the story if the disciples are reciting Scripture back to their rabbi in the middle of a crisis? Why do you think the disciples are so often portrayed as clueless?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q4:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes observe that the disciples seemed content once they "got the lesson" about Jesus' authority, but Jesus was taking them on a mission to actual people: "Sometimes we're content with intellectual understanding when God is calling us to uncomfortable obedience."[^6] Where are you more comfortable understanding the lesson than going to the other side?

</div>

### Among the Cast Out Ones <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#among-the-cast-out-ones}

*On the far shore waits a man rejected by every society around him.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Mark 5:1–17 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes suggest that the manuscript variations (Gerasenes, Gadarenes, Gergesenes) may all be trying to translate a Hebrew term, *Gerusim*, "the cast out ones," which referred to the seven nations cast out of Canaan in Joshua 3:10.[^7] The man himself was rejected by Jewish society and by Greco-Roman society, lived among tombs, broke every chain, and cut himself with stones.[^8] What do you notice about how Mark describes him, and about how quickly he and Jesus meet?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes point out that the 10th Roman Legion was stationed just north of Susita (Hippos) and that its standard was a boar. So demons named Legion enter pigs and are sent into the sea, the abyss.[^9] How does this political layer change the way you hear the story? What might it have meant to people living under Roman occupation?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** After the miracle, the townspeople ask Jesus to leave. The notes say the Kingdom threatened both their economy (two thousand pigs) and their social structures, since the healed man no longer fit in his assigned box. "This mirrors modern resistance to the Kingdom - we prefer comfort and economic security over God's transformative work."[^10] Where do you see the Kingdom threatening comfortable arrangements today, in a community or in your own life? How do people politely ask Jesus to leave?

</div>

### Go Home and Tell <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#go-home-and-tell}

*Jesus turns down the perfect recruit and sends him home with nothing but his story.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Mark 5:18–20 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes call this man the perfect candidate for discipleship: no family, job, or community ties. Yet Jesus refuses and sends him home. He had perhaps an hour or two with Jesus and "no theological training, no Bible knowledge."[^11] The original notes put it simply: "Marty will argue we should know our text better BUT even when we don't, a story is still enough."[^12] Why do you think Jesus sent him home instead of taking him along?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The episode traces what happened next. When Jesus later returns to the Decapolis, 4,000 people come to meet him. Hippos eventually had seven churches and a cathedral, and according to church history its bishop helped pen the Nicene Creed.[^13] Our traditions relate to creeds in different ways, but every tradition in the room cares about passing on the faith. What strikes you about a line that runs from one untrained man's story to a whole region?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The original notes say, "All you need is a story," and add that some people think their story is pretty normal, and some think they don't have a story, but "Everyone has a story."[^14] Look at what you wrote this week. What is the part of your story where Jesus moved? Who in your own "Decapolis" might need to hear it?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Jesus did not stay where it was comfortable. He put his disciples in a boat, crossed the waters of chaos, and walked into the land of the cast out ones, echoing a reluctant prophet sent to pagans long before. There he freed a man no society wanted, and the town asked him to leave because the Kingdom was bad for business. Then Jesus sent that man home with only his story, and his story was enough.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each: where is your "other side," the place or people you would least like to follow Jesus to?

</div>
