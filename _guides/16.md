### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Joseph's second coat, his dreams and his brothers' hatred landed him in a pit, and the word "recognize" kept exposing a family caught in deception. The notes left us asking whether a story spinning out of control could find its way back.[^1]

**This week:** It does. Joseph's pattern of pits is reversed before Pharaoh, Judah offers his own life for his youngest brother, and Genesis ends with forgiveness.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This episode completes Joseph's story and the book of Genesis. The hosts trace a template that repeats in Joseph's life: gifts, dreams, a coat stripped away, and a pit. It happens with his brothers and again with Potiphar, and then before Pharaoh it runs backward: out of the pit, new clothes, dreams interpreted, gifts received. Drawing on Rabbi David Fohrman, they suggest Joseph can read Pharaoh's dream because it echoes his own family's story.

The turning point of the family's story, though, belongs to Judah. Shaped by his encounter with Tamar, he offers his own life as a guarantee for Benjamin, and that act sets up Joseph's choice between vengeance and forgiveness. Elle explains that forgiveness does not excuse or minimize evil; it rests on trust that God can bring good out of it. The notes call forgiveness one of, if not the, ultimate expression of trust in God's story.

### Out of the Pit <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#out-of-the-pit}

*Joseph is pulled out of the dungeon to interpret Pharaoh's dreams, and the shape of his story turns around.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 41:14–16 and 41:25–40 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes put Joseph's response simply: "Joseph takes the bad news and tells Pharaoh that the news is bad but we can do something about it. We are going to get through it." They call this his *chutzpah* (bold confidence and determination), the same fire his fathers had, and say "This is why G-d picks this family."[^3] As you listen, what do you notice about what Joseph says about God, what he says about the famine, and what he proposes?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes lay out a template: gifts, dreams, coat stripped, into the pit, first with his brothers and then with Potiphar. With Pharaoh it reverses: Joseph is taken out of the pit, clothes are put on him, and he interprets someone else's dreams before receiving gifts and authority. They add: "The word 'pit' is not used to refer to a dungeon except in the story of Joseph."[^4] What changes when the pattern runs backward, and what might the storyteller want us to see in it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Fohrman notes that the cows grazed among the reeds, and the Hebrew word for "reeds" (*ahu*) shares roots with the word for "brothers." Using a midrash from Akiva, if seven means "years," the dream interprets itself and takes Joseph back to Leah and Rachel, the plain and the beautiful, for whom Jacob worked seven years each. The notes conclude, "This is a story about Joseph's own life."[^5] They also imagine Joseph in prison, wondering why his father never came looking for him.[^6] How might Pharaoh's dream have forced Joseph to face his own story?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes describe the *chutzpah* that made young Joseph annoying becoming, after suffering, a tool for saving lives.[^7] What is a trait in you that has caused trouble, and what might it look like for God to refine it rather than remove it?

</div>

### Judah Steps Forward <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#judah-steps-forward}

*The family's reconciliation hinges on a brother willing to put his own life on the line.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 43:1–14 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes describe Reuben trying to act as the firstborn by offering his two sons to Jacob, who refuses. Judah, instead of offering his children, offers his own life. The notes ask whether Judah learned something about restorative justice from Tamar, and they observe that this is what moves "Jacob" to become "Israel" in the story.[^8] Listen for which name the text uses for the father, before and after Judah speaks. What do you notice, and what might the shift suggest?

*Read Genesis 44:18–34 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes see Judah's single act of sacrifice for Benjamin setting up a story in which Judah and Benjamin keep saving each other later in Scripture, pointing to David and Jonathan and to the book of Esther.[^9] Hearing Judah's speech, what stands out to you about the man who once suggested selling Joseph?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes name Judah, not Joseph, as the true hero here, because his willingness to take responsibility catalyzes the family's reconciliation.[^10] When has someone taking responsibility (or your own taking responsibility) opened a door to reconciliation that seemed shut?

</div>

### What You Meant for Evil <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#what-you-meant-for-evil}

*Joseph must decide who he is, and the book that began with creation ends with forgiveness.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 45:1–8 and 50:15–21 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Elle explains that forgiveness does not excuse or minimize evil. It is grounded in trust that God can transform any situation, and it requires being "grounded in God's goodness and sufficiency."[^11] Look at Joseph's words in both passages. What does he name honestly about what his brothers did, and what does he say about God?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe Joseph facing an identity crisis when his brothers arrive: "Is he a part of Jacob's betav or is he part of Pharaoh's family?" (*beit av* means "house of the father").[^12] The notes frame his choice as one between vengeance and forgiveness. How might choosing forgiveness also be choosing which family he belongs to?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes conclude: "Genesis ends with forgiveness which is one of, if not THE, ultimate expression of trust." In forgiving, they say, you lay down your right to vengeance, hatred and anger and trust God to own the story.[^13] Bring to mind the person or situation you thought about this week; you do not need to name them. Without pretending the harm was not real, what would one step toward trusting God to own that story look like for you?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Genesis began with a God who invited people to trust his story instead of living from fear. When the story seemed hopeless, a man left his home to partner with God, and his family carried that calling through hospitality, failure, wrestling and deception. Now, at the end of the book, Joseph's pit becomes a palace, Judah offers himself for his brother, and Joseph chooses forgiveness over vengeance. The notes say this family understands justice and forgiveness and how to put the story back together, even when they struggle with selfishness.[^14]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one thing from Genesis you most want to carry with you as we move into Exodus?

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 17 — A God Who Hears the Cry](/guides/17)** (41:00): Genesis is complete, and the notes describe the series as moving from the preface and introduction into the main narrative. This episode steps back from the text to look at Egypt and what empire does to people, and it sets a *hagah* project (from the Hebrew for a lion's low growl over its prey, a picture of persistent study) on the strange scene where God seems to seek Moses' life and Zipporah circumcises their son. Four Hebrew words link the Exodus to the story of Sodom, presenting a God who hears the cry of the oppressed and acts to set things right.[^15]

**What to bring next week:**
- Read Exodus 4:18–31 and write down every question the passage raises for you, the stranger the better.
- Read Exodus 3:7–9 and notice what God says he has seen and heard.
