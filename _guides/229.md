### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Dallas Jenkins described the "manna program," a funding model that requires daily dependence on God's provision, and named plausibility as the guiding creative principle of The Chosen.[^1]

**This week:** Dallas returns to talk about how he decides what Jesus knows and when, how story can unite people that interpretation divides, and the weight of making a show that shapes the faith of millions.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The heart of this conversation is what Dallas calls the "God goggles" debate: did Jesus walk around constantly accessing divine omniscience, or did he live as a human being who received what he needed from the Father through prayer and the Spirit? Dallas admits he does not fully know and deliberately portrays a middle ground, grounded in Philippians 2. That choice has drawn criticism from both sides, and ten seconds of Jesus working on a sermon became the most controversial moment in the series.[^3]

Dallas also explains his guardrails (a consultant board from three traditions that raises red flags but holds no veto), his observation that the Gospel stories themselves rarely divide Christians, and his principle that every strength is also a weakness. He closes on a personal note: an experience in the synagogue at Magdala, the recurring "look at me" theme in the miracle scenes, and an honest admission that he needs to get better at Sabbath and rest.[^4]

### God Goggles and the Self-Emptying Jesus <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#god-goggles-and-the-self-emptying-jesus}

*Dallas grounds his portrayal of Jesus in a passage about what Jesus did with his equality with God.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Philippians 2:5–11 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Dallas notes that this passage does not say Jesus gave up his divinity; it says he "emptied himself" and did not treat equality with God as something to hold onto.[^5] As you listen, what does Paul say Jesus did, and what does he say Jesus did not do? Report the verbs you notice.

*Read Mark 5:25–34 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Dallas believes that when Jesus asks questions such as "Who touched my garment?" or where Lazarus was buried, these were genuine questions, "not tests."[^6] What do you notice about how Jesus asks, how the woman answers, and what he says once he knows? How does it land for you to imagine that Jesus genuinely asked?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes say Dallas's middle position has been criticized from both directions: some viewers thought Jesus seemed too divine (knowing the Samaritan woman's history), others too human (struggling with sermon preparation). Ten seconds of Jesus working through the wording of a sermon "generated more debate than any other scene," and some viewers withdrew their support.[^7] Why do you think a picture of Jesus learning or preparing unsettles people so deeply? What do you think is at stake for them?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Dallas explains Jesus's knowledge through prayer: "just as the Holy Spirit reveals things to believers in prayer, the Spirit could reveal things to Jesus through his perfect prayer life and relationship with the Father."[^5] If Jesus himself lived by listening to the Father, what does that suggest about the place of prayer in your own week?

</div>

### Guardrails, Story, and Strength as Weakness <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#guardrails-story-and-strength-as-weakness}

*Dallas describes how he listens to other traditions, why the stories unite people, and how he writes flawed but brilliant disciples.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Dallas's consultant board (a Catholic priest, a Messianic Jewish rabbi and an evangelical scholar) raises "red flags" but has no veto. They ask two questions: "Is it accurate?" and "Is it plausible?" When Rabbi Jason Sobel objected to Simon Peter fishing on Shabbat, the scene stayed but was reframed so that Peter knows he is crossing a serious line.[^8] What do you think is wise about listening closely to other traditions without handing them control? What would it look like to practice that in a church or a small group?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Dallas observes that the stories of Jesus generate surprisingly little disagreement across traditions; divisions tend to come from interpretations of who Jesus was "at the beginning of time or in infinity" or from theological developments "500 years later."[^9] Think diagnostically about the tradition you know best (whether Church of Christ, Baptist, Christian Church, Methodist or another). Where have interpretive frameworks, more than the Gospel stories themselves, drawn the lines between your tradition and others? What might change if conversations between traditions began with the stories?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Dallas writes characters on the principle that "every strength is also a weakness when operating in human flesh." Peter's fierce loyalty makes him fall to his knees when called, but it also leads him to fish on Shabbat for his family and to confront Matthew with a recitation of Jewish history that Dallas says was "absolutely true" yet harsh. Marty adds that Peter's offer to build shelters at the Transfiguration shows theological astuteness, not ignorance.[^10] What is one of your own strengths that can turn into a weakness on a bad day, and how do you notice when it has?

</div>

### Look at Me <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#look-at-me}

*The project has kept its creator dependent, and the miracle scenes carry his own lesson.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Dallas receives messages saying the show brought someone to Jesus, and he calls the responsibility beautiful and terrifying. He values thoughtful critics, describing podcasts like BEMA as "warning lights on a dashboard."[^11] Who plays that role in your life, people who love you enough to tell you when something is off? How do you receive them?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes say the recurring words in the miracle scenes ("Look at me," "Look up," "Don't look at him, look at me") reflect Dallas's own experience of being drawn away from viewership numbers and impact back to simply looking at Jesus. He also admits, three days before a family move and buried in work, that he needs to improve at practicing Sabbath and rest.[^12] What are the "numbers" that pull your attention in this season, and what is one concrete way you could practice looking up this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Dallas Jenkins does not claim to know exactly how Jesus's divine and human natures worked together, so he shows a Jesus who claims God's name and also prays, asks, and listens. He builds guardrails by listening to other traditions, and he trusts that the stories of Jesus can gather people whom arguments have scattered. Along the way, the work has kept him in "Red Sea situations," dependent on God and repeatedly called back from the numbers to the person of Jesus. The invitation for us is the same one his miracle scenes keep repeating: look at him.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one thing that tends to pull your eyes away from Jesus, and what will help you look up this week?

</div>
