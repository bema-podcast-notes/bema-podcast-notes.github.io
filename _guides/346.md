### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Exodus 29 dealt with sin seriously, but always for the sake of presence: a shared table, a daily rhythm of repentance, and God's promise to dwell among his people.

**This week:** The sixth day makes humanity in God's image, and the furnishings of Exodus 30 invite us to wonder at God's saving work instead of trying to own or control it.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This is a contemplative episode, not a teaching episode. Brent reads the sixth day of creation aloud five times, and four of those readings are followed by an item from Exodus 30: the golden altar of incense, the census ransom, the bronze basin, and the anointing oil and sacred incense. After each pairing Marty gives a short prompt and leaves a long silence. Placing humanity's creation in God's image beside the Tabernacle furnishings suggests that image-bearers are meant to be places where God's presence and holiness live.

Each item opens a different door into wonder. The incense altar received no payment offerings at all, only rising fragrance. The half-shekel cost rich and poor exactly the same. The basin speaks of cleansing and a rebirth that Marty admits is hard to accept. The oil and incense made holy whatever they touched, yet could never be copied for personal use. Marty's closing principle is that holiness belongs to God, and God's redemptive work is not something we can claim or control.

### Image-Bearers and Free Offerings <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#image-bearers-and-free-offerings}

*Before the furnishings, the reflection keeps returning us to the day humanity was made.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 1:24–31 aloud slowly, then sit together in silence for one minute.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes point out that only after humanity is created does God call everything "very good," and they explain image (*tselem*) and likeness (*demut*) against an ancient background where an "image" was often a statue that showed a god's or king's presence and rule[^1]. Read the passage once more. What words or phrases repeat, and what changes once humanity appears? Share one thing you noticed.

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The golden altar could hold no burnt, grain or drink offerings, only incense, morning and evening. The notes describe incense as "rising fragrance, not a payment or exchange," a picture of gratitude, prayer and recognition[^2]. What is the difference between an offering that pays for something and an offering that simply expresses what God has done?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Marty invites us to think about offerings that cost nothing. The notes suggest examples such as telling a friend specifically how God has been at work in your life, keeping a gratitude journal as "morning and evening incense," or pausing at meals or at the edges of the workday[^3]. Which free offering could you actually make a regular part of this week, and when in your day would it rise?

</div>

### The Price of a Life and the Gift of a New Start <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-price-of-a-life-and-the-gift-of-a-new-start}

*The census ransom and the bronze basin turn wonder toward atonement and rebirth.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Exodus 30:11–21 aloud, then sit together in silence for one minute.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Every Israelite counted in the census gave a half-shekel as a "ransom for his life," with one rule: "The rich are not to give more… and the poor are not to give less." The notes connect the equal price to everyone standing on equal footing before God's redemptive work, and to how communities welcome and honor people across economic lines[^4]. What would it look like for a church to take the equal half-shekel seriously in the way it treats people?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> At the basin, Marty asks, "Have you accepted the reality of being reborn?" and admits that rebirth is hard to accept[^5]. Why do you think accepting a new start can be harder than believing in forgiveness in general?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** After the census ransom, Marty asks whether we have become "desensitized by bad theology," whether familiar or transactional ways of explaining salvation have drained the awe out of atonement[^6]. Our traditions each have their own words for how salvation works, and those words carry real convictions. Without naming anyone else's view as the "bad" one, where have familiar phrases in your own way of talking about atonement gone flat for you? The notes suggest reading a redemption account slowly, "as if for the first time"[^7]. Which passage would you choose to read that way this week?

</div>

### A Holiness That Spills but Is Not Ours <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#a-holiness-that-spills-but-is-not-ours}

*The anointing oil and incense hold together two surprising truths about holiness.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Exodus 30:22–38 aloud, then sit together in silence for one minute.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> "Whatever touches them will be holy," yet copying the oil's formula was forbidden, and anyone who made the incense "to enjoy its fragrance" was to be cut off[^8]. Marty concludes: "Holiness is something that belongs to God. Just as the incense didn't even belong to Moses, so God's sacred redemptive work is not something that we can claim or control."[^9] How do these two truths, holiness that spreads and holiness that cannot be owned, fit together?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Marty's last prompt asks whether wonder at God's holiness helps us notice it spilling into "all other spaces and all other people"[^10]. The notes give examples like seeing an ordinary kitchen, office or commute as a place where God's presence can be found, and resisting the urge to decide who "deserves" God's grace[^11]. Name one ordinary space and one person in your coming week. How would you treat each of them if you expected God's holiness to spill there?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The sixth day tells us that humanity was made in God's image, and only then did God call creation very good. Exodus 30 adds layer after layer of wonder: incense that pays for nothing, a ransom that costs everyone the same, a basin that offers a fresh start, and oil and incense that make holy whatever they touch. Through all of it runs one boundary: holiness belongs to God, and his redemptive work is a gift to receive and marvel at, not something to control or use for ourselves. Marty's closing blessing asks that God's love, mercy and work in the world would "inspire regular wonder."[^12]
{: .guide-summary}

*Sit together in silence for one minute before the closing question.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** Going around the room in one sentence each, with no comments until everyone has spoken: what is one thing about God's work that you want to wonder at again this week?

</div>
