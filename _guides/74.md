### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We stood at the doorway of the Gospels, reviewed the story of Sessions 1 and 2, and heard why the four centuries between Malachi and Matthew were anything but silent.

**This week:** The first stop in those centuries is the synagogue: how the exile reshaped Judaism around Torah, what a first-century synagogue looked like, and how a demanding education system changes the way we read Jesus calling his disciples.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode argues that Judaism after the Babylonian exile was fundamentally different from Judaism before it. The reasoning ran like this: the exile happened because "we didn't obey the rules," and that happened because "we didn't know the rules," so the answer was a community where everyone would know Torah intimately.[^1] The synagogue, which likely began among scattered communities in exile, was never meant to replace the Temple but became the center of Torah study and community life.[^2]

Marty and Brent walk through seven elements of a synagogue (mikvah, basilica, bema, chief seats, Torah closet, Moses seat and study room) and then describe the education system: *Bet Sefer*, *Bet Midrash*, and the rare few who became *talmidim*, disciples of a rabbi.[^3] The episode ends with a striking reframe of the fishermen Jesus called. They were not leaving successful careers behind; they were young people who had already been told they were not good enough, and a rabbi had just told them otherwise.[^4]

### A Building That Teaches <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#a-building-that-teaches}

*The episode reads the synagogue's architecture as a set of theological statements.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** In the center of the synagogue floor stood the *bema*, a slightly raised platform where the reader stood. The notes stress that it "wasn't a stage for performance": the community "literally gathered around the Text in a circle, emphasizing that worship was participatory rather than spectatorial."[^5] They contrast this with modern rows facing a stage and language like "good sermon" that "reinforces performance dynamics."[^6] What changes in a gathering when the Text is in the middle of the people rather than in front of them? What would you expect people to do differently?

*Read Luke 4:16–21 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes explain that there were no clergy in synagogue services. Families took turns, and whoever read that week sat in the Moses seat. A rabbi read only in his hometown synagogue, so Jesus read during services only in Nazareth, even though he taught in many synagogues.[^7] Read the passage again slowly. What details does Luke give about where Jesus is, what he does with the scroll, and how the room responds? What do those details look like now that you know how the service worked?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Synagogue buildings were often plain, but Torah closets received lavish artistic attention, and a complete Torah scroll is estimated to have cost about $750,000 in today's terms.[^8] Pillared sections let in sunlight so that people could "read God's light by God's light."[^9] If a visitor walked into your home or your church, what would the most carefully decorated or most expensive spot announce about what you value most?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The benches along the walls, the chief seats, were reserved for gray-haired elders who had shown a lifetime of faithfulness. The community would insist that an elder take a seat, the elder would humbly refuse, and the community would insist again.[^10] The notes contrast this with modern Western culture, which "Marginalizes older adults" and "Pushes elders to the periphery."[^11] Who are the faithful elders in your life or community, and what would it look like for you to give them a "chief seat" this month?

</div>

### Know What the Rabbi Knows <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#know-what-the-rabbi-knows}

*Alongside the synagogue grew an education system that took biblical literacy to a level we can hardly imagine.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** In *Bet Sefer* (roughly ages 5–9), children memorized Torah so thoroughly that a rabbi could stop mid-sentence anywhere in the five books and a student would continue. Only 1–2% went on to *Bet Midrash* (ages 10–13) to memorize the rest of the Tanakh.[^12] Most students heard: "You obviously love God and I've seen how much you love Torah, but you don't have what it takes. Go home and pray that your children will be able to do what you could not."[^13] The notes add that this "wasn't considered shameful" because nearly everyone heard it. What is your honest reaction to this system: admiration, discomfort, something else? What do you make of a culture where being "sent home" still meant carrying the whole Torah with you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The original episode notes record Marty's own methods (writing out the text, memorizing a portion each week) and a challenge: "Memorize 50 scriptures in the next 52 weeks."[^14] What memorization techniques have worked for you in the past, even outside Scripture (songs, phone numbers, lines in a play)? Which might you try in this session?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The goal of discipleship is captured in one phrase: "To know what the rabbi knows, in order to do what the rabbi does, for the reasons that the rabbi does them, in order to be just like your rabbi in his walk with God."[^15] Read it slowly and notice its four parts. Which part comes most easily to people like us, and which part do we tend to skip? Which part do you most need in your own walk with Jesus right now?

</div>

### Called by a Rabbi <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#called-by-a-rabbi}

*With the education system in view, a familiar call story looks very different.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 4:18–22 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Read the passage again and notice who is doing what, and with whom. Where are Peter and Andrew? Where are James and John, and who is with them? The notes point out that James and John were fishing with their father, which suggests they had not yet been bar mitzvahed and may have been only 10–12 years old; Peter, possibly 18–20, was likely the oldest.[^16] What picture of the disciples does this create, and how does it compare with the picture you grew up with?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes say modern Bible studies often ask, "Why did they just abandon their nets?" From the disciples' side, though, a rabbi had just told them they were good enough, something they had been told was not true, and their families would have celebrated.[^17] So the real cultural question becomes: "How did these failures catch the attention of a rabbi?"[^18] What does it say about Jesus that he chose disciples the system had already passed over? How does that change what "follow me" meant to them?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes claim that even students who left the system could have "run circles around a whole room of educated, internet-educated, doctrine, Bible-believing evangelicals today," and immediately add, "This isn't meant to shame modern Christians."[^19] If not shame, what response does that comparison invite in you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q11:** Many of us carry our own version of "you don't have what it takes," whether it came from a teacher, a parent, a church or our own inner voice. Where have you heard that message about your life with God? What would it mean for you to hear Jesus say to you what he said to those fishermen?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> After the exile, God's people decided they would never again lose their way for lack of knowing Torah. They built rooms where the Text sat in the middle, honored their faithful elders, spent fortunes on scrolls, and trained their children to carry Scripture in their hearts. Into that world came a rabbi who read in his hometown synagogue and then walked to the shore to call people the system had sent home. The notes conclude that "The exile did work." The question left for us is what it would look like for us to gather around the Text that seriously, and to hear that we, too, have been chosen.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one thing from the synagogue world you would like to bring into your own life with Scripture?

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 75 — Silent Years — Welcome to Hellenism](/guides/75)** (31:17): The episode introduces Hellenism, the Greek worldview that put humanity rather than God at the center, and Alexander the Great's strategy of conquering through education, healthcare, entertainment and athletics. It traces the history from exile to Herod and introduces the five Jewish groups (Sadducees, Herodians, Essenes, Zealots and Pharisees) whose different responses to Hellenism shape the Gospels.

**What to bring next week:** Notice three slogans, ads or cultural messages this week that promise to make your life better, and write them down. Read Matthew 16:24–26 and Matthew 20:25–28 and think about how they would sound to someone who believed those slogans.
