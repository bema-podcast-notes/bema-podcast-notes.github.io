### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** In the oracles of Isaiah 13–23, we saw God's care reach every nation, great and small, with Jerusalem itself in the middle of the list and no people exempt.[^1] (Episode 368 has no notes yet.)

**This week:** Marty and Brent read all four chapters of Isaiah 24–27, which work as a capstone to the oracles against the nations.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Isaiah 24 describes the whole earth laid waste because humanity has broken the "everlasting covenant." Right after that comes an outburst of praise, a promised banquet for all peoples, a song of trust, resurrection imagery, the defeat of Leviathan and a renewed vineyard. Marty argues that these passages of hope are not random sprinkles of optimism; they belong to the same "day" as the desolation.[^2]

Isaiah is asking his listeners how they will respond to destruction: with despair, or by planting their feet in hope. Several questions touch on times when something in our own lives has fallen apart. Share only what you are comfortable sharing.

### Opening <span class="guide-time">10 min</span> {#opening}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-opening" markdown="1">

**Opening Question:** Think of a place or a person you have watched rebuild after something fell apart. What made rebuilding possible?

### The Whole Earth Laid Waste <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#the-whole-earth-laid-waste}

*After ten chapters on individual nations, the prophet zooms out to the whole earth, and Jerusalem gets no exception.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 24:1–6 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Verse 2 lists pairs of people who will share the same fate: priest and people, master and servant, mistress and maidservant, seller and buyer, borrower and lender, debtor and creditor. The hosts say judgment here erases every social category, and status pyramids collapse.[^3] Look at the pairs. What do they have in common, and what does the list say about who is safe from this judgment?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Brent raises the obvious question: didn't God promise in Genesis 9 never to destroy the earth again? Marty quips that "the prophets would be real big on the fine print of Genesis," since the promise was about never again destroying the earth *by flood*. More importantly, the first "everlasting covenant" in Scripture is the one with Noah and all creation (Genesis 9:16), which makes Isaiah's indictment universal, not only about Israel under Torah.[^4] Read Genesis 9:8–17. Why might it matter that the covenant broken in Isaiah 24 is one made with every living creature?

*Read Isaiah 24:14–16 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Voices from the west, the east and the islands shout for joy and give "glory to the Righteous One." The text does not say who is singing; Marty suggests creation itself, groaning under injustice, or the oppressed, who have suffered constant exploitation. Isaiah's own reaction is different: "I waste away, I waste away! Woe to me!" He stands in solidarity with the guilty.[^5] One of the episode's applications is to notice who hears "judgment" as good news.[^6] In a time of upheaval, where do you tend to find yourself: with the voices singing, or with Isaiah wasting away? What might you learn from the other response?

</div>

### Praise in the Rubble <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#praise-in-the-rubble}

*Hope in these chapters does not arrive years later. It is spoken while the smoke is still rising.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 25:1–9 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Chapter 25 opens in praise *because* God has made the ruthless city a heap of rubble, having been "a refuge for the poor" and "a shelter from the storm." Then comes a banquet on the mountain for all peoples, the destruction of the shroud over all peoples, death swallowed up forever, and tears wiped from all faces. Brent points out that Judah's leaders ran before the sword arrived, and Marty concludes that the people saying "we trusted in him, and he saved us" are the oppressed, not the elites.[^7] Who is singing in this passage, and what are they celebrating? Point to the lines that tell you.

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The phrase "in that day" repeats through chapters 25–27, roughly seven times by Brent's count, and it refers to the same day of judgment, "as the smoke is still rising." Brent suggests that many Christians read most of the Bible as "setting the table" for something later, which can make Scripture feel distant and hard to apply.[^8] Where have you tended to push a passage off to "someday"? What happens when you read it as speaking to its own moment first?

*Read Isaiah 26:1–6, 13–19 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Chapter 26 turns pastoral. Faced with desolation, Marty says, a person can despair and stop investing in a world that is falling apart, or see the destruction as God clearing ground to rebuild the *shalom*-filled world humanity failed to build, and plant their feet in hope. He suggests someone in war-torn Ukraine would grasp these chapters right away, and, paraphrasing Rabbi Jonathan Sacks, describes courage as looking despair in the face and saying, "Hand me a shovel." The notes also caution against jumping straight to the final resurrection in 26:19 and missing what Isaiah says to his own people: God "loves to make beautiful things out of dead things."[^9] Sharing here is optional. Think of something in your life (an institution, a career, a relationship) that has fallen apart or is falling apart. What would it mean to ask, "Is something dying so that something truer can live?"[^10]

</div>

### A Vineyard That Fills the World <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#a-vineyard-that-fills-the-world}

*Isaiah 27 picks up the vineyard song of Isaiah 5 and sings it the other way around.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 5:1–7 and Isaiah 27:2–6 aloud, one after the other.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The hosts set the two songs side by side. In Isaiah 5, God tears down the hedge, briars and thorns take over, the vineyard yields bad fruit and God withholds rain. In Isaiah 27, God guards the vineyard day and night, would march against any briars and thorns, waters it continually, and Israel will "fill all the world with fruit."[^11] Before looking at that list, what reversals did you hear? Which one strikes you most?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Isaiah 27:1 pictures God killing Leviathan, "the gliding serpent… the monster of the sea." The hosts read it as poetry: God puts to death the evils that seem impossible to defeat. In verses 4–5 ("I am not angry… let them make peace with me"), Brent sees another moment where God's heart breaks through, since even the briars and thorns are invited to make peace.[^12] What do these two images, a slain monster and an invitation to the thorns, add to your picture of God's judgment?

*Read Isaiah 27:12–13 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The harvest runs from the Euphrates to the Wadi of Egypt, and a great trumpet calls the exiles home, yet Israel is "gathered up one by one." The hosts note that God's attention to whole nations does not swallow up the individual; every person matters.[^13] Where do you need to hear that you are gathered "one by one"? Or who is someone you could help gather this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> It is easy to treat Isaiah's hopeful passages as small comforts dropped between pages of doom, or as predictions about a distant future. Isaiah 24–27 holds together: the same "day" that brings the devastation of the earth also brings a banquet for all peoples, the defeat of death, the slaying of Leviathan and a vineyard that fills the world with fruit. For the poor and oppressed, the collapse of ruthless powers is good news. For everyone, the destruction is an invitation to choose despair or to trust that God makes beautiful things out of dead things. Hope like this takes courage, the kind that looks at the rubble and says, "Hand me a shovel."[^14]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: where will you pick up a shovel this week?

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 370 — Isaiah — Woes](/guides/370)** (54:06): In Isaiah 28–33, God turns from the nations to speak directly to his own covenant people. Marty begins by naming three pitfalls in reading the prophets (cold academic distance, using them as weapons against others, and taking every word personally while forgetting the original audience). The hosts then follow pride that blinds, the habit of running to "Egypt" for safety, and God's steady commitment to mercy: "The Lord longs to be gracious to you" (30:18).

**What to bring next week:** Read Isaiah 30:1–18. Think about what your own "Egypt" might be, the place you tend to run for safety instead of trusting God.
