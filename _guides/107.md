### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Jesus called the most devoted towns of the Galilee to repent for failing those in need, then offered rest to the weary in words that echoed God, Moses and the ancient path of Torah.

**This week:** Two Sabbath controversies open Matthew 12. Jesus does not set Torah aside; he shows how to read it when two good commands seem to pull in different directions.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode begins with a distinction: Torah, the five books of Moses with their 613 commandments, and *halakhah* (from the Hebrew for "walk"), the oral tradition about how to walk Torah out in daily life.[^1] The notes argue that Jesus never broke Torah; he challenged particular interpretations when they missed the heart of God's law.[^2] They also ask us to see the rabbinic "fence around Torah" with charity, as an act of devotion rather than legalism.[^3]

Every rabbi had a "yoke," an interpretive lens, and the episode describes the two great schools of Jesus's day, Hillel and Shammai.[^4] Through grain picked on the Sabbath, a man with a shriveled hand and an old debate about a donkey in a pit, the episode asks which commandment is weightier when two collide. It closes with Matthew's portrait of Jesus as Isaiah's gentle servant.[^5]

### Grain on the Sabbath <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#grain-on-the-sabbath}

*Jesus's disciples pick grain on the Sabbath, and the Pharisees call it harvesting.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 12:1–8 aloud, then 1 Samuel 21:1–6.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Jesus answers the Pharisees with three arguments. Find them in verses 3–7 and name them in your own words. The notes summarize them as David's example, the priests' example, and "God's priority."[^6] What do the three have in common?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes explain that after the exile, Jewish teachers built a "fence around Torah," later written down in the Mishnah and Talmud, and that "The motivation was not legalism but devotion."[^7] Marty tells of Moshe, a Jerusalem shop owner who bought twenty oranges when his pregnant wife wanted one, and who said: "My obedience as a Jew following all these oral commands and traditions is not because I'm a legalist, it's because it's my gift to Adonai."[^8] The notes say this "helps modern readers understand the Pharisees with greater charity."[^9] How does Moshe's story change the way you picture the people who questioned Jesus here?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes warn that when Christians say "Jesus broke the law," they "misunderstand this critical distinction" between Torah and *halakhah*, and in a Jewish setting would undercut Jesus's sinless life.[^10] Our traditions have talked about Jesus and the law in different ways. What have you been taught about whether Jesus broke the law, and how does the Torah and *halakhah* distinction sit with what you learned?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q4:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes list what Sabbath reminds us of, drawing on Genesis and Session 1: "We have enough," "We can rest," "We can play," "No work," and "God loves us."[^11] Which of those five reminders do you most need to hear right now, and what would one Sabbath-shaped hour look like for you this week?

</div>

### The Donkey in the Pit <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-donkey-in-the-pit}

*In the synagogue, the Pharisees bring a man with a shriveled hand and a question meant as a trap.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 12:9–14 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Compare the question the Pharisees ask in verse 10 with the conclusion Jesus states in verse 12. What word changes? The notes observe that Jesus "elevated the question from technical compliance to moral purpose."[^12] What difference does that change of word make?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes describe the yoke of Shammai, whose second greatest commandment was to obey the Sabbath, and the yoke of Hillel, whose second greatest commandment was "Love your neighbor as yourself."[^13] A yoke works like a pair of glasses for reading all of Torah, and the notes say Jesus consistently adopted Hillel's yoke, with one exception about divorce that comes later.[^14] The donkey in the pit was one of the great rabbinic debates, and both schools agreed the donkey must be rescued.[^15] How does knowing about these yokes help you hear Jesus's argument in verses 11–12?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes offer a modern case: Christians hiding Jews during the Holocaust, asked at the door whether they had Jews inside. Rather than saying "Whatever you do, you'll break a commandment," Jesus's yoke says: "Don't look at it as breaking a command, look at it as fulfilling the right command."[^16] The notes also caution against using this principle "to dismiss commands we find inconvenient."[^17] How would you tell the difference between choosing the weightier command and simply choosing the easier one?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes ask, "What yoke do we bring to Scripture? Do we read through a lens of love or mere obligation?"[^18] Think of a recent situation where you had to decide what faithfulness required. Which lens did you reach for, and what did it lead you to do?

</div>

### A Bruised Reed <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#a-bruised-reed}

*After the confrontation, Jesus withdraws, and Matthew reaches for Isaiah.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 12:15–21 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** List what Isaiah's servant will not do, and then what he will do. The notes point out that "Jesus withdraws rather than promoting Himself or winning arguments."[^19] What strikes you about how Jesus responds after being plotted against in verse 14?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes leave an unresolved thread: Jesus had just called John "a reed blown by the wind," Isaiah's servant will not break "a bruised reed," and Isaiah 42:7 speaks of releasing "from the dungeon those who sit in darkness" while John sat in prison.[^20] What possibilities do you see in Matthew placing these texts so close together?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q11:** Who in your life right now is a bruised reed or a smoldering wick, and what would gentleness toward them look like this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Jesus did not break Torah. He read it through the yoke of love for God and neighbor, the yoke of Hillel, and he asked which command weighs more when two seem to collide. The priest who fed David, the priests who work on the Sabbath, and the donkey everyone agrees to rescue all point the same way: "I desire mercy, not sacrifice." The people who questioned him were often sincerely devoted, and the issue was not their devotion but their priorities. Then, after the argument, Jesus withdrew, the servant who does not break a bruised reed.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one place this week where you will choose mercy as the weightier command?

</div>
