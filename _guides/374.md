### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Elle Grover Fricks and Marty disagreed, as friends, about how biblical texts came together, but agreed that Isaiah moves from diagnosis to healing, and that we are always "painting a picture" with Scripture.[^1]

**This week:** The second half of Isaiah begins; the voice turns from warning to comfort for people already in exile, and Marty frames it as God repairing the world with his servant as a partner.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Marty calls Isaiah 1–39 "a father disciplines his sons" and titles chapters 40–66 "Isaiah and Tikkun Olam," using a later Hebrew phrase that means "the repairing of the world." God's people are no longer being warned about Babylon; they are living in exile, and the message becomes "Comfort, comfort my people." The hosts point out how often the servant in these chapters is named as Jacob/Israel, and they trace images of God as Helper, Advocate, Savior, and Counselor, which Marty links to Jesus' teaching on the Holy Spirit in John.[^3]

The episode's central claim is that faithful endurance can make God visible: the nations see God in *how* Israel suffers, not by fighting fire with fire. Marty is careful to say this is about a nation under imperial oppression and is "not a teaching about individual trauma or a reason to stay in abuse." The episode closes with God's judgment on proud Babylon and a warning to Israelites who had grown comfortable inside the empire. Questions about suffering tonight are invitations, not requirements; share only what you choose to.[^4]

### Comfort, Comfort My People <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#comfort-comfort-my-people}

*Isaiah's voice turns to people who have already lived through the discipline.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 40:1–11 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes call Isaiah 40 a big shift in tone: chapters 1–39 warn of coming discipline, while chapters 40–66 speak to people already in exile, "sitting in time-out" in Babylon. The message moves from "destruction is coming" to "your hard service has been completed."[^5] What words and images in this passage tell you the setting has changed? Which line would land most strongly if you were an exile?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes explain that *tikkun olam* is not biblical Hebrew; it comes from after the biblical period and became a widely used idea in Jewish thought. Marty uses it to sum up Isaiah 40–66: "God repairs the world, and his people take part in that repair."[^6] What changes when you think of salvation as God repairing the world with us, rather than only rescuing us from it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** "The grass withers and the flowers fall, but the word of our God endures forever." The notes list what is temporary ("people, kingdoms, empires, ideologies, and our own clever theologies and strategies") and what is permanent ("God's word, goodness, redemptive plan, and love for creation").[^7] What in your life right now feels like grass, and what are you learning to lean on that endures?

</div>

### The Servant and the Bruised Reed <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-servant-and-the-bruised-reed}

*The servant is first God's people, called to partner in repairing the world.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 41:8–10, 44:1–2, and 44:21 aloud. Then read Isaiah 42:1–4 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes collect the servant passages: "But you, Israel, my servant, Jacob, whom I have chosen" (41:8); "Remember these things, Jacob, for you, Israel, are my servant" (44:21). They conclude that the Servant Songs "are first a calling for God's people, not only a prediction about one future person," and that the servant is a **partner**: God works through a people he formed "in the womb" for this purpose.[^8] What do you notice about how insistently the text names the servant? How does that shape the way you hear Isaiah 42:1–4?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty's four images for God in these chapters are Helper, Advocate, Savior, and Counselor, which he matches to John's description of the Holy Spirit as the *paraklētos*, "the one who comes alongside." An advocate in the ancient world came alongside someone to plead their case; God says, in effect, "I've got you."[^9] Which of the four images do you most need right now, and why?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Matthew 12 says Jesus fulfilled "A bruised reed he will not break, and a smoldering wick he will not snuff out" by the way he restored hurting people. The notes call this "ministry with no collateral damage," and Marty asks whether our churches and ministries are built to live out this same calling.[^10] Every tradition has practices that heal and practices that can bruise. In the churches you have known, what has helped people who were already hurting, and what has made things harder for them?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Marty pushes back on reading these passages *only* as predictions about Jesus, while affirming that they carry messianic overtones and that Jesus fulfilled them perfectly. He quotes a saying whose author he does not know: "Jesus didn't go to the cross so that I don't have to. Jesus went to the cross to show me how to." He connects it to John 14, where "I am the way" also means "I'm showing you how to do it."[^11] How does that saying sit with what you were taught about the cross? What does it add, and what questions does it raise for you?

</div>

### How We Suffer, and Leaving Babylon <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#how-we-suffer-and-leaving-babylon}

*Faithful endurance makes God visible; comfortable compromise does not.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 45:14 and Isaiah 46:1–4 aloud. Then read Isaiah 48:17–20 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** In 45:14 Egypt, Cush, and the Sabeans come to Israel saying, "Surely God is with you." The notes say the nations see God "because of *how* Israel endured exile, not by trading sword for sword," and that God had stripped Israel of its own power so they could show the way of the kingdom.[^12] What do you notice about this role reversal? Why might endurance reveal God more clearly than victory would?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Isaiah 46 contrasts Babylon's gods, which must be loaded onto beasts of burden, with a God who says, "I have made you and I will carry you… even to your old age and gray hairs." Babylon, meanwhile, claims, "I am, and there is none besides me," a phrase that belongs to God.[^13] What are the idols today that we have to carry, and what does it mean to be carried instead?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** Not every exile was at the bottom of the power pyramid: Babylon drew some upward into its way of life. Isaiah 48 tells them, "Leave Babylon, flee from the Babylonians!", a call Revelation 18:4 echoes. The notes invite readers to ask "what systems ('Babylons') they have joined themselves to."[^14] What is one "Babylon" you have grown comfortable inside? What would leaving it, even in a small way, look like this month?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Isaiah 40 opens with comfort for people who have already lived through God's discipline. God is repairing the world, and he names his people, Jacob and Israel, as the servant who partners in that repair. The bruised-reed servant, whom Matthew saw in Jesus' gentle ministry, shows a way of bringing the kingdom without harming the hurting. The nations see God in how his people endure. And the call to leave Babylon still asks where we have grown comfortable.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, name one person who is a "bruised reed" in your world and one gentle thing you can do for them this week.

</div>
