### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We named spiritual abuse as "the use of spiritual power or authority to coerce, shame or bring harm," heard why it is so hard to see from the inside, and took "Can you question this?" as a test of a healthy community, all framed by Micah 6:8.[^1]

**This week:** In part three of the series, Elle Grover Fricks, a former mental health therapist who attended Mars Hill from 2009 until it closed, joins Marty and Brent to reflect on Christianity Today's podcast *The Rise and Fall of Mars Hill*.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

As in the past two weeks, sharing is optional, you may pass on any question, and what is shared here stays here. Some of us may have been part of churches with dynamics like the ones described; please speak only about your own experience, and listen to others without rushing to judge.

The hosts use Mars Hill as a mirror rather than a target. The key question shifts from "why did he do it" or "why did they do it" to "why do we do it?"[^3] They name the conditions that let spiritual abuse flourish: big charismatic personalities, franchised church growth models, personal brands, us-versus-them identity, and success measured by numbers.[^4] Elle insists that Mars Hill was both a place of genuine transformation and community and a system that enabled abuse, and that flattening the story into pure condemnation or pure defense harms survivors.[^5] The episode contrasts an "empire" model of ministry success with a "flourishing" model measured by the fruit of the Spirit, and it asks whether pastoring, leading, managing and running an organization have been collapsed into one role.[^6]

### Why Do We Do It? <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#why-do-we-do-it}

*The episode turns the question about one church's leaders into a question about all of us.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read 1 Samuel 9:1–2 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes compare a Christian culture that "prefers narcissistic leaders" to Israel demanding King Saul, "the tall, charismatic 'donkey herder' rather than David."[^7] What does the text emphasize about Saul when we first meet him? What kinds of qualities still catch our eye when we choose or follow leaders?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes list what reinforces this pattern: the boomer megachurch movement, church growth conferences that franchise successful models, origin stories "packaged and leveraged for association and branding," us-versus-them tribal identity, and metrics that measure numeric growth rather than spiritual fruit.[^7] Which of these have you seen up close, in any church or organization? Why do you think the hosts insist the real question is "why do we do it?"[^3]

*Read Genesis 11:1–9 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty admits he has an almost subconscious sense of how to build platforms and "leverage energy," which can serve the kingdom or self-interest, so he surrounds himself with people who can hold him accountable. Elle connects this to Babel, where God sees that the people "can do anything."[^8] What does the Babel story suggest about the gift and the danger of being able to gather people and energy?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** One attendee described viewing shocking statements as "tongue-in-cheek": "of course he said that, but we know what he really meant because he's saying things that need to be said." The notes call this confirmation bias, which happens "in any context where big vision and charismatic personality create strong loyalty."[^9] When a leader you admire says something that troubles you, what do you usually do with it? What would help you take that discomfort seriously?

</div>

### Both Things Are True <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#both-things-are-true}

*Elle asks listeners to hold genuine good and real harm together without letting either cancel the other.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Elle describes a welcoming culture where people showed up "with tattoos, messy pasts, and current struggles without judgment," and the notes call the "deep, authentic connection" there "a genuine good that coexisted with harmful dynamics." She compares this to abusive relationships, where "genuine love and care coexist with harm."[^10] Why do you think it is so tempting, from a distance, to flatten a story like this into all good or all bad? How does flattening it hurt the people who lived it?[^5]

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe a complex picture on gender. Mark Driscoll directly called abusive men in the congregation to repentance and encouraged women to report abuse to law enforcement, which the notes call a protective factor, yet this "coexisted with" theology and messaging that caricatured women and asked them to "recede."[^11] Traditions in our group hold different convictions about men's and women's roles, so the question here is not which view is right. What does this example teach about how protective and harmful messages can live side by side in one church?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** If someone told you their old church both changed their life and deeply hurt them, what would it look like to listen without pushing them toward either verdict?

</div>

### Empire or Flourishing <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#empire-or-flourishing}

*The episode argues that how a church defines success shapes everything else.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Galatians 5:22–23 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes contrast an empire model, where success is "numeric growth, multiplication, and replication" and "people are meeting Jesus" can justify any means, with a flourishing model, where success is the fruit of the Spirit and "that people leave in a better place spiritually, even if they never return."[^6] Read against Galatians 5, what would a church measure if it took the flourishing model seriously? What would it stop measuring?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say contemporary church culture has merged four distinct roles (pastor, leader, manager and CEO), and they cite Eugene Peterson's observation that a pastor can truly care for only 50–60 people.[^12] Elle and the hosts also recognized the culture of pulling off in six weeks an event that normally takes six months, and the alternative is a leader willing to say, "maybe we won't get as many baptisms, maybe our church won't grow as much, maybe we won't have the leverage we want, but that's okay because it's not worth abusing our staff."[^13] Where have you seen growth or deadlines take priority over people's health?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes name two measures of a church's integrity: how "the marginalized, vulnerable, and non-tithers are treated," and how emergencies are handled "in the wings," backstage with staff and volunteers.[^14] Apply those two measures to a place where you serve or have influence. What is one thing you could do to make it healthier?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> This week we looked at the rise and fall of Mars Hill, not to point at one church, but to ask why we keep choosing Saul. Big personality, flashy vision, franchised models and numeric success create the conditions for spiritual abuse, and all of us help build those conditions when we reward them. Elle reminded us that genuine good and real harm can live in the same place, and that survivors need us to hold both. Galatians 5 offers a different measure of success: love, joy, peace and the rest of the Spirit's fruit, and people leaving more whole than they came.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one thing you want to start valuing, or stop valuing, in the leaders and communities you follow?

</div>
