### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Saul's Damascus road experience looked less like switching religions than like seeing his own faith clearly. Peter's vision was about people, not food, and the first Gentile household received the Spirit.

**This week:** Paul's first journey takes him to Cyprus and to Pisidian Antioch, where a synagogue full of three different kinds of listeners hears the message and reacts.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

On Cyprus, Saul begins to be called Paul. The episode suggests that this unusual, self-chosen name change honors his first high-profile convert, the Roman proconsul Sergius Paulus, whose name is connected with a city gate in Pisidian Antioch. That city, built as a "miniature Rome on seven hills," sat at the edge of Galatia.

In its synagogue, Paul addresses three groups: Jews by birth (brothers), converts to Judaism (*b'nei Avraham*, children of Abraham), and God-fearing Gentiles (*theosabes*). The episode explains how two Jewish schools of thought, those of Shammai and Hillel, disagreed about how Gentiles could stand before God. It argues that the local Jewish community welcomed Paul's message about Jesus at first, and that opposition came when the synagogue filled with Gentiles. The notes are careful to turn that observation back on us: churches, too, can welcome a message until it fills the room with people unlike them.

### From Saul to Paul <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#from-saul-to-paul}

*On Cyprus, a Roman official believes and Saul takes a new name.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Acts 13:6–12 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Notice who Sergius Paulus is, what stands in his way, and the moment Luke begins calling Saul "Paul." The notes call Paul's self-initiated name change "highly unusual in Scripture" and suggest it "likely honored his first convert, Sergius Paulus, and marked a pivotal moment in his identity as apostle to the Gentiles."[^1] What do you notice in the passage that supports or complicates that reading?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Pisidian Antioch was one of fourteen Antiochs Rome planted in Asia Minor, built as a "miniature Rome on seven hills" to display Roman power. Sergius Paulus funded its city gate, and the city sat on the edge of Galatia, "the frontier between Roman sophistication and tribal conservatism."[^2] Why might it matter that Paul's first major synagogue sermon happens in a place like this?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Paul's new name tied him to the people he was being sent to. What is a name, role or identity you have taken on that ties you to the people God has put in your path? What does it cost you, and what does it open up?

</div>

### Brothers, Converts, and God-Fearers <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#brothers-converts-and-god-fearers}

*Three kinds of people sat in a first-century synagogue, and Paul speaks to each.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Acts 13:14–16 and 13:26 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Notice how Paul addresses his audience. The episode identifies three groups: the brothers, "born into the covenant community"; the children of Abraham (*b'nei Avraham*), converts who had been circumcised and fully adopted Jewish law; and the *theosabes* ("God-fearers"), Gentiles who worshiped Israel's God without fully converting.[^3] Where do you see these groups in Paul's words? What would it have felt like to be a God-fearer in that room?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode explains that the school of Shammai believed Gentiles must fully convert to be saved, while the school of Hillel taught that Gentiles could be justified by faith like Abraham, though they remained "distant cousins" rather than full family. Most of Asia Minor leaned toward Hillel, while remote regions like Galatia attracted followers of Shammai.[^4] How does knowing about this debate prepare you for the letter to the Galatians?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Being a "distant cousin" means being accepted, but not quite family. Without naming individuals, who in church life tends to be welcomed as a guest but not treated as full family? How would they know?

</div>

### When the Synagogue Filled Up <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#when-the-synagogue-filled-up}

*The second Sabbath looks very different from the first.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Acts 13:42–48 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Notice the difference between the response after Paul's first sermon and the scene a week later. What does Luke say about the crowd and about the reason for the opposition? The episode argues that Jewish communities "initially welcomed Paul's message about Jesus and the resurrection," and that "their opposition arose not from rejecting Jesus, but from jealousy over Gentile inclusion."[^5]

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe Paul's strategy of going "to the Jew first" before turning to the Gentiles as "fulfilling Israel's calling to be 'a light to the nations.'"[^6]

*Read Isaiah 49:6 aloud.*

How does reading Paul's turn to the Gentiles as the fulfillment of Israel's own calling, rather than as God abandoning Israel, change the way you hear Acts 13:46–47?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes are careful not to stop at the first century: "we may claim to welcome God's universal love until our own synagogues or churches fill with people who don't look, think, or live like us."[^7] The reaction in Antioch is a very human one, and it shows up in churches of every tradition. Who would have to fill your church for you to feel that discomfort? What would faithfulness look like if they came?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> On Cyprus, Saul becomes Paul and a Roman proconsul believes. In Pisidian Antioch, Paul speaks to brothers, converts and God-fearers, three groups the synagogue had already learned to sort. The episode argues that the message about Jesus was welcomed; the trouble came when Gentiles filled the room. Paul's turn to the nations fulfills Israel's own calling to be a light, and the question it raises is one we still face: what happens when our own rooms fill up?
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each: whom could you treat as full family, not a distant cousin, this week?

</div>
