### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Avram never walked the blood path, so God walked it alone, as both the torch and the smoking firepot, saying in effect that when Avram failed, God would be the one to pay the price. Then in Genesis 17 God handed the covenant sign to his people to keep.[^1]

**This week:** Genesis 18–22 shows us who Abraham has become: a man of startling hospitality, bold enough to argue with God, and, on the hardest morning of his life, a father who tells his son "here I am."[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This episode moves quickly through five chapters. Abraham, just circumcised, runs to feed three strangers with far more than they need, and then pushes back on God over the fate of Sodom and Gomorrah. Marty draws on his own experience of Bedouin hospitality and asks hard questions about how much fear shapes the way we welcome people.

The center of the episode is a pairing the hosts believe is deliberate: Hagar and Ishmael sent into the wilderness (Genesis 21) and Abraham and Isaac walking up the mountain (Genesis 22). The two stories echo each other point by point, with one large difference. Hagar leaves her son under a bush and moves away; Abraham stays beside his. The Hebrew word *hineni* ("here I am") becomes the key to what the episode says about Abraham, about God, and about us.

### An Open Tent and a Bold Prayer <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#an-open-tent-and-a-bold-prayer}

*Genesis 18 shows Abraham's hospitality and his nerve, and both turn out to matter to God.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 18:1–8 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes point out that Abraham is resting in his tent after just being circumcised, yet he hurries out to strangers. He asks Sarah for three seahs of flour (about 60 pounds, roughly 80 loaves by hand), runs to choose a tender calf, and serves the guests himself even though 318 other people in his household could have helped.[^3] The notes sum it up: "This is not just hospitality but this is radical generosity."[^4] Listening to the passage again, which verbs and details stand out to you, and what do they tell you about Abraham?

*Read Genesis 18:20–33 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe Abraham showing *chutzpah* (guts, audacity, fire in the belly) as he calls God out over the cities and bargains with him.[^5] They also recall a midrash about three characters in Genesis: Noah the Insulator, who cares only for his own; Lot the Assimilator, who rises in Sodom but loses his ability to affect it; and Abraham the Engager, who stays distinct from his culture while still engaging and impacting it.[^6] What does it suggest about God that he seems to welcome this kind of pushback from his partner?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Marty imagines 54 Muslim strangers walking toward his own town and wonders whether he would lock the door or throw it open. He confesses, "I am far too worried about my own security... The kingdom of G-d cannot come to a place of fear and insecurity."[^7] Where does fear or concern for security shape how you welcome people, and what would one step toward an open tent look like for you?

</div>

### Two Mornings, Two Sons <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#two-mornings-two-sons}

*The hosts believe Genesis 21 and 22 were placed side by side so we would read them together.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 21:14–19 and Genesis 22:3–13 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** If the room is large enough, split into two groups, one following each passage. The notes list five parallels: each story begins "early the next morning"; Abraham places supplies on Hagar's shoulders and wood on Isaac's; Hagar puts the boy under a bush and Abraham lays the boy on the wood; Hagar looks up and sees a well, Abraham looks up and sees a ram; and each story ends with a covenant.[^8] The notes add that Ishmael is a teenager here, close in age to an Isaac old enough to carry wood.[^9] What echoes did your group hear between the two stories, and where do they differ most?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes observe that child sacrifice was normal in Abraham's culture, so God's command may not have shocked him. Instead of simply telling Abraham that child sacrifice is wrong (a Western, informational approach), God gives him an experience he will never forget (an Eastern, experiential approach), revealing a God who provides the sacrifice rather than demanding it.[^10] Our churches have taught this story in different ways. How was the binding of Isaac explained to you, and what does this reading add to or challenge in what you were taught?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes contrast the two parents: Hagar leaves her son under a bush and sits "about a bowshot away," while Abraham tells his son, in effect, "I'm right here, I'm not going anywhere, I will never leave your side."[^11] Without judging Hagar, who was in an impossible place, think about your own life. When has someone stayed close to you in a crisis, and when have you found yourself keeping a bowshot's distance from someone else's pain?

</div>

### Here I Am <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#here-i-am}

*A two-word answer becomes a window into God's own name and into the story of Jesus.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 22:1–2, 7–8 and 11–12 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes lay out Genesis 22 as a chiasm (a mirrored structure). It opens and closes with Abraham saying "Here I am," and at its center is Abraham answering Isaac on the walk with "Yes my son?" The word behind these answers is *hinnê*, a unique form with no ordinary dictionary entry of its own, and the notes gloss it this way: "Hinnê: I will be with you until the very end."[^12] What do you notice about where this answer appears, and why might the storyteller put a father's reply to his son at the center rather than the knife or the ram?

*Read John 18:4–8 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes connect *hinnê* to the name God gives Moses, with God saying in effect, "I chose Avraham because he is who I am like," and sending word to the slaves in Egypt that he never left them. Marty also suggests that if Jesus answered the Temple guards in Hebrew, he would have said *hinnê*, and then stayed in the moment of their greatest need to become "the ram caught in the thorns."[^13] How does reading Genesis 22 and John 18 together change what you hear in either one?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes end with this line: "In your moment of greatest need, I have not left your side."[^14] Who in your life right now needs someone to say "here I am," and what makes staying present with them hard?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Abraham is not a flawless hero. He repeats his old mistake with Abimelek, and the notes ask, "Who can't relate to making a mistake even when we know better?"[^15] Yet he keeps choosing other people over his own security: running to feed strangers, arguing for cities that are not his, and walking beside his son when everything in him must have wanted to look away. The episode presents the God who chose him as a God who sees, who provides, and who does not leave. *Hineni* is both Abraham's answer to God and God's answer to us.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: where is one place this week you want to say "here I am"?

</div>
