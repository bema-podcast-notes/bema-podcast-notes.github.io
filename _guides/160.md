### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Philippians showed us joy that does not depend on circumstances, a Christ who would not cling to divinity, and a contentment Paul learned whether "well fed or hungry."[^1]

**This week:** In Colossians, Paul confronts a philosophy that split reality into a pure spiritual world and an inferior physical one. His answer is Christ, in whom God's fullness lived in a body and through whom "all things" are reconciled.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Colossae was a small city off the main road between Hierapolis and Laodicea. It was destroyed by earthquakes in the AD 60s, never successfully rebuilt, and remains unexcavated today.[^2] The episode explains the teaching Paul was facing through Plato's theory of forms, in which the material world is only shadows of a perfect spiritual realm. That dualism produced two opposite extremes: anything goes in the body, or harsh self-denial meant to rise above it. It also produced an elite of the "enlightened."[^3]

Paul's response centers on the incarnation and the cosmic reach of Christ's work, and on wisdom available to every believer rather than a select few. The episode closes with a challenge to our own habits of thought: much Christian talk about heaven, the hosts argue, owes more to Plato than to the Bible.[^4] Each of our traditions sings and preaches about heaven in its own way, so that part of the conversation is an invitation to listen to one another.

### Shadows on the Wall <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#shadows-on-the-wall}

*Before reading Paul's answer, we need to understand the philosophy he was answering.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes describe Plato's view that the material world consists of "mere shadows or imperfect copies of perfect forms that exist in a spiritual realm," so that spiritual reality is "superior, pure, and eternal" and physical reality "inferior, corrupt, and temporary." In the allegory of the cave, most people see only shadows, while the "enlightened" few escape to the real world.[^5] *Read Colossians 2:8–10 aloud.* What does Paul warn against, and what does he set in its place? What do you notice about the phrase "in bodily form"?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say this dualism led to two opposite behaviors. One said physical actions do not matter, so people could "eat whatever, do whatever, engage whatever" because "the real action is somewhere else." The other pursued extreme self-denial to rise above the body.[^6] How can two such different ways of living grow from the same root belief?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Where do you notice the attitude that "the real action is somewhere else" in your own week, whether in how you treat your body, your work, your meals or the place you live?

</div>

### All Things in Christ <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#all-things-in-christ}

*Paul answers a divided world by insisting that everything belongs to Christ.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Colossians 1:15–23 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Count how many times Paul says "all things" in this passage. Then look at the pairs Paul uses: what kinds of things does he include? The notes say Paul "deliberately uses inclusive language," emphasizing "all things" both "in heaven and on earth, visible and invisible," in order to counter dualism.[^7] Why would that repetition have mattered so much to people who believed the physical world was second-rate?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> *Read Colossians 1:9–14 aloud.* The notes highlight verse 13: God "has rescued us from the dominion of darkness and brought us into the Kingdom of the Son he loves," noting the past tense as "completed action rather than future hope." They pair it with the call to "live a life worthy of the Lord and please him in every way, bearing fruit in every good work."[^8] If the rescue has already happened, where does Paul expect that fruit to be borne?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The episode argues that the common picture of heaven as "some perfect platonic place, other dimension" reflects Greek philosophy more than the Bible, and that it can leave Christians feeling like "spirits trapped in prisons of flesh, walking in a world that is doomed to destruction, just waiting for 'some glad morning when this life is o'er.'"[^9] Think back to the songs and sayings shared at the opening. What do our different traditions' hymns, funerals and sermons teach about heaven and earth? What would we gain, and what might feel unsettling, if we took Colossians 1:20 as seriously as the hymns we grew up with?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes describe God's Kingdom operating "here, now, deeply engaged with this world of flesh and blood, soil and spirit."[^10] Name one physical, ordinary part of your life (your body, your work, your neighborhood, a piece of creation) that you would treat differently this week if you believed Christ was reconciling it too.

</div>

### The Appearance of Wisdom <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#the-appearance-of-wisdom}

*Paul exposes a spirituality that looks impressive and achieves nothing.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Colossians 2:16–23 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** List the rules and practices Paul mentions in this passage. What is his verdict on them? The notes quote Paul that such practices "indeed have an appearance of wisdom, with their self-imposed worship, their false humility and their harsh treatment of the body," but "lack any value in restraining sinful indulgence."[^11] Why do you think strict rules like "do not handle, do not taste, do not touch" feel so spiritual even when they do not change us?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say Paul answers spiritual elitism by insisting that the "treasures of wisdom and knowledge" hidden in Christ (2:3) are available to all, and they warn against tendencies toward elitism "whether based on education, experience, or theological sophistication."[^12] A group like ours, which loves learning the background of the text, could drift that way too. How might we keep what we are learning from creating insiders and outsiders?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes offer a test for any spiritual practice: "not whether something appears sophisticated or demanding, but whether it actually produces spiritual transformation."[^13] Choose one practice, rule or habit in your own spiritual life. Using that test, what is it actually producing in you, and what might you need to add, change or release?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Colossae was a small city that disappeared within decades, but the letter Paul sent there still speaks. The teaching he faced divided the world into a pure spiritual realm and an inferior physical one, and it produced both carelessness and harsh self-denial, along with an elite who thought they knew more. Paul answered with Christ, in whom all the fullness of God lived in a body and through whom all things, visible and invisible, are reconciled. The Kingdom is not an escape from flesh and soil. It is God's rescue at work here and now.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one ordinary, physical part of your life you will treat as Kingdom ground this week?

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 161 — 1 Thessalonians — Parousia](/guides/161)** (21:43): The episode reads 1 Thessalonians 4:16–18 against the Roman imperial *parousia*, when an emperor returned to inspect what a city had rebuilt with the money he left behind after an earthquake. It argues that Paul's picture of Christ's return is a call to active, accountable work in the world rather than a promise of escape from it.[^14]

**What to bring next week:** Read 1 Thessalonians 4:13–18. Write down, in a sentence or two, what you were taught about this passage growing up, and bring it.
