### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Obadiah condemned Edom for standing aloof, and then exploiting its brother Judah, on the day of Judah's disaster, and showed that God holds every nation accountable for how it treats people.[^1]

**This week:** Joel pictures the Babylonian conquest as a locust plague that strips the land bare, then turns toward restoration and the promise of God's Spirit poured out on all people.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Joel is three short chapters. It opens with wave after wave of locusts devouring everything, an image that blurs into the Babylonian army climbing walls and crawling through windows. The book moves back and forth between this picture and calls to repent, then pivots to hope: God will repay the years the locusts have eaten, and new wine that was snatched away at the start will drip from the mountains at the end.[^3]

The episode places Joel at a turning point in the prophets, moving from woes toward hope as God's people live through discipline. It highlights the call to "rend your heart and not your garments" and Joel's promise that God will pour out his Spirit on sons and daughters, young and old, even servants, which Peter quotes at Pentecost.[^4] Joel's promise touches questions our traditions answer differently, such as the Spirit's work and the roles of women, so the questions invite us to notice what the text says and describe how we each hold it.

### What the Locusts Have Eaten <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#what-the-locusts-have-eaten}

*Joel's opening picture leaves nothing standing.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Joel 1:1–12 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes describe four kinds of locusts consuming the land in waves, and the image of new wine "snatched from your lips," like a glass of fine wine pulled away just as it reaches your mouth.[^5] What do you notice about who is called to respond in these verses (elders, drinkers, farmers, vinedressers) and about how complete the loss is?

*Read Joel 2:7–9 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes explain that locust plagues were real events in the ancient Middle East, and that Joel deliberately blurs literal locusts with the Babylonian army, a "mighty army without number," using descriptions that fit both.[^6] Why might a prophet choose a natural disaster to describe a military invasion, and what does the blending do to you as a reader?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes describe three phases of prophetic voice: warning (before Assyria), woes (during the Assyrian and Babylonian periods), and hope (in exile). Joel bridges woes and hope, as if to say, "You have been disciplined—now hang in there because God is going to put it back together." The episode compares it to a parent helping a child through to the other side of discipline.[^7] Which of those three voices do you most need to hear in your own life right now, and why?

</div>

### Rend Your Hearts <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#rend-your-hearts}

*God asks for a torn heart, not just a torn coat.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Joel 2:12–17 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes say Joel's prophecy moves between vivid images of destruction and invitations to turn back to God, and this passage is one of those invitations.[^8] What does God ask the people to do in verses 12–13, what reasons are given, and who is included in the gathering of verses 15–17?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes explain that tearing one's garment from collar to hem was a dramatic, visible expression of grief in Eastern culture. The episode tells of a man who literally rent his garment on hearing of his father's death, and connects this to the tearing of the temple curtain at Jesus' death, heard by a Jewish reader as God the Father rending his garment in grief over his Son. The notes add that the curtain was reportedly four to six inches thick.[^9] How does that image change the way you picture the moment of Jesus' death?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes say Joel calls for "actual transformation of the heart" rather than "merely the outward show of religious emotion," and that "God consistently values the condition of the heart over external ritual compliance."[^10] Every tradition, whatever its style of worship, has its own outward signs of devotion that can become performance. Without pointing at anyone else's church, where is that risk in yours, or in you? What would rending your heart, not your garments, look like for you this week?

</div>

### Restoration and the Spirit <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#restoration-and-the-spirit}

*What was stripped away will be repaid, and the Spirit will fall on everyone.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Joel 2:23–29 aloud, then Acts 2:14–21.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes list who receives the Spirit in Joel's prophecy: sons and daughters, old men and young men, servants both men and women. They call this inclusive in scope, gender, age and social standing, and note that Peter quotes Joel at Pentecost to explain what is happening.[^11] What categories of people do you find in these passages, and what do you notice about what they will do once the Spirit is poured out?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes describe Joel's explicit inclusion of women receiving the Spirit as revolutionary, and list among questions for further study: "What does this reveal about God's design for spiritual community?"[^12] Our traditions practice the roles of women in the church in different ways, and this group holds a range of convictions. Without trying to settle that tonight, how have you seen this passage read in your own tradition, and what does Joel's list stir in you?

*Read Joel 3:18 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes point out that the book is bookended by new wine, snatched away at the beginning and dripping from the mountains at the end, and that the fountain flowing from the temple to water the acacias calls back to the acacia wood of the Tabernacle and God's provision in the wilderness. The episode asks: "Will we remember our own story?"[^13] What story of God's past provision do you most need to remember right now?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** God promises, "I will repay you for the years the locusts have eaten." The notes draw practical counsel from Joel for walking through hard seasons: don't give up, weather the storm well, resist despair, and trust in restoration.[^14] Think of the loss you brought this week. What would weathering it well look like, and what would it mean to trust God with its restoration?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Joel looks honestly at a land stripped bare, swarm after swarm, army after army, the wine snatched from people's lips. He does not offer cheap comfort, but he insists the situation is far from hopeless. He calls for torn hearts rather than torn garments, and he promises that God will repay the years the locusts have eaten. At the end, new wine drips from the mountains and God's Spirit is poured out on sons and daughters, young and old, servants included.[^15]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what would you like God to restore from your own "years the locusts have eaten"?

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 61 — Ezekiel — Strength](/guides/61)** (24:03): The series turns to the exilic prophets, whose voice shifts from warning and woe to hope and strength. Ezekiel's name means "God strengthens" (from *chazak*), and the episode introduces apocalyptic literature as images and symbols that convey hope to the present rather than a roadmap of the future. Its central picture is God's presence leaving the temple and going east with the exiles to Babylon, then returning with them.[^16]

**What to bring next week:**
- Read Ezekiel 1:4–21 and Ezekiel 10:18–19. Notice the wheels, the eyes, and which direction the glory of the Lord moves.
- Consider the question the exiles asked, "Has God forsaken us?", and bring one place where you have wondered the same.
