### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** John the Baptist questioned Jesus from prison. Jesus answered with healing, honored John publicly, and described a Kingdom that has been explosively advancing since John kicked open the gate.

**This week:** Jesus turns to the towns where he did most of his miracles, the most devoted communities in the region, and finds them in need of repentance. Then he offers rest to the weary in words his listeners would have recognized.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Chorazin, Bethsaida and Capernaum, which the notes call the Triangle, were "the most orthodox, devoted Jewish communities," people who knew scripture and lived committed lives.[^1] Jesus pronounces judgment on them and says Tyre, Sidon and Sodom would have fared better. The episode argues the issue was not belief but repentance, and follows Jesus's hints back to Ezekiel to find what those pagan cities were known for: pride, comfort, and failure to care for the poor and needy.[^2]

The second half turns to Matthew 11:28–30. The episode hears three Old Testament echoes in "Come to me," and suggests that each one makes a startling claim about who Jesus is.[^3] Along the way it introduces the rabbinic practice of reading by hints, which will return in later weeks.[^4]

### Woe to the Triangle <span class="guide-time">35 min</span> {#woe-to-the-triangle}

*Jesus speaks hard words to the towns that saw most of his miracles.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 11:20–24 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Look at verse 20 and the verses that follow. What exactly does Jesus say these towns failed to do? The notes point out that Jesus performed "most of His miracles" here, and they argue "The issue was not intellectual assent to truth but refusal to change their behavior."[^5] They also insist these towns were the "Harvard" of Jewish religious life, "not hypocrites in the two-faced sense, but deeply sincere believers."[^6] Why might it matter that Jesus says "repent" to sincere people, and not "believe"?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes observe that "their very devotion created a system that excluded and burdened rather than liberated and welcomed."[^7] How can sincere devotion and a failure to care for people live side by side in the same community?

*Read Ezekiel 16:49–50 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes follow Jesus's mention of Sodom back to this passage as a *remez*, a "hint" or allusion to another biblical text. It is one of four rabbinic levels of reading called PaRDeS, and the notes add that "God works equally at all levels."[^8] What sins does Ezekiel name? The original notes sum up the answer to "What was the sin of Tyre, Sidon, and Sodom?" in one phrase: "Not taking care of those in need. The mumzer."[^9] (The *mamzer* is the "illegitimate one," the outcast Matthew's Gospel keeps defending.)[^10] What changes when you read Jesus's comparison through Ezekiel's list rather than through what you may have assumed Sodom was about?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Marty applies this to his own evangelical world: "We are so wound up about preserving our comfort and the world that we feel like we built."[^11] The original notes ask us to keep the conversation light rather than heavy-handed, and to trade questions like "Do I go to church?" for one like "Am I the kind of person who is compassionate and loving and gracious and forgiving?"[^12] Thinking about your own life, not anyone else's, which of those two kinds of question do you tend to ask yourself, and what would change if you asked the second one more often?

</div>

### Come to Me <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#come-to-me}

*After the hard words, Jesus offers rest, and his choice of phrases would have stopped his audience cold.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 11:25–30 aloud. Then read Exodus 33:14, Numbers 12:3 and Jeremiah 6:16.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Match each Old Testament verse to a phrase in Matthew 11:28–29. What words or ideas do you hear repeated? Report what you find before reading further.

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes hear three claims in those echoes. "I will give you rest" recalls God's promise in Exodus 33:14, so Jesus claims to give what only God gives; "gentle and humble in heart" recalls Moses in Numbers 12:3, so Jesus presents himself as the new Moses; "rest for your souls" recalls the "ancient paths" of Jeremiah 6:16, and "The 'way' in Jewish thought is Torah," so Jesus claims to be Torah in flesh.[^13] The original notes say Jesus "would have been expected to say, 'God has said I will give you rest' or 'God will give you rest' but certainly not, 'I will give you rest.'"[^14] Which of the three claims seems boldest to you, and why would it land so hard on people who knew their scriptures well?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Rabbis had a "yoke," a way of interpreting Torah, and the notes say Jesus's yoke is light "not because it is easy (the way is still narrow)," but because it does not burden people "with guilt, shame, and impossible expectations while ignoring actual human need."[^15] The notes suggest this "challenges both legalism (which adds burdens) and cheap grace (which eliminates the narrow way entirely)."[^16] How can a path be narrow and the load still be light?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Jesus invites "all you who are weary and burdened." What load are you carrying right now that was never part of his yoke, and what might it look like to set it down this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The towns that heard Jesus most and saw his miracles up close were sincere, learned and devoted. Jesus did not fault their belief; he called them to repent, and through Ezekiel he named what Sodom was known for: comfort, pride, and no help for the poor and needy. Devotion that builds walls around the margins is not the devotion God asks for. Then, to the weary, Jesus said "Come to me," speaking in the voice of God, the humility of Moses and the rest promised to those who walk the ancient path. His yoke is narrow, and it is light.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: who is one person on the margins of your world that this passage brings to mind?

</div>
