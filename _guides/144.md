### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We stepped back to see the Bible as one story, with Acts as an open-ended epilogue and the letters as "inspired midrash," the same story applied to different communities.[^1]

**This week:** We open the first of those letters, Galatians, and meet the community it was written to and the pressure it was under.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This episode begins a verse-by-verse study of Galatians with chapter 1. Marty treats Galatians as likely Paul's earliest letter, written before the Jerusalem Council of Acts 15, and reads it with help from "New Perspective on Paul" scholars who stress Paul's historical setting. The letter's first readers included *theosebes* ("God-fearers"), Gentiles who worshiped Israel's God without fully converting. In Galatia, the conservative school of Shammai expected full conversion, and a Roman arrangement exempting Jews from emperor worship made conversion a matter of life and death.[^2]

Against that backdrop, Paul insists his gospel came directly from Jesus, not from human teachers or the other apostles. He points to three years in Arabia and Damascus rather than to his training under Gamaliel. Marty connects this to a gospel that has always fought for people's access to God's table, while distinguishing that from universalism.[^3]

### Belonging Under Pressure <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#belonging-under-pressure}

*We picture the world of the Galatian believers before we read what Paul wrote them.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes describe *theosebes* as Gentiles attracted to Judaism who worshiped the God of Israel without fully converting, and who found themselves "in a precarious position" in conservative regions influenced by Shammai. Hillel's more moderate school allowed some inclusion without full conversion, "though still not as full siblings in the family of Abraham."[^4] Imagine being a God-fearing Gentile in Galatia. What would you hear from your Jewish neighbors, and what would you want to be true about your place in God's family?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes explain the "Jewish exception": an arrangement between Herod the Great and Caesar exempted Jews from emperor worship in exchange for daily sacrifices offered "for" (not "to") the emperor. For a non-Jew, refusing to worship Caesar was a capital offense.[^5] How does this change your sense of why becoming fully Jewish would have been so tempting for Gentile believers in Galatia?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes call this "a case study for understanding how social, political, and religious factors influence religious conversion and community belonging."[^6] Where do you see social or political pressure shaping who feels safe in churches today?

</div>

### A Gospel Received, Not Inherited <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#a-gospel-received-not-inherited}

*We read Paul's account of where his gospel came from.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Galatians 1:1–24 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes' first takeaway is that Paul's gospel "was received directly from Jesus Christ, not through human teaching or the other apostles."[^7] Go back through the chapter. Where does Paul make this point, and how many different ways does he make it? Whom does he say he did and did not consult, and when?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Marty compares Paul's training under Gamaliel to having a "PhD from Harvard," and notes that Paul grounds his authority not in those credentials but in his direct calling and three years of instruction. The notes see a parallel with the other apostles' three years with Jesus.[^8] Why might Paul set aside his most impressive credentials when his authority is being questioned? What does that tell the God-fearers about whose gospel they have received?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Think about the credentials you lean on to feel qualified in your faith: a degree, a family heritage, years of attendance, a role in the church. What would it look like to hold those the way Paul held his?

</div>

### Welcome Without Universalism <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#welcome-without-universalism}

*We consider what kind of inclusion Paul's gospel announces.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes say Marty connects Paul's message to contemporary conversations about inclusion, arguing that the gospel "has always been fundamentally inclusive, fighting for people's access to God's table, while distinguishing this from universalism."[^9] Our traditions draw lines in different places (around baptism, membership, communion, or doctrine). In your tradition, how is the line between welcome and boundaries usually explained? What questions does Paul's fight for the God-fearers raise about where we draw it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode reviews Thomas Lancaster's book *The Holy Epistle to the Galatians* as a model of appreciating valuable scholarship "while maintaining discernment about theological conclusions."[^10] When you read or hear a teacher you partly disagree with, how do you hold onto what is good without swallowing everything? What practice could help you do that this season?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The first readers of Galatians were Gentiles who loved Israel's God and were being told, by respected teachers and by the pressure of Rome, that they were not fully in. Paul answers by pointing to the source of his gospel: not his credentials, not the Jerusalem leaders, but a direct revelation from Jesus. That gospel fights for people's place at God's table. As we read Galatians, we will keep asking who is being pushed to the edges and what Paul's gospel says to them.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: who is one person or group you want to keep in mind as we read Galatians together?

</div>
