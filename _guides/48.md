### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We met the prophets as God's messengers with present-tense words for real audiences, arranged across five historical periods, and we were urged to listen for the underlying message[^1].

**This week:** Amos, the first of the pre-Assyrian prophets, speaks to a prosperous northern kingdom and measures it against God's plumb line[^2].

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Amos spoke to the northern kingdom of Israel during a season of peace and economic success, before the Assyrian threat appeared. People were building stone mansions and planting lush vineyards, and many likely took their prosperity as proof of God's blessing[^3]. The episode challenges the common assumption that the prophets mainly condemned idolatry, arguing that Amos's overwhelming concern is justice: the exploitation of the poor and needy through unfair business, corrupt courts and taxes on basic necessities[^2].

Three images carry the episode. Amos condemns seven neighboring nations before turning the same formula on Israel. He calls the wealthy women of Samaria "cows of Bashan," a picture of excess rather than enough. And in his visions he sees God holding a plumb line against a wall that was once built "true to plumb" but has grown too crooked to stand[^4]. The episode closes with the Hebrew distinction between restorative and retributive justice and asks uncomfortable questions about how our own comfort is built[^5].

### Seven Nations, Then Israel <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#seven-nations-then-israel}

*Amos draws his audience in before he turns toward them.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Amos 1:3–5 aloud, then Amos 2:6–8.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Amos repeats one formula seven times, "For three sins of [nation], even for four, I will not turn back my wrath," against Damascus, Gaza, Tyre, Edom, Ammon, Moab and even Judah, before turning it on Israel[^6]. What do you notice about the formula, and what kinds of actions are named once Amos reaches Israel? Point to specific phrases.

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode explains a basic difference between worldviews: in Western thinking sin is mainly wrong belief, while in Eastern thinking sin is mainly wrong action. The notes suggest this is why Western readers expect the prophets to be about idolatry[^7]. Before hearing this episode, what did you assume the prophets were most upset about, and where did that assumption come from?

*Read Amos 4:1 and Amos 8:4–6 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes say the "cows of Bashan" image is about excess, not weight: fattened cattle on lush pastures, a picture of having more than enough, set against the biblical ethic of "just enough." In Amos 8:5 the merchants keep the new moon and Sabbath while waiting for them to end so they can sell grain[^8]. What does it look like when religious observance and a profit-first heart live side by side, and why might that be hard to see from the inside?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The hosts asked questions such as "How much does our comfort, luxury, and security come on the backs of vulnerable people?" and "Where are our shirts made?"[^9] These questions can feel heavy, and no one has to solve the global economy tonight. Where is one place in your ordinary week (shopping, work, money, time) where Amos's question touches your life, and what is one small thing you could pay attention to?

</div>

### The Plumb Line and the Anti-Story <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-plumb-line-and-the-anti-story}

*God measures the wall he built and finds it out of true.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Amos 7:1–9 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Amos sees three visions. After the locusts and after the fire, he intercedes and God relents. After the plumb line, God says he will spare them no longer[^10]. What do you notice about Amos's prayers in the first two visions, and what is different about the third? What does the sequence suggest about God's patience?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes explain that a plumb line uses a weighted string and gravity to show a true vertical. God built Israel as a wall "true to plumb," but it has become so crooked it must be torn down and rebuilt[^10]. The episode adds that Israel has become the "anti-story": rescued from Egypt and called to bless the nations, they have become oppressors who exploit the vulnerable, just as Egypt once exploited them[^11]. Why might becoming the opposite of your calling be more serious than simply breaking some rules?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> *Read Amos 8:1–2 aloud.* The basket of ripe fruit signals that judgment is not arbitrary but has reached its natural culmination[^12]. The notes also warn that economic success and national security are not automatically signs of God's approval[^13]. Where are we tempted, as individuals, churches or a nation, to read comfort as proof that God is pleased, and what would it take to check that reading against a plumb line?

</div>

### Justice That Restores <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#justice-that-restores}

*The prophets' word for justice is about putting things back where they belong.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The episode contrasts two Hebrew words. *Mishpat* is restorative justice, putting things back in their proper place; *diyn* is retributive justice, punishment for wrongdoing. A *shophet* (judge) is the one who actually carries out restoration, often at the *sha'ar* (city gates). The notes also connect *tsedekah* (righteousness) with generosity toward those in need[^14]. How does it change your picture of "God's justice" if its main aim is restoration rather than punishment?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe a familiar movie moment: when someone kills the person who murdered their family, another character asks, "So what does that do for you now?" The episode says we are "well aware of the futility of redemptive violence"[^15]. Think of a wrong in your own life, large or small. What would putting things back where they belong look like there, as opposed to simply seeing someone pay?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Amos spoke to people who felt secure and blessed. He told them that God had built them straight, to be a blessing at the crossroads of the world, and that their prosperity had been built on the backs of the poor. The plumb line does not measure how comfortable we are; it measures whether our lives match the story God called us into. And the justice the prophets long for is not revenge but restoration, the world put back together.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, where do you sense God's plumb line held up against your own life this week?

</div>
