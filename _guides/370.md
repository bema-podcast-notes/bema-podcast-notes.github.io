### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** In Isaiah 24–27, the hope passages belong to the same "day" as the desolation, so the collapse of ruthless powers becomes an invitation to choose: despair, or plant your feet in hope and say, "Hand me a shovel."[^1]

**This week:** In Isaiah 28–33, God turns from the nations to speak directly to his own covenant people about pride, misplaced trust, and the kind of kingdom he is building.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Marty opens by naming three pitfalls in reading the prophets: studying them from a cold academic distance, using them as weapons against other people, or taking every word personally and forgetting the original setting. Then he and Brent follow a thread through five chapters: pride that blinds (the "drunkards of Ephraim"), a people who keep running to "Egypt" for safety, a God who "longs to be gracious," and a call to choose where our allegiance lies.[^3]

Two images frame the episode. The first is the contrast between two "cities of David": humble Bethlehem, where David came from, and proud Jerusalem ("Ariel"), where he settled. The second is the closing picture of Zion as "a tent that will not be moved." Throughout, the hosts set worldly power (horses, chariots, alliances, winning) against a kingdom that grows through humility, self-giving, and becoming "a shelter from the wind" for others.[^4]

### Pride and the Two Cities of David <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#pride-and-the-two-cities-of-david}

*Isaiah warns that pride makes God's people stop watching for what God is doing.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Marty names three pitfalls in reading the prophets: academic distance (never asking how the text should change us), weaponizing (aiming the prophets' curses at others), and total internalization (hearing every word of condemnation as God speaking directly to us). His goal is to keep the discipline of the first, the awareness of the second, and practice the third "in a *healthy* way."[^5] Which of the three do you tend to drift toward when you read a prophet, and what does that tendency protect you from?

*Read Isaiah 28:1–4 aloud. Then read Isaiah 29:1–4 and Micah 5:2–5 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** Isaiah addresses the "drunkards of Ephraim" even though the northern kingdom had already fallen; Robert Alter suggests Ephraim's desolation is held up as a warning to Judah. Isaiah 29 calls Jerusalem "Ariel," "the city where David camped," while Micah calls Bethlehem "small among the clans of Judah." The notes contrast them as where David came from and where David settled: small and overlooked against powerful and impressive.[^6] What do you notice when you hear these passages side by side? What does God seem to prefer working through?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes treat drunkenness as a metaphor: "When we indulge ourselves we stop being watchful. We assume we already know God's ways, theology, and answers." Verse 10's Hebrew, *tsav latsav, qav laqav*, is often read as mocking the prophet's teaching as baby talk.[^7] Where do you see the confidence of already knowing make people, or churches, stop listening?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Brent connects Bethlehem to Deuteronomy's call to remember where you came from: in Micah's day the people heeded it and were spared from Assyria, but "within a generation they had lost the plot again." The notes suggest habits of remembering, like testimony, Sabbath, and confession, help individuals and churches stay watchful.[^8] What habit of remembering helps you keep your "Bethlehem" in view, and which one would you like to recover?

</div>

### Going Down to Egypt <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#going-down-to-egypt}

*God's people keep reaching for the power that once enslaved them.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 30:1–3 and 30:15–18 aloud. Then read Isaiah 31:1–3 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes point out that going to Egypt "made perfect sense politically. It was exactly what God had told his people not to do." Isaiah 30 then turns: "Yet the Lord longs to be gracious to you," and the notes describe God's discipline as part of his *mishpat* (restorative justice), with a constant chance to return (*shuv*).[^9] What do you notice about how these chapters move between warning and mercy? What does that suggest about the purpose of the woes?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Isaiah 30:10 describes people who tell the seers, "Tell us pleasant things, prophesy illusions." Marty cites Beth Moore's point that history will judge preachers who failed to speak the truth and also the congregations and institutions that would not let them, and he links it to Heschel's line, "Few are guilty, but all are responsible."[^10] Without naming any particular church, what makes it hard for a congregation to hear a challenging word? What helps a community stay open to one?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Marty says horses and chariots today would be "tanks and Humvees." Isaiah's audience did not have chariots; they were tempted to trust someone else's. American readers, by contrast, can put their allegiance in God and military power at once, which Marty calls a "luxury," and a trap. He is careful not to judge particular conflicts, but he notes that peace kept by alliances and arms sales "doesn't put the world back together."[^11] How does it change Isaiah 31 to read it from the side that holds the chariots? Where do you feel resistance to that reading, and what might the resistance be guarding?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Brent points out how often we return to the same habits, coping mechanisms, or securities that have hurt or enslaved us, "because they promise to deliver."[^12] Share only what you are comfortable sharing: what is one "Egypt" you find yourself going back to, and what does it promise you?

</div>

### Shelter, Streams, and a Tent <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#shelter-streams-and-a-tent}

*The kingdom comes as God's people become refuge for others.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 32:1–2 and 32:8 aloud. Then read Isaiah 33:20 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** "A king will reign in righteousness and rulers will rule with justice." The notes say that for Christians the King is Jesus, and the "rulers" (plural) are God's people, each one "like a shelter from the wind… streams of water in the desert." What heals the world, in this reading, is "not the right superpower winning the right war" but God's people becoming refuge, shade, and water for others.[^13] What do you notice about the images Isaiah chooses for these rulers? What kind of power do they describe?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The people already had a permanent temple, yet Isaiah's future vision pictures Zion as "a tent that will not be moved." The notes call it "the house David knew, not the house David built."[^14] Why might a tent be a better picture of God's kingdom than a temple?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q11:** Marty's core question is whether we believe God's kingdom comes because *we win*, or because God builds it through "generosity, hospitality, self-sacrifice, mercy, and forgiveness." He admits that many of us give God's way lip service while in practice trusting what "works."[^15] Where in your week do you most often trust what works? What would being "a shelter from the wind" look like there instead?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Isaiah 28–33 asks God's own people where their trust really lies. Pride blinds, and "Egypt" keeps promising a safety it never delivers. Yet even inside the woes, God longs to be gracious, and his discipline aims at healing. The kingdom Isaiah pictures is small like Bethlehem and portable like a tent. It grows when ordinary people become shelter, shade, and streams of water for someone else.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, name one person for whom you could be "a shelter from the wind" this week, and how.

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 371 — Isaiah — The Hulk Factor](/guides/371)** (33:25): Isaiah 34–35 returns to the hard reality that people keep saying no. Marty and Brent look at God's anger, arguing that it is not blind rage but comes from his love for shalom, is aroused by injustice, and aims at restoration. Isaiah 34 shows creation unraveling for "all nations," and Isaiah 35 shows the desert blooming as the redeemed return home.[^16]

**What to bring next week:** Read Isaiah 34–35, aloud if you can, and mark any phrase that reminds you of Genesis or Exodus. Come ready to say, briefly, how you were taught to picture God's anger growing up.
