### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We stepped into the synagogue, where the community gathered around the Text, and heard how a demanding education system makes Jesus's call to young fishermen all the more surprising.

**This week:** A new worldview arrives with Alexander the Great. Hellenism puts humanity at the center of everything, and the Jewish people split into five groups over how to respond to it.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode describes Hellenism as "the first systematic worldview in human history that placed humanity, rather than the divine, at the center of existence," summed up in the phrase "Man is the measure of all things."[^1] Alexander the Great spread it less by armies than by culture, through four pillars: education (the gymnasium), healthcare (the Asclepion), entertainment (the theater) and athletics.[^2] The notes trace the history from the Babylonian exile through the Ptolemies and Seleucids, the Maccabean revolt remembered at Hanukkah, the quick Hellenization of the priestly Hasmonean rulers, and the rise of Herod under Rome.[^3]

From that history come five Jewish responses that will shape the rest of Session 3: Sadducees and Herodians, who embraced Hellenism; Essenes, who withdrew to the desert; and Zealots and Pharisees, who resisted it in very different ways.[^4] The episode's most uncomfortable claim is that modern Western culture is itself deeply Hellenistic, which makes it hard for us to notice when Scripture is challenging our assumptions.[^5]

### The Gospel of Alexander <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-gospel-of-alexander}

*Before Jesus announced good news, the Greeks had a "good news" of their own.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes explain that every earlier ancient worldview, pagan or monotheistic, centered on the divine, and that the Greeks "fundamentally inverted this hierarchy." The gods were not eliminated but "demoted," pushed to the edges while human comfort, leisure, luxury and security took center stage.[^6] Greek myths of demigods reveal the assumption that "humanity's chief limitation is lack of power, not moral or intellectual deficiency."[^7] In your own words, what is the difference between a world centered on God and a world centered on us? Where do you see that difference show up in ordinary decisions?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The Greek word *euangelion* ("good news" or "gospel") predates Christianity. The notes say it was first applied to the Hellenistic message: "Greek culture is here to improve your life."[^8] What changes for you when you hear that the Gospel writers chose a word already in use for an empire's message? What would the early hearers of Jesus's "good news" have been comparing it to?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Alexander's four pillars were education, healthcare, entertainment and athletics. The notes say of education, "Control what people learn, and you control what they think," and point to modern examples like political discourse dominated by education, healthcare and entertainment, and congressional hearings on baseball steroids.[^9] As a group, list as many places as you can where these four pillars shape life today. Which of them has the strongest pull on your own time and attention?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The original episode notes ask, "Are these things evil? No but they come with a worldview."[^10] The notes name modern Hellenistic anthems such as "Make your mornings about YOU" and "Have it your way."[^11] The episode's discussion questions put it sharply: "Where does Hellenism have its talons?"[^12] Without judging anyone else, where do you notice a me-at-the-center story shaping your own habits, spending or expectations?

</div>

### From Hanukkah to Herod <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#from-hanukkah-to-herod}

*The history of these centuries explains why first-century Judaism was so divided.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** After Antiochus Epiphanes sacrificed a pig on the Temple altar, the Maccabees revolted and won, an event celebrated as Hanukkah. Wanting to avoid the mistakes of monarchy, they entrusted rule to the priests. Within twenty years, the notes say, those priests had become thoroughly Hellenized, loving Greek athletics and theater.[^13] The notes call this a "tragic irony" that "warns against assuming institutional religious authority guarantees spiritual faithfulness."[^14] Why do you think a movement can so quickly become what it once fought against?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe Herod as heir to the Nabataean spice trade, "the ancient equivalent of controlling the world's oil supply," and explain that the Hellenized priests, fearing loss of power, leveraged his wealth to make peace with Rome.[^15] What does this alliance of religious authority, money and military power lead you to expect when Jesus appears in the Gospels?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes describe a pattern of "desert clarity followed by urban seduction": after the harsh desert, the beautiful Greek cities with running water and entertainment made Hellenism look "obviously superior," making it easy to forget lessons learned in the desert.[^16] Think of a season when you depended on God in an obvious way. What happened to that dependence when life became more comfortable?

</div>

### Five Answers and a Different Kingdom <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#five-answers-and-a-different-kingdom}

*Five groups gave five answers to one question: "How do we maintain covenant faithfulness in a Hellenistic world?"*[^17]

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Here are the five responses as the notes describe them:[^18]

- **Sadducees:** priests who believed one could love God wholeheartedly while enjoying Hellenistic luxury
- **Herodians:** non-priests who shared that view and supported Herod's dynasty
- **Essenes:** priests who withdrew to the desert, devoted to Torah study and copying
- **Pharisees:** descendants of the *Hasidim* ("pious ones") who moved to the Galilee and pursued meticulous Torah observance, believing righteousness rather than violence would bring God's kingdom
- **Zealots:** also from the *Hasidim*, who chose violent resistance

Before we study each group in the coming weeks, which response sounds most reasonable to you at first hearing, and why? Which do you think a church like yours would most naturally choose?

*Read Matthew 16:24–26 and Matthew 20:25–28 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes suggest that when Jesus spoke of losing your life to find it, taking up your cross, serving rather than being served and becoming last to be first, he "wasn't offering spiritual platitudes but declaring war on Hellenism's fundamental assumptions about human flourishing."[^19] Read the two passages again. Which phrases would have sounded strangest to someone who believed "Man is the measure of all things"? Which sound strangest to you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> When the priesthood became Hellenized, the *Hasidim* moved north, and the Galilee "became known for passionate, fundamentalist Judaism." The notes connect this to why Jesus spent most of his ministry there.[^20] What difference might it make to read Jesus's teaching as aimed first at people intensely committed to resisting compromise?

*Read Luke 22:42 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q11:** The notes close by asking what the counter-cultural anthem of God's kingdom sounds like if "Make your mornings about YOU" is a Hellenistic anthem: "Perhaps it begins with 'Not my will, but yours be done.'"[^21] What is one specific place this week where you could practice that anthem instead of "have it your way"?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Alexander brought a gospel of his own: put humanity at the center, and education, healthcare, entertainment and athletics will make life better. The Jewish people answered in five ways, from embracing it to fleeing it to fighting it. Jesus stepped into a world deeply divided over that question, and his teaching about losing life to find it cuts against Hellenism's deepest assumptions. The episode suggests that we, too, live in a Hellenistic world, which means his words may challenge us more than we notice. Over the next five weeks, we will meet each group and look for ourselves among them.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one place where you noticed the me-at-the-center story today?

</div>
