### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** A woman shut out for twelve years reached for the tassel of Jesus' garment, trusting Malachi's promise of "healing in its wings." Jesus looked at harassed and helpless crowds the way Ezekiel said a true shepherd would, and spoke of a plentiful harvest with too few workers.

**This week:** Jesus sends those workers out. In his second major discourse in Matthew, he gives the twelve their mission instructions and is unusually honest about what it will cost them.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode begins with a structure: Matthew may be built around five major discourses, echoing the five books of Moses and presenting Jesus as a new Moses. Matthew 10 is the second. Jesus has shown his disciples how to live the Sermon on the Mount; now he sends them to "the lost sheep of Israel" to try it themselves. The twelve come from clashing worldviews, and they go with no money, no bag, and no spare clothes, depending entirely on hospitality.

Jesus warns that the hardest opposition will come not from pagans but from within the community of faith, and calls them to be "shrewd as serpents and innocent as doves." The episode reads his sayings about a sword and divided households through Micah 7, explains his demand for total commitment through the rabbi-disciple relationship, and pauses over a reference to hell, urging us to keep reading in context. Marty speaks honestly about a hard month of his own, which may resonate with anyone who has been hurt in church.

### Sent Out Empty-Handed <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#sent-out-empty-handed}

*Jesus sends a mixed group of disciples out with nothing, so that they will have to receive.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q1:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe a theory, supported by BibleProject and others, that Matthew is structured into five major sections, each ending with a discourse, paralleling the five books of Moses: the Sermon on the Mount (chapters 5–7), the mission instructions (chapter 10), the parables (chapter 13), and two more later. Marty even wonders whether the parallels run in reverse, "symbolizing a return to the Garden."[^1] What would it mean for Matthew's first readers to see Jesus as a new Moses?

*Read Matthew 10:1–15 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes sort the twelve by worldview: Peter, Andrew, James, John, and Philip from the Pharisaic "Bethsaida Boys"; Matthew the tax collector and Thomas among the Herodian-leaning; and Simon the Zealot, possibly with Judas.[^2] The episode compares it to "putting staunch Republicans in the same party with staunch Democrats."[^3] Look at the list in verses 2–4. What would it have taken for these men to travel and eat together? What does it suggest about the kind of community Jesus was building?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Jesus sends them without gold, silver, bag, extra shirt, sandals, or staff. The notes call this "a deliberate teaching method" that forced them to rely on hospitality, experience dependence, and "learn humility in receiving from others."[^4] The original notes add: "Be the message, not just share the message."[^5] What do you notice about what the disciples are told to do and what they are told to receive? Why might learning to receive be part of the mission?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Towns that refuse hospitality, Jesus says, will fare worse than Sodom and Gomorrah. The episode argues that "the primary sin of Sodom and Gomorrah was failure to show hospitality," pointing to Genesis 19, where guests were violently threatened.[^6] Jesus' logic, according to the notes: if pagan cities were judged for inhospitality, how much more God's own people.[^7] How would you describe the hospitality of your own church toward people who bring something unfamiliar? What would it take to become a person of peace for someone else?

</div>

### Sheep Among Wolves <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#sheep-among-wolves}

*Jesus is honest that the hardest opposition will come from within the household of faith.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 10:16–33 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes stress that the persecution Jesus describes comes "not from pagans but from within the community of faith": flogging in synagogues, betrayal by family. The original notes put Jesus' meaning this way: "I'm not sending you to the pagans, I'm sending you to the believers."[^8] Read verses 16–23 again. What do you notice about where the disciples will face trouble? Why do you think Jesus was so honest about it up front?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Marty shares that he was "having a bad month," and that he finds Jesus' honesty about opposition from within "soothing to my soul." Many listeners write to him about their struggle to "find their way in the church because it's not always this incredible place full of love and hospitality and grace."[^9] At the same time, the original notes ask a challenging question: "Do we neuter the gospel by assuming we're supposed to be this passive, cross-eyed, dumb version of believers that don't think critically and don't engage anything with any depth or complexity or nuance?"[^10] What does being "shrewd as serpents" look like when conflict arises among believers? What does being "innocent as doves" look like in the same moment?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> On "you will not finish going through the towns of Israel before the Son of Man comes," Marty connects back to the paralyzed man two weeks ago, where "Son of Man" was tied to forgiveness. He wonders whether Jesus is telling his disciples "they're going to have to get really good at forgiveness."[^11] How does that reading land with you? Where has serving others required you to forgive again and again?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** On the warning to fear "the one who can destroy both soul and body in hell," the notes say Marty does not deny or dismiss it, but notes that Matthew has not yet given a full teaching on hell. The point is reverence for God over fear of people, and we shouldn't "blackout theologically" when we hear the word.[^12] Our traditions teach about hell differently, and this group does not need to agree. What happens to the way you read this verse when you keep it inside its context of mission and persecution? What do you fear more than you should, and what would it mean to fear God instead?

</div>

### A Sword and a Household <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#a-sword-and-a-household}

*A hard saying about family division turns out to be a quotation from a prophet who ends in hope.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 10:34–42 aloud, then Micah 6:8 and Micah 7:1–7.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes say Jesus' words about a sword and family division quote Micah 7:6, where the prophet describes a society so corrupt that "A man's enemies are the members of his own household." Micah's chapter turns, though: "As for me, I watch in hope for the LORD." Jesus is not promoting family conflict; he is "diagnosing the reality" when justice, mercy, and humility clash with the status quo.[^13] Read Micah 7:1–7 alongside Matthew 10:34–36. What do you notice? How does the context change the way you hear "I did not come to bring peace, but a sword"?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes quote a Talmudic teaching: "The only relationship stronger than that between a father and son is the relationship bond between a rabbi and his disciple." Jesus' point is that "You cannot be half-in, half-out as a disciple."[^14] The rewards he promises are present-tense, so that "how you live determines what you reap."[^15] Where are you tempted to be half-in? What would being all-in look like this month, without abandoning the people you love?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Jesus sent his disciples out with nothing so they would have to receive, and he sent them to God's own people, not to strangers. He was honest that the hardest resistance would come from inside the household of faith. So he asked them to be shrewd as serpents and innocent as doves, to forgive again and again, to fear God rather than people, and to keep hoping like Micah. The cost is total commitment, and the reward is the life that grows from living this way.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each: where do you most need to be shrewd, and where do you most need to be innocent, in the week ahead?

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 105 — An Explosive Kingdom](/guides/105)** (44:26): From prison, John the Baptist sends his disciples to ask Jesus, "Are you the one who is to come, or should we expect someone else?" The episode explains two Jewish views of how God's Kingdom would arrive, one expecting swift catastrophic judgment and one expecting a Kingdom that grows gradually like a mustard seed. It argues that John's question was a challenge to Jesus' methods, and that a hard saying about the Kingdom "suffering violence" may really describe a Kingdom that is explosively advancing.

**What to bring next week:** Read Matthew 11:1–19. Come ready to describe what you were taught to expect about how God's Kingdom comes: suddenly, gradually, or something else.
