### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** In the season one finale, Jesus revealed himself to a Samaritan woman at Jacob's well, Nicodemus came "so close" to following, and Jesus told Eden, "I see you."[^1]

**This week:** After eight weeks reviewing The Chosen, we hear from the man who makes it. Dallas Jenkins, the show's creator and director, talks about how it is funded, how it is written, and what he believes his job is.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Dallas Jenkins describes what his wife named the "manna program": a funding model in which, despite the show's success, each season stays unfunded until God provides, much like the manna Israel gathered one day at a time. He tells the story of coming off a box office failure, thinking crowdfunding was "ludicrous," and then watching the campaign break the all-time crowdfunding record at $10 million.[^2]

He also explains two working principles. First, on set his job is to make the best and most entertaining show he can and to let God concentrate on Kingdom impact. Second, every scene must pass one test: "Is this plausible?" He notes that the best descriptions of the show have come from outside his evangelical background (a Jewish rabbi who called it midrash and a Catholic priest who called it "very Ignatian"), and that the harshest criticism has come from evangelicals worried about adding to Scripture.[^3]

### The Manna Program <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-manna-program}

*Jenkins reads his show's finances through one of Israel's wilderness stories.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Exodus 16:13–21 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Notice the instructions that come with the manna and what happens when people try to keep extra. Jenkins sees in this story "God intentionally creating a posture of daily dependence," and his wife borrowed it to name the show's funding model, the "manna program."[^4] What do you notice about how much each person gathers, and why? What do you think God is teaching through bread that will not keep?

*Read Matthew 6:11 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Jenkins hears the same posture in the Lord's Prayer: "give us this day our daily bread."[^5] What changes when you pray for today's bread rather than this year's? How does it feel to pray that line slowly?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Even as one of the most successful Christian projects in the world, Season 3 remained unfunded when this interview was recorded. Jenkins describes weekly "Red Sea moments," situations where the only way forward is for God to part the waters. His wife heard God say, "I do impossible math," and Jenkins calls this both painful and one of the most beautiful experiences of his life.[^6] People in any room are in very different financial places, so answer in whatever way feels right. Where in your life do you prefer to store up extra rather than depend day by day? What might a single day lived in the "manna" posture look like for you?

</div>

### Five Loaves of Craft <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#five-loaves-of-craft}

*Jenkins separates following Jesus, which shapes his whole life, from the craft he offers in his work.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Jenkins describes his primary focus on set this way: "When I'm on set and when I'm writing, I can't be thinking about, is this going to impact someone for Jesus? I can't be thinking about building the Kingdom. I have to let God concentrate on that." He compares his job to bringing "the best five loaves and two fish he can through his trade," and letting God multiply them.[^7] How do you react to that philosophy? What might be freeing about it, and what questions does it raise for you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Jenkins admits an irony: the show is celebrated as "free from Hollywood," yet nearly all of its cast and crew come from that system, and "99% of what Jenkins knows about filmmaking comes from Hollywood influence." He compares it to plumbing or architecture, which you do not learn from the Bible, though biblical principles like discipline and surrender shape how you work.[^8] Where does your own craft come from? How do your faith and your skill relate to each other in your work?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes contrast this approach with much Christian filmmaking of the past 50 years, where the focus on saving souls sometimes came "at the expense of quality," and Jenkins admits his own first 20 years may have fallen into that trap. He also says plainly, "I am not good enough to do what The Chosen has been doing. I'm just not."[^9] What would it look like this week to offer your best "five loaves and two fish" in your own work, paid or unpaid, and leave the multiplying to God?

</div>

### Is This Plausible? <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#is-this-plausible}

*The show's creative freedom has one boundary, and it raises a question every tradition answers differently.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Two of the most accurate descriptions of the show came from outside evangelicalism: Rabbi Jason Sobel, one of the show's consultants, called it a biblical midrash, a Jewish tradition of interpretive storytelling, and a Catholic priest called it "very Ignatian," after Ignatius of Loyola's practice of imagining yourself in a biblical scene. Meanwhile, the most vicious attacks have come from evangelicals who say the show violates the command to "never add to Scripture." Jenkins answers: "The Bible is the Bible. We're not adding to the Bible because your Bible has not changed since The Chosen came out. The Bible has a very distinct purpose. This is a TV show."[^10] This is a diagnostic question, and the traditions in this room may feel it differently. Why do you think the concern about adding to Scripture runs so strong in some churches, and what is it trying to protect? Does Jenkins's distinction answer that concern for you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Jenkins says "Jesus moments are five times more impactful when we've seen what led up to them." His example is the Shabbat dinner at Mary Magdalene's house that we discussed in Week 17: none of it comes directly from Scripture, yet it "feels the most like what this would have been." In an early church short film, a joke from Jesus after an arm-wrestling upset ("Even I didn't see that coming") drew a stronger response than verse-by-verse reenactments.[^11] Looking back at the Shabbat episode, does knowing none of it is in the Gospels change how you remember it? What did its backstory help you see?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** Under the plausibility principle, Jenkins can create new backstories for characters "as long as they're historically and culturally plausible and don't contradict the character or intentions of Jesus or the Gospels."[^12] Has imagining a Bible scene, through a show, a sermon or your own prayer, ever helped you read a passage more truly? Has it ever pulled you away from the text? How might you use imagination this week while keeping Scripture first?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Dallas Jenkins describes a project that has never been allowed to get comfortable: each season waits on provision the way Israel waited on manna, one day at a time. In that posture, he tries to do the part that is his, bringing the best loaves and fish his craft can offer and leaving the multiplying to God. His one creative boundary is plausibility, which lets him imagine the people around Jesus without claiming to add to the Bible. The notes picture the show's success as what God does "when someone offers their best work while maintaining the posture of empty hands waiting for daily bread."[^13]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is your "daily bread" request for tomorrow?

</div>
