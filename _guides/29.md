### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Jeremiah set the ar'ar bush, lush outside and empty inside, against the acacia, which looks dead but holds life. The slow-growing tamarisk showed Abraham's trust in God's long promise and left us asking, "How many tamarisk trees did you plant today?"[^1]

**This week:** Three final desert images: wadis, where you cannot see what is around the next bend; En Gedi and living water; and the wadi floods that are the desert's greatest danger.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The biblical deserts are not sandy dunes but rocky canyon systems called wadis, cut over thousands of years by rain rushing down from the Judean hills. At the bottom of a wadi you cannot see far in any direction, so the desert teaches you to follow a voice. Marty tells of getting lost in the desert with Ray Vander Laan and having to trust the guide, and admits that trusting this way is very hard for Americans.

En Gedi, the "spring of the deer," is an oasis in the desert, and it opens up the Hebrew idea of *mayim hayim*, "living water": water that comes from God as a spring or as rain, unlike the stagnant water of a cistern. In Psalm 63 David learns in the desert that God is his living water, and the prophets call God's people to become springs for others. The Sukkot water ceremony gives the setting for Jesus' words in John 7 about rivers of living water flowing from within. Finally, flash floods, the number one cause of death in the desert, change how we hear Jesus' parable of the wise and foolish builders.

### Wadis: Around the Next Bend <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#wadis-around-the-next-bend}

*In a wadi you cannot see the destination, only the next turn, so you learn to listen.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes describe wadis as deep canyons carved by rain that falls in the Judean hills and rushes east and south toward the Rift Valley and the Dead Sea. At the bottom, the rock walls bend left and right and you cannot see around you; the desert teaches you to follow a voice because you do not know where you are going.[^3] Marty recalls getting lost with Ray Vander Laan and having to trust the guide, and the attitude he learned: "Let's see what's around the next bend." "Let's see what the Lord provides around the corner." He adds that learning to trust this way is very hard for Americans.[^4] What makes not knowing what is around the next bend so hard for people like us, and what does the wadi suggest is the alternative to having the whole map?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes mention that Israel trips sometimes deliberately give no detailed itinerary, to teach dependence on the leader, and they describe this as switching from control mode to trust mode.[^5] Where in your life right now are you asking God for the full itinerary when you have only been given the next bend? What would following step by step look like there this week?

</div>

### En Gedi: Living Water <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#en-gedi-living-water}

*God is the spring in the desert, and God asks us to become springs for others.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Psalm 63:1–8 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** En Gedi, an oasis in the desert, gives us the image of *mayim hayim* ("living water"). The notes read Psalm 63, written when David was in the Desert of Judah, as David saying that in the desert he has learned God is his living water, his En Gedi.[^6] As you listen, what words of thirst and satisfaction do you hear? What does David remember, and when does he remember it?

*Read Jeremiah 2:13 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q4:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> According to the notes, living water must come from God, which means it comes in only two forms: from the ground as a spring, or from the sky as rain. God offers living water, and the people instead dig cisterns, which do not even hold water well and offer gross, stagnant water.[^7] What are some "cisterns" people dig for themselves today, and why might stored, stagnant water feel safer than a spring we do not control?

*Read Isaiah 58:9–12 and John 7:37–39 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes say that, just as with shade, God is water for us and also asks us to be water for others (Isaiah 32:2; Isaiah 58:11).[^8] At Sukkot, worshipers shook their *lulavs* (bundles of palm, myrtle, willow and citron) so they sounded like rain, and a priest carried water from the Pool of Siloam to pour on the altar. During that ceremony Jesus says rivers of living water will flow "from within them," emphasizing that believers become conduits, not only recipients.[^9] The notes also record Marty's observation that non-believers sometimes provide safer spaces for honest struggle than fellow Christians, and that the church can be the "least safe place to be."[^10] Our churches have different strengths here. What, in your experience, makes a community feel like a spring to someone who is struggling, and what makes it feel like a cistern?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The episode closes this image with a challenge: identify who has been "En Gedi" to you, and consider whether others would identify you as theirs. The notes mention giving a photo of the springs at En Gedi, with a thank-you note, to someone who has been living water to you.[^11] Who has been your En Gedi in a dry season, and what is one way you could thank them, or be En Gedi for someone else, this week?

</div>

### Wadi Floods: Where You Build <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#wadi-floods-where-you-build}

*The desert's greatest danger is not the heat but the water.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 7:24–27 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Flash floods are the number one cause of death in the desert. Rain that falls far away rushes down through the wadis; the warning is a sound like an approaching train, with perhaps 40 seconds to two minutes to escape.[^12] The notes read Jesus' parable as being about location, not building materials: sand is found in the wadi bottom, where floods run, and rock is the bedrock and cliff faces above the flood zone.[^13] What do you notice in the parable once you picture a wadi? What changes when the question is not "what did you build with?" but "where did you build?"

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes connect this to building careers, relationships or identities in dangerous "low places" and to recognizing early warning signs.[^14] Without naming anyone else, where have you seen (or felt) the sound of the train coming, and what would it look like for you to move up to higher ground?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The desert has one more set of lessons. In the wadi we cannot see around the bend, so we learn to follow the guide's voice and trust that the Lord will provide around the corner. At En Gedi we learn that God is our living water, and that rivers of living water are meant to flow from within us to people in their own deserts. And in the wadi floods we learn that wisdom is about where we build. As Marty puts it, the desert is a "crucible" that strips away our self-reliance and teaches us to trust.[^15]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: who will you thank as your En Gedi, or for whom will you try to be living water, this week?

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 30 — Lead with Your Voice](/guides/30)** (40:23): With the desert images in hand, we return to the story in Numbers 20, where Moses strikes the rock and is told he will not enter the land. Marty and Brent argue that the usual explanations fall short and that the real issue is a return to Pharaoh's way of leading with the stick instead of God's way of leading with the voice. The story is deliberately paired with the rock at Massah in Exodus 17.[^16]

**What to bring next week:**
- Read Exodus 17:1–7 and Numbers 20:1–13 side by side and list at least three differences between the two stories.
- Note which explanation you were taught for why Moses could not enter the land.
