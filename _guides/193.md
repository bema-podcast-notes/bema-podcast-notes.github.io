### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Marty told us up front that every history is a story told with bias, and 2 Peter 3 left us asking why the return of Christ, which seemed so near to the first believers, did not come.[^1]

**This week:** The history begins. Marty traces how Jewish and Gentile followers of Jesus came apart in the second century, and what the church lost when they did.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode describes the early Jesus movement as predominantly Jewish, with Gentile God-fearers worshiping alongside Jews, possibly until the early second century.[^3] Marty presents two scholarly theories about when the Jewish-Gentile schism happened. He holds the later one: when Roman persecution under Trajan and Hadrian targeted Jews, Gentile believers distanced themselves to escape it, and the rupture never healed.[^4]

The notes then trace the cost. Without Jewish teachers, the "walking libraries" of Scripture in a world without printing, Gentile Christianity drifted toward Hellenistic dualism.[^5] The episode also discusses Marcion, the Muratorian Canon, anti-Jewish writing among early church fathers, and modern Bible reading habits that Marty calls Marcion-like.[^6] It ends with a provocative suggestion: the schism may have delayed the return of Christ.[^7] This is hard material, and the episode itself frames it as a warning about patterns that can appear in any community.

### Before the Break <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#before-the-break}

*For a time, Jewish and Gentile followers of Jesus worked out hard questions together.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Acts 15:6–21 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Marty believes the apostles successfully used "binding and loosing," a rabbinic process of theological decision-making, at the Jerusalem Council in Acts 15: wrestling with how Torah applied to new situations while keeping unity.[^8] As you listen, notice who speaks, what each speaker appeals to (experience, Scripture, the Spirit), and how the group lands on a decision. Report one thing you noticed about how they disagreed and decided together.

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe recent finds in modern Turkey: synagogue structures with both Jewish and Christian symbols, inscriptions suggesting mixed congregations, and shared worship spaces, supporting David Flusser's long-unpopular theory.[^9] Picture a Sabbath gathering in one of those spaces. What would you expect to see and hear, and what about it surprises you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Marty's view is that the schism came in the early second century, when Roman persecution specifically targeted Jews and Gentile believers abandoned their Jewish brothers and sisters by claiming, in the notes' words, "We're not Jewish - we're Gentiles."[^4] The notes also record the reverse, that an exceptional group of Jews had once betrayed Gentile believers to Rome.[^10] What makes self-preservation so tempting when a community is under threat? What did that choice cost both the people abandoned and the people who walked away?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The episode presents this not only as history but as a warning: "The same tendency to abandon vulnerable communities for self-preservation tempts us today."[^11] Where, in ordinary life, are you tempted to put distance between yourself and someone more vulnerable so that you stay safe or respectable?

</div>

### What the Church Lost <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#what-the-church-lost}

*Losing Jewish teachers meant losing far more than relationships.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes list what Gentile believers lost: the "walking libraries" who had memorized Scripture, the Jewish narrative framework, interpretive tools, a living tradition of application, and access to the text itself in a world where printing was still a millennium away.[^5] Which of these losses strikes you as the most serious, and why?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Left without Jewish teachers, Gentile Christianity defaulted to Hellenistic dualism: spirit as good, body as bad, and even Docetism, the belief that Jesus only appeared to be human.[^12] The notes add that scholars are still learning about Gnosticism and some earlier descriptions may be overstated. Where do you still hear spirit-good, body-bad assumptions in Christian talk today?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Marcion rejected the Tanakh, kept only Luke among the Gospels, and accepted Paul. He was declared a heretic, yet Marty observes that much modern evangelical practice works with a similar canon: the Old Testament largely ignored, Paul heavily emphasized, Jesus's Jewish context minimized.[^6] The notes also say early church fathers like Irenaeus contributed important theological work while also writing anti-Jewish material.[^13] Treat this as a diagnosis, not a verdict: across your own Bible reading and the preaching you have heard, which parts of Scripture get the most attention, which get the least, and what might that pattern be teaching without anyone saying it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** People often tell Marty that "BEMA made the Old Testament come alive," a sign of how much had been neglected.[^14] What is one specific step you could take this month to reconnect your reading with the Jewish roots of the faith?

</div>

### Speeding or Delaying the Coming <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#speeding-or-delaying-the-coming}

*Last week's passage returns with a sobering question attached.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read 2 Peter 3:11–13 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe third-century Rome cycling between persecution and tolerance and scapegoating Christians to distract from its own problems, and Marty draws parallels to modern rhetoric that blames "those people" for a society's troubles.[^15] What helps a community notice when it is being invited to scapegoat someone?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** Marty suggests the schism between Jew and Gentile may have been the "something" that delayed Christ's return, the rupture that kept the age to come from arriving as the apostles expected.[^7] Hold that alongside Peter's call to "speed the coming." What would it look like for you to help repair, rather than widen, a divide between people this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> This week we watched the family of Jesus split apart, only a few generations after the Jerusalem Council had worked to hold Jews and Gentiles together. Under pressure, self-preservation won, and Gentile believers lost their Jewish brothers and sisters, their teachers, and much of their grip on the text. The church that followed often read Scripture through borrowed philosophy and carried prejudice into its earliest writings. The episode asks us not to look away, because the same temptations to abandon the vulnerable and to scapegoat others are still with us, and repairing what was broken is part of the work Peter calls us to.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one thing from this week's history that you want to keep in front of you as we continue?

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 194 — AD 300–500](/guides/194)** (23:41): Constantine's conversion ends persecution and begins what Marty calls Christendom, roughly 1,400 years of "imperial Christianity." Four great councils define who Christ is, and Marty asks what changed when orthodoxy became about affirming beliefs more than living a particular way.[^16]

**What to bring next week:** Read Acts 15:22–29 and notice what the Jerusalem Council actually asked of Gentile believers. If your church uses a creed or statement of faith, bring a copy or a line you remember from it.
