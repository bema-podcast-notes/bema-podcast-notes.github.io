### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** The Tabernacle retold the creation story in the middle of Israel's camp, and when it opened the people fell on their faces in joy rather than fear.

**This week:** Marty takes one lesson from the Tabernacle (God's people create a space and God fills it) and turns it into a practical tour of spiritual disciplines.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This episode steps out of the Exodus narrative to ask a practical question. Marty draws on a lesson from his teacher Ray: in creation, God made a space and told humanity to fill it ("be fruitful and multiply"), and that did not go so well. With the Tabernacle, God reverses the pattern and asks his people to create the space so that he can fill it. From this Marty draws a principle he admits is not stated directly in Scripture but which he believes: "If you create the space, G-d will fill it."

Most of the episode is a list of spiritual disciplines beyond the two our culture talks about most, Bible study and prayer. Marty describes prayer disciplines, disciplines of presence (solitude, silence, Sabbath, simplicity), lifestyle disciplines (generosity, submission, gratitude, memorization), and the rhythm of fasting and feasting. He tells the story of a mentor who sent him to sit by a lake for eight hours. The goal, he says, is not to do every one of these practices, but to find the ones that make room for God in your season of life.

### If You Create the Space <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#if-you-create-the-space}

*God reverses the creation pattern: his people make the space and he fills it.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Marty describes Ray's lesson as a reversal. In the creation story, God created a space and told humanity to fill it. In the retelling of creation with the Tabernacle, God says, in effect, I want you to create the space and I will fill it.[^1]

*Read Genesis 1:26–28 and Exodus 40:34–35 aloud.*

Who makes the space and who fills it in each passage? What do you notice about the difference?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty's mentor Bill Westfall once told him, "I had a challenge for you but you know... nevermind. I don't think it's for you." The challenge turned out to be taking a sack lunch to a lake and sitting in silence for eight hours. It was not until about the five- or six-hour mark that Marty could empty his mind of busyness and settle.[^2] What is your honest reaction to that challenge? What do you think would surface in you around hour three?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Marty says, "If you create the space, G-d will fill it," and adds that God "may not fill the space in the way that we would like him to fill it, but he will fill the space nonetheless." He is open that this principle is not directly from Scripture.[^3] On times when God feels distant he says, "If we're not going to create a space for G-d, I don't know how in the world we can be upset when G-d doesn't fill our lives."[^4] Some of us will hear this as freeing and some as a burden, especially those of us who have been taught to guard against anything that sounds like earning God's presence. How does it land for you? Where in your life right now is there no space left for God to fill?

</div>

### More Than Bible Study and Prayer <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#more-than-bible-study-and-prayer}

*The disciplines are tools for making room, not a list to complete.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes list many disciplines beyond Bible study and prayer, while stressing that the goal is not to do every one of them:[^5]

- Prayer: prayer journaling, fixed-hour prayer, contemplative prayer, praying the text
- Presence: solitude, Sabbath, and silence; practicing the presence of God; simplicity
- Lifestyle: generosity and secrecy, submission, gratitude, meditation and memorization, a spiritual mentor
- Calendar: fasting and self-denial, feasting and celebration

Our traditions differ here. Some of us grew up with fixed prayers and a church calendar; others were taught to be cautious about written prayers or seasons like Lent. Which of these practices did your tradition encourage, and which felt foreign or even suspect? Which one are you most curious about now?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** In fixed-hour prayer, as the notes describe it, an alarm goes off at set times through the day and you stop for 30 seconds to recite the Lord's Prayer.[^6] Let's try it once together.

*Read Matthew 6:9–13 aloud together, slowly. Then sit in silence for 30 seconds.*

What did you notice in yourself during those 30 seconds? What would it be like to be interrupted this way five times a day?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty used to picture memorization as "stacking," building up until he could recite a whole book. Ray taught him instead to memorize a weekly portion, then put it away and move on: "You've done your part now let the Holy Spirit do his part."[^7] What do you make of that approach? How does it change what "success" in a discipline looks like?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Before a season of generosity, the notes suggest deciding in advance: when I see a homeless person, I'm going to say yes; if someone asks "can I?" the answer will be yes; I'm going to double my tips; I'm going to give twice as much.[^8] Read that list again slowly. Which line would cost you the most? What might God fill if you made that much room for a month?

</div>

### Fasting and Feasting <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#fasting-and-feasting}

*Fasting connects us to our humanness, and celebration is a discipline too.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes quote Brian McLaren: "Fasting does not create space where we are supposed to be more spiritual, fasting connects us to our humanness." Marty admits he got angry when fasting, obsessed with food when he expected to feel spiritual. He observes that, with two exceptions, people fast in Scripture for two reasons, humility and mourning, and that "We often use fasting like a vending machine to get answers from G-d."[^9] When you have fasted, or thought about fasting, what were you hoping it would do? How does Marty's view change that?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Brent asked, "Fasting reveals our weakness in a way that creates space for G-d to be strong in our life?" and Marty agreed.[^10] Where has weakness, rather than strength, made room for God in your life?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** On the other side, the notes cite the Talmud (Marty is careful to say not the Bible): "man will have to answer on Judgement day for every permissible deed he did not partake in." The calendar helps with both: Lent invites us to fast and Celebration Sunday invites us to celebrate, and Advent brings us down before Christmas invites us to celebrate.[^11] Some of us are frugal and need to learn to celebrate; others need to learn when to say no. Which do you need more right now, and what would one step look like this month?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> In creation God made a space and asked us to fill it. In the Tabernacle he asked his people to make a space so that he could fill it. Spiritual disciplines are simply ways of making that space in a crowded life: a pause for prayer, an afternoon of silence, a season of saying yes, a fast that reminds us we are human, a feast that reminds us God is good. No one is meant to do all of them. The invitation is to make room, and to trust that God will fill it in his own way.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: which one practice will you try this week, and when?

</div>
