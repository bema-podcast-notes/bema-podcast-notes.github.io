### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Dr. Jennifer Rosner named supersessionism as an often unconscious assumption and asked how Christians can affirm both God's ongoing covenant with Israel and the saving mission of Christ.[^1]

**This week:** Two essays from *Understanding the Jewish Roots of Christianity* argue that the New Testament cannot stand apart from the Hebrew Scriptures, and that Jesus did not set out to start a new religion.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Marty and Brent discuss chapters 2 and 3 of *Understanding the Jewish Roots of Christianity*. In the first, Dr. Mark Gignilliat argues that the New Testament "does not even exist apart from its relation to the Old Testament," borrowing language from the church's teaching about Christ's two natures to make the point. He opens with a story about Jeremiah, a lover of the Old Testament who was "aghast" at a chapel with ten painted panels, only one of them from the Old Testament.[^3]

In the second, Matthew Thiessen asks whether Jesus planned a new religion by looking at his relationship to the temple, ritual purity and the Sabbath. The notes conclude that Jesus affirmed all three while offering critique and deeper interpretation. Thiessen also adds an honest caveat: he no longer finds efforts to get behind the Gospels to a "historical Jesus" persuasive, a reminder to hold our reconstructions humbly.[^4]

### No New Testament Without the Old <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#no-new-testament-without-the-old}

*Dr. Gignilliat argues that the two Testaments are one continuous story.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes say the chapel with ten panels and only one Old Testament scene illustrates a long Christian tendency, particularly since medieval theology, to marginalize the Hebrew Scriptures, treating them as "mere backstory rather than essential foundation."[^5] Without blaming anyone, think diagnostically about the churches you have known. What signals, in what gets preached, painted or taught, tell people which part of the Bible matters most?

*Read Luke 4:16–21 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes say that when Jesus reads from Isaiah 61 in the synagogue at Nazareth, he is "not starting something new but claiming to be the fulfillment of Israel's story."[^6] What do you notice about where Jesus is, what he does before he speaks, what he reads, and what he claims afterward? Report what stands out.

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Gignilliat borrows the church's language about Christ's two natures. Christ's humanity is fully real (the notes connect this to resisting the "God goggles" that diminish Jesus's humanity) yet has no independent existence apart from his divinity. In the same way, "the New Testament has no being apart from its relation to the Scriptures of Israel."[^7] Put this analogy in your own words. Where does it help, and where does it raise questions for you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes draw out practical implications: reading the Old Testament "is not optional or merely historical background," and New Testament passages should be studied with their Old Testament references and allusions in view.[^8] What is one change you could make in how you read the Bible this month to take that seriously?

</div>

### Did Jesus Start a New Religion? <span class="guide-time">40 min</span> {#did-jesus-start-a-new-religion}

*Matthew Thiessen examines Jesus's relationship to the temple, ritual purity and the Sabbath.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes trace a long line of interpretation that set Jesus against Judaism: Martin Luther read the worm that kills Jonah's plant as Jesus killing Judaism, which the notes call "a shockingly anti-Semitic interpretation," and John Dominic Crossan argues that "Jesus saw himself as the functional opponent, alternative, and substitute to the Jewish temple." Against this, Thiessen points to Jesus calling the temple "his father's house" and to the false charges at his trial that witnesses could not make stick.[^9] Why do you think readings that cast Jesus against the temple and Judaism have been so attractive to Christians over the centuries?

*Read Mark 5:25–34 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Building on Mary Douglas and Jacob Milgrom, the notes explain that ritual impurity represented the forces of death; it was not sinful or morally wrong. Thiessen writes: "At no point does Jesus tell them not to worry about being ritually impure. Not once does he deny the existence of impurity and not once does he break the law in relation to those various people." Instead, Jesus removes the source of impurity, "almost as though he wants people to be free of ritual impurity so that they can visit the Jerusalem temple."[^10] What do you notice in this story about what Jesus stops, and what the woman can now do? How does this change the way you have heard the story told?

*Read Mark 3:1–6 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> In this Sabbath healing, the notes say, "Jesus literally does nothing" beyond two brief commands. "When confronted on these issues, Jesus always makes legal Torah-centric arguments to support his actions," assuming the sanctity of the Sabbath while arguing that human life is of even more value, and "In this, he's hardly alone among fellow Jews of his time."[^11] What does Jesus actually do and say in this passage? What does it suggest about whether he is rejecting the Sabbath or interpreting it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes suggest that this reading of the healing stories reshapes holiness: not separation from "dirty" or "sinful" people, but "bringing life, healing, and restoration that enables fuller relationship with God."[^12] Where in your life this week could you move toward someone with life and restoration, rather than keeping your distance?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> This week's essays insist that the Bible is one story, not two. The New Testament leans on the Scriptures of Israel for its very sense and cannot stand apart from them, and Jesus himself stands up in a synagogue and reads Isaiah as the story he is fulfilling. Far from founding a new religion against Judaism, Jesus honors the temple, takes purity seriously while defeating death's power, and argues about the Sabbath from Torah like the Jewish teacher he was. The invitation is to read the whole Bible as his Bible, and to practice a holiness that brings life.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one Old Testament book or story you want to spend more time with, and why?

</div>
