### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We met Hellenism, the worldview that put humanity at the center, and the five Jewish groups that formed around how to respond to it.

**This week:** The first group is the Sadducees, the priestly families who embraced Hellenism and controlled the Temple in Jesus's day.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode traces the Sadducees back to Zadok, the high priest chosen in the days of David and Solomon; the name comes from *Zadokim*, "descendants of Zadok."[^1] After the Maccabean revolt, priestly families took power and, within about twenty years, became thoroughly Hellenistic. By Jesus's time, seven chief priest families ran the Temple system in a way the episode calls a "religious mafia," backed by Herod, who auctioned the high priesthood to Annas, whose house kept it for about a century.[^2] The notes describe archaeological evidence of priestly wealth, a Temple Guard that acted as an enforcement arm, and an informal Sanhedrin that met in the high priest's house.[^3]

Two points of balance run through the episode. First, historical precision matters: the notes stress that the chief priests, not "the Jews" broadly and not the Pharisees, had Jesus arrested and crucified, and that this precision matters "for countering anti-Semitism."[^4] Second, even this group had a God-given role. Priesthood itself was good, and some priests, like Zechariah the father of John the Baptist, served faithfully inside a corrupt system.[^5]

### From Zadok to the Chief Priests <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#from-zadok-to-the-chief-priests}

*The Sadducees began with a legitimate calling and drifted far from it.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Galatians 4:4 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes connect this verse's "fullness of time" to the moment when Judaism was "experiencing intense tension while grappling with how to respond to Hellenism."[^6] They then trace the Sadducees from Zadok's line to the priestly rulers who, after the Maccabean victory, "became completely Hellenistic" within about twenty years.[^7] What do you notice about how quickly that happened? Why might a group with a legitimate, God-given role be especially vulnerable to this kind of drift?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** Archaeologists have uncovered a priestly home about 300 yards from the Temple Mount with 17 bedrooms, 21 *mikvah* (ritual bath) pools and elaborate mosaics.[^8] The notes describe this as "wearing Judaism well while wearing corruption even better."[^9] What strikes you about the fact that a home this lavish also had so many ritual baths? What does that combination say about how outward religious practice and inward priorities can come apart?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes point out that ordinary Jews could not simply switch temples the way people might switch churches today. They had one Temple, and God commanded them to use it, so "every faithful Jew was forced to either disobey God or participate in a system that enriched corrupt leaders."[^10] How do you imagine ordinary, faithful people coped with that tension week after week?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes list subtler versions of the Sadducees' temptation, including "Using religion or spirituality primarily for social status, business networking, or political advantage" and "Prioritizing personal comfort, security, and advancement over service to God and others."[^11] Which of these subtler forms do you think is most tempting for people in circumstances like ours, and where do you notice its pull in your own life?

</div>

### The Machinery of Control <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-machinery-of-control}

*The episode describes how a small group of families kept control, and why that matters for reading the arrest of Jesus.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** According to the notes, Herod auctioned the high priesthood among the chief priest families, a practice for which Torah "provided no mechanism." Annas won, and the office stayed in his house for about 100 years until the Temple was destroyed in AD 70.[^12] The notes also describe two Sanhedrins: a formal council evenly divided between Pharisees and Sadducees, and an informal one in the high priest's house whose decisions the formal council rubber-stamped, because "Everyone understood that opposing decisions from the informal Sanhedrin could cost you your life."[^13] What picture of power in Jerusalem does this create for you?

*Read John 18:12–14 and John 18:19–24 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Read the passages again and notice the details. Who arrests Jesus? Where is he taken first, and to whom? Who questions him, and what happens when he answers? The notes describe the Temple Guard not as "good Jewish people with swords trying to do the right thing" but as the enforcement arm of the priestly leadership.[^14] How do the details you noticed fit, or not fit, the picture the episode paints?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes say common Christian teaching has often blamed "the Pharisees and Sadducees" or even "the Jews" for Jesus's death, which "represents a fundamental misunderstanding of first-century Judaism."[^15] They point out that Jesus spent three contentious but nonviolent years with the Pharisees, who "actually tried to save Jesus' life twice," and only one week with the Sadducees, whose chief priests had him arrested and crucified.[^16] How was the story of Jesus's death told in the church or tradition where you first learned it? What does the notes' insistence that "Generalizations obscure important truths and can feed prejudice" ask of us when we read and teach the Gospels?[^17]

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes compare the informal Sanhedrin to situations where "real decisions happen in private among the powerful, then get rubber-stamped publicly," whether in boardrooms, politics or church structures.[^18] Where have you seen that pattern, and what helped or would have helped bring decisions into the open?

</div>

### A Righteous Priest in a Corrupt System <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#a-righteous-priest-in-a-corrupt-system}

*Even inside the Sadducees' world, the episode finds a faithful priest.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Luke 1:5–7 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** Read the passage again. What does Luke tell us about Zechariah and Elizabeth: their family line, their character, their circumstances? The notes say that calling a priest righteous was "an almost oxymoronic designation in that context," indicating that Zechariah fulfilled his duties faithfully despite the corrupt system.[^19] What do you think it took to stay faithful in that setting?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes insist that the Sadducees held "a God-ordained role" and that "The priestly function itself was good and necessary."[^20] Why do you think the episode takes care to name something positive even in a group it critiques so sharply? How might that habit shape how we talk about groups we disagree with?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q11:** The notes recognize that many people have experienced corrupt or harmful spiritual leadership. The episode's discussion questions ask what the most appropriate response is: "The ability to voice our truth; that boundaries be set?" "That someone should be held accountable?" "Where does forgiveness play a part?"[^21] No one needs to share personal history here. In general terms, which of these responses do you think communities most often skip, and what would it look like for your community, or for you, to take that one seriously?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The Sadducees began with a real calling from God: the line of Zadok, entrusted with the priesthood. Within a generation of the Maccabean victory, that calling had been bent toward wealth, comfort and power, protected by auctions, guards and backroom councils. The notes insist on precision: it was this small circle of chief priests, not the Jewish people and not the Pharisees, who moved against Jesus. And they insist on balance, reminding us that a priest like Zechariah could still be called righteous in that system. The Sadducees warn us how quickly a good role can be bent toward personal gain, and they invite us to examine our own.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one way you want to use whatever influence you have differently after today?

</div>
