### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Jacob, the "overreacher," took the birthright and the blessing, met God in a nowhere place called Bethel, and met his match in Laban. The notes suggested God prefers a fire in the belly to apathy.[^1]

**This week:** On his way home to face Esau, Jacob wrestles through the night and comes away limping, with a new name. The episode calls wrestling with God a defining mark of God's people, and then shows that one night did not finish Jacob's transformation.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode covers Genesis 32–35. Jacob, afraid of Esau, arranges his family in a defensive line, then spends a night wrestling a figure the text calls "a man." He demands a blessing, is asked his name, and is renamed Israel. The notes treat this as a major premise of BEMA: God chooses people who are willing to wrestle with him, and questions are part of faith rather than a threat to it.

The chapters that follow are messy. Jacob tells Esau he will meet him in Seir but settles in Sukkoth instead. His daughter Dinah is assaulted and his sons take violent revenge on Shechem. God renames him a second time at Bethel, and Rachel dies giving birth to Benjamin. The notes see in all this a transformation that is real but not linear. Churches differ in how welcome questions and doubts have felt, so this week invites us to describe our experience rather than judge anyone's tradition.

### Wrestling at the Jabbok <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#wrestling-at-the-jabbok}

*The night before he meets Esau, Jacob wrestles a stranger and will not let go.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 32:22–32 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes point out that the text says Jacob wrestles with "a man," and that Jacob speaks of wrestling with God only when he remembers back. They also note that Jacob asks a stranger for a blessing, which says a lot about the power of words in this culture.[^3] Walking through the passage again, what details do you notice about the wrestler, what Jacob asks for, and what he carries away from the fight?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes explain that in Hebrew culture names communicate essence and identity, not just labels, and that name changes typically come from someone with authority. Before Jacob receives a new name he must say his old one, "heel holder," and the question "What is your name?" forces him to confront who he really is.[^4] Why might it matter that Jacob has to say his own name out loud before he receives a new one?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes ask whether Jacob wrestled with God literally or figuratively wrestled with God through his life. The hosts also discuss other readings of the wrestler, including "the spirit of Esau."[^5] How do these different readings change what you think Jacob was really wrestling with that night?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes call this a major premise for BEMA: "G-d chooses people who are willing to wrestle with him." They also suggest that churches which discourage questions can miss the biblical model of faith.[^6] Our traditions have handled questions in different ways, and many of us have experienced more than one. Thinking about your own story, where did you learn whether wrestling with God was welcome, and what is one question you are wrestling with now (if you are willing to share it)?

</div>

### The Turning Point That Wasn't <span class="guide-time">35 min</span> {#the-turning-point-that-wasnt}

*After the wrestling match, Jacob's story keeps going, and not in a straight line.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 33:12–17 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes observe that it is common to believe the wrestling match is the turning point in Jacob's story. "However, Jacob decieves Esau when he says they'll meet in Seir but instead, Jacob goes and settles in Sukkoth."[^7] What do you notice in this passage about how Jacob speaks to Esau and what he actually does?

*Read Genesis 35:1–15 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes point out two things about this return to Bethel. Abraham and Isaac built altars and "called on the name of the Lord," but the text does not say Jacob did. And God changes Jacob's name a second time, which raises the question: hadn't God already changed it?[^8] The notes offer a possible answer: "Israel means Conquored G-d but it can just as easily mean G-d Conquored." At the Jabbok, Jacob won; the second time, perhaps God finally takes hold of Jacob's life.[^9] What do you make of this two-step renaming, and how does it change the way you read the night at the Jabbok?

*Read Genesis 35:16–20 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes compare this scene to earlier moments in Genesis (Adam and Eve, and Noah leaving the ark) where "be fruitful and increase in number" is followed by tragedy. As Rachel dies she names her son Ben-Oni, which the notes translate "son of my trouble," but Jacob names him Benjamin, "son of my right hand." The notes also say Jacob later takes responsibility for Rachel's death because of the oath he swore when Laban searched for his stolen idols.[^10] What do you notice about the two names, and what might it mean that one version of the notes sees Jacob's deeper change beginning only after this loss?[^11]

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes describe spiritual transformation as messy and non-linear: Jacob does not become perfect after his encounter with God.[^12] Think of a time when you believed something was your turning point (a decision, an experience, a commitment) and then found old patterns coming back. What did you learn about how God changes people?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Jacob limps away from the Jabbok with a blessing and a new name, but the next chapters show him still bargaining, still avoiding his brother, and still building altars without calling on God's name. God renames him a second time, and the notes wonder whether this time God is the one who wins. Israel, the name God's people will carry, comes from a man who wrestled with God. The episode suggests that the wrestling, the questions and the slow, uneven change are not failures of faith but part of what faith looks like.[^13]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one thing you are still wrestling with God about, or one thing you sense God is still wrestling out of you?

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 15 — Into the Pit](/guides/15)** (55:51): The story turns to Jacob's sons in Genesis 37–40. Joseph receives a second coat that marks him as the chosen heir, shares his dreams in ways that provoke his brothers, and ends up in a pit, in Potiphar's house and in prison. The episode challenges the "heroic Joseph" many of us learned, and traces a Hebrew word for "recognize" that exposes a family pattern of deception.[^14]

**What to bring next week:**
- Read Genesis 37 and Genesis 38, and mark every time clothing or the word "recognize" (some translations say "examine" or "identify") appears.
- Think about how Joseph was presented to you as a child, and bring one word that sums up that picture.
