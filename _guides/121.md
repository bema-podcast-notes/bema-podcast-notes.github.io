### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** On the mountain, the Transfiguration retold Moses' Sinai experience, Peter's idea of building tabernacles turned out to be sharp Torah thinking rather than bumbling, and the story marked the turning point where Jesus sets his face toward Jerusalem.

**This week:** We walk through all of Matthew 18, from the disciples' argument about greatness to one of the most searching parables Jesus told: a forgiven servant who will not forgive.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This episode covers a whole chapter. It begins with a child placed among disciples who are arguing about who is the greatest, moves through Jesus' vivid warning about millstones (an image his Capernaum audience could see around them), and compares Matthew's and Luke's versions of the lost sheep. It then looks at Jesus' instructions for handling conflict, where the guiding principle is to keep the circle of people involved as small as possible.

The episode builds to Peter's question about how often to forgive and the parable of the unforgiving servant. Jesus' answer of "seventy times seven" echoes Lamech's boast of vengeance in Genesis 4, and a close reading of the parable's last verses suggests that the unforgiving servant is handed the key to his own prison. The session also touches briefly on how the word "eternal" is used, a topic our traditions may approach differently.

### Greatness, Millstones, and Lost Sheep <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#greatness-millstones-and-lost-sheep}

*Jesus answers an argument about status by pointing to a child, a millstone, and a single lost sheep.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 18:1–6 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The episode suggests the disciples' question about "who is the greatest" reflects the many different worldviews inside Jesus' *havurah* (fellowship group), and that we never really outgrow this argument: "we simply become more sophisticated in how we assert our greatness."[^1] The notes point to social media posts, theological debates, and political arguments that work as declarations of "I am the greatest" dressed in spiritual or intellectual language.[^2] Where do you see that sophisticated version of the argument today, in the culture or in yourself?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Capernaum was known for making millstones from dark basalt rock, and they were exported as far as Laodicea. The episode describes the rabbinic principle of "never teaching about anything you cannot point to," and pictures a large millstone as being about the size of a very large pickup truck tire.[^3] How does a concrete, visible image change the way a warning lands, compared with an abstract one?

*Read Matthew 18:12–14 aloud, then read Luke 15:3–7 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Listen to the two versions of the lost sheep. The notes say Luke emphasizes the celebration of a sinner who repents, while Matthew emphasizes the shepherd's joy in finding, and ends with the Father not wanting "any of these little ones" to perish. That ending fits Matthew's steady concern throughout his Gospel for the *mamzer*, the outcast or outsider.[^4] What differences do you notice between the two tellings? What would each version say to someone who feels like an outsider?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q4:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> In this chapter Jesus also speaks of "eternal fire." The episode argues that the Greek word *aionios* ("eternal") is "a qualitative term rather than a quantitative one," describing the nature of a reality rather than only its length. It connects this to Paul's picture in 1 Corinthians 3 of fire that refines gold, silver, and precious stones but burns up wood, hay, and straw.[^5] Our traditions have taught different things about judgment and hell, so start by naming what you were taught and how this reading sits alongside it. Then, setting the debate aside, what in your own life feels like gold, and what feels like straw?

</div>

### Keep the Circle Small <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#keep-the-circle-small}

*Jesus' instructions on conflict are less a formula than a posture.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 18:15–20 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The episode says many people use these verses as a strict formula, but the broader principle is "keep the circle of involvement as small as possible," escalating only when necessary.[^6] Walk through the steps Jesus gives. What do you notice about who is included at each stage, and who is deliberately left out?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** If someone refuses to listen, Jesus says to treat them as "a pagan or tax collector." The notes observe that throughout his ministry Jesus has taught his followers to love, dine with, and welcome exactly those people, so this is "not a call to harsh excommunication but rather continued love and pursuit."[^7] The binding and loosing language that follows is applied here to personal relationships, giving divine backing to efforts at reconciliation.[^8] How does this reading change what you think it means to treat someone as a "tax collector"?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes contrast Jesus' small circle with our habit of posting about conflicts publicly, "involving hundreds or thousands of people who have no stake in the resolution."[^9] Think of a conflict you are carrying now or have carried recently. What would it look like to shrink the circle around it this week?

</div>

### Seventy Times Seven and the Prison Key <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#seventy-times-seven-and-the-prison-key}

*Forgiveness must reach further than evil does, and refusing it builds a prison.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 18:21–22 aloud, then read Genesis 4:23–24 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The episode explains that Jewish oral tradition in the Mishnah suggested forgiving up to three times, so Peter's offer of seven probably seemed generous. Jesus' reply echoes Lamech, from Cain's line, who boasted of being avenged seventy times seven. The notes summarize Jesus' point this way: "You must out-forgive evil. There is no end to forgiveness."[^10] What do you notice when you hear the two passages side by side? What does it mean to you that forgiveness has to outrun vengeance?

*Read Matthew 18:23–35 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes list several plain observations: the first debt is about three lifetimes of wages and impossible to repay; the servant's promise to "pay back everything" is a lie or a delusion; the second debt is about three months' wages; and the first servant "left the presence of a compassionate king completely unchanged."[^11] Which of these details stands out most to you as you hear the story read? Why do you think Jesus made the gap between the two debts so enormous?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The episode, drawing on a sermon by Reed Dent, offers a reading of verse 34. The word translated "pay back" can carry the sense of release or cancel, and the pronouns may allow the second "he" to refer to the fellow servant. The master could then be saying: "I will imprison you until you forgive/release the debt that the other servant owes you." On this reading, "the unforgiving servant is thrown into prison and given the key at the same time."[^12] The notes add that the debts we hold against others may be "real wrongs or simply unmet expectations."[^13] What do you make of this reading? Without naming anyone, is there a debt you are holding that has quietly become a prison for you?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Matthew 18 turns the disciples' question upside down. Greatness looks like a child, conflict is handled in the smallest circle possible, and even the person who will not listen is treated the way Jesus treated tax collectors: with continued love. Jesus asks us to out-forgive evil, matching Lamech's seventy-times-seven vengeance with seventy-times-seven forgiveness. And in the parable, the real tragedy is a servant who received unimaginable mercy and walked away unchanged. The episode suggests we may be holding the key to our own prison.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one small step toward release you could take this week?

</div>
