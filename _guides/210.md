### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Whatever happened in Noah's tent, the Midrash pointed us to the same lesson: hurt that lashes out in insecurity becomes a curse that echoes for generations.[^1]

**This week:** Noah again, this time set beside Lot and Abraham, as three ways of responding to a world under God's judgment.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode opens with a reading from Rabbi David Fohrman's *Genesis: A Parsha Companion*, which invites readers to approach the Torah with "kindergarten basics": "Which of these things is not like the other?", "What happens next?" and "Where have we heard these words before?"[^3] Using that approach, the hosts compare three characters. Noah is "the man in the fur coat," the insulator who obeys but never intercedes. Lot is the assimilator who moves from pitching his tents near Sodom to sitting in its gate. Abraham is the engager who argues with God for the city.[^2]

The episode closes with a diagnostic question for each of us, "which one am I?", and with Marty's striking description of much of today's church: "assimilators wearing fur coats."[^4]

<div class="guide-q guide-q-opening" markdown="1">

**Opening Question:** When bad news about the world reaches you, what is your first instinct: hunker down, tune out, or lean in? Share a small, everyday example.

### Kindergarten Reading <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#kindergarten-reading}

*Fohrman's simple reading tools let us notice what the text is doing before we decide what it means.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 3:6 and Genesis 13:10–12 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Try Fohrman's third tool: "Where have we heard these words before?" The notes point out that Eve and Lot both look and see something pleasing to the eye, and that Lot's plain is compared to "the garden of the Lord."[^5] What words or images do you hear echoing between the two passages in your translation? What might the author be signaling by placing Lot's choice next to Eve's?

*Read Genesis 19:1 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes trace Lot's movement from someone who "pitched his tents near Sodom" (Genesis 13:12) to "sitting in the gateway of the city" (Genesis 19:1), the place where officials gathered.[^6] What has changed for Lot between these two verses? How does assimilation like that usually happen: all at once, or a step at a time?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Fohrman says that reading this way reveals "whole other layers of meaning just waiting to be discovered," and that the result is falling in love with the text itself.[^3] Which of the three tools do you think would most change your own Bible reading? Name a familiar passage you would like to try it on.

</div>

### Three Postures <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#three-postures}

*When God announces judgment, Noah and Abraham respond in very different ways.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 6:13–22 and Genesis 18:20–33 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Use the tool "What happens next?" When God announces judgment, what does Noah do and say? What does Abraham do and say? List the differences you notice, including what each man asks for.

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Abraham bargains from fifty righteous people down to ten. The Midrash explains why he stops: he knows there are ten people in Lot's household and assumes they will be righteous. The notes add that even Lot's sons-in-law refused to leave.[^7] What does the number ten tell you about Abraham's hope? What does the outcome tell you about Lot's influence on his own family?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Genesis 6:9 calls Noah "a righteous man, blameless among the people of his time," and the notes ask how to reconcile that praise with the critique that he never interceded: "Is perfect obedience sufficient, or is engagement also required?"[^8] How would you answer?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Marty imagines that if God announced judgment today, "there are so many Jesus followers that would say, 'It's about dang time,'" and he describes many Christians as "assimilators wearing fur coats," people who absorb the culture's values while insulating themselves from real engagement.[^4] The hosts also say personality does not decide posture: an introvert can be an engager.[^9] Rather than diagnosing others, which posture do you lean toward, and in which part of your life? What would one step toward engagement look like there?

</div>

### Arguing with God <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#arguing-with-god}

*Abraham's boldness is presented as faithfulness, not disrespect.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes describe Abraham's *chutzpah* ("boldness") in Genesis 18: "Will not the judge of all the earth do right?"[^10] They add that this kind of wrestling "may feel uncomfortable to some religious traditions," yet "the Bible presents this wrestling as faithful rather than presumptuous."[^11] In the tradition you grew up in, was questioning God encouraged, discouraged or simply never mentioned? What did that teach you about what kind of relationship God wants?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe the engager as one who stands "in the gap between God and the world."[^2] For whom, or for what place, would you like to stand in the gap this week? Name it if you are willing, and say what that might look like in prayer and in action.

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Noah obeyed and built his ark, and he stayed warm in his fur coat while the world drowned. Lot looked at the plain, saw that it was pleasing, and kept moving closer until he sat in Sodom's gate and could not persuade even his own family. Abraham heard that judgment was coming and argued with God for mercy, because he trusted the Judge of all the earth to do right. We are called to be engagers, which means wrestling with God and standing in the gap for the world. The honest question this week is which one of the three we really are.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: which posture do you most want to grow out of, and which do you want to grow into?

</div>
