### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We finished Galatians: Jews and Gentiles are both set free, with different callings that display one gospel, and freedom is for serving one another in love.[^1]

**This week:** We begin Romans by meeting the church Paul was writing to, a "blended family" pulled apart by exile and return.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This episode introduces Romans as a pastoral letter addressing real divisions, not primarily a theological treatise about salvation. The notes tell the story behind it. The Roman church likely began as a mostly Jewish community with Gentiles included. When Emperor Claudius expelled the Jews from Rome, Gentile believers had to lead on their own for several years. When Jewish believers returned, power struggles and identity conflicts followed among three groups: new converts from pagan backgrounds, mature Gentile believers, and returning Jewish believers.[^2]

Paul's argument in chapters 1–3 shows that no group has grounds for superiority, because each falls short of its own "measuring stick": pagans who made themselves "the measure of all things," mature Gentiles who could not live up to their own moral standards, and Jewish believers who had Torah but did not keep it perfectly. Righteousness, then, must come from God through faith. The notes also read Paul's discussion of sexual practices in Romans 1 against ancient Roman culture; this guide treats that reading as part of the larger argument and leaves room for different convictions in the room.[^3]

**Opening Question:** Think of a season when your church, family or workplace went through a "blended family" moment, when newcomers and long-time or returning members had to find their places together. What made it hard, and what helped?

### A Church Reshaped by Exile <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#a-church-reshaped-by-exile}

*We picture the church in Rome before we read Paul's letter to it.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes describe the Roman church as beginning "as a primarily Jewish community with included Gentiles, likely established by Jews who returned from Pentecost." After Claudius's edict, the Gentile believers "had to develop their own leadership and spiritual direction without Jewish guidance or their memorized knowledge of Scripture."[^4] Put yourself in the place of each group when the Jewish believers came back. What might the Gentile leaders have felt? What might the returning Jewish believers have felt walking into a church they once led?

*Read Romans 1:1–15 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** Listen to how Paul introduces himself and greets this church he has not yet visited. What does he emphasize about the gospel, about the nations, and about his hopes for being with them? How might these opening lines land with each of the groups you just imagined?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say these conflicts "often mask deeper issues of identity, belonging, and spiritual authority," and compare them to tensions between cultural, generational or theological groups in churches today.[^5] Where have you seen a disagreement about practice turn out to be about who belongs or who is in charge? What did, or could, help people find each other again?

</div>

### Three Measuring Sticks <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#three-measuring-sticks}

*We follow Paul's argument as he speaks to one group after another.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Romans 1:18–25, Romans 2:1–4, and Romans 2:17–24 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes say Paul addresses each group's claim to superiority by examining its "measuring stick."[^6] In these three passages, whom does Paul seem to be speaking to in each? What words or phrases signal a change of audience?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** According to the notes, the Hellenistic pagans used themselves as "the measure of all things," the mature Gentiles "claimed moral superiority over pagans but still failed to live up to their own standards," and the Jewish believers "possessed Torah as God's measuring stick but also failed to perfectly observe it."[^7] Imagine each group listening as the letter was read aloud. Who would be nodding along at each point, and when would they stop nodding?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes read the sexual practices Paul describes in Romans 1 as ancient pagan practices such as pederasty, part of a "Roman dominance culture where sexual roles were determined by power dynamics rather than gender," rather than modern categories.[^8] Our traditions read Romans 1 in different ways, and this group does not need to settle that tonight. Whatever your reading, what changes when Romans 1 is heard as the first step in an argument that goes on to say, in Romans 2:1, that those who pass judgment are doing the same things?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Every group in Rome had a reason to feel superior. What is your measuring stick, the standard that lets you feel a little better than someone else? What does it cost you, and what does it cost them?

</div>

### Righteousness from Outside Ourselves <span class="guide-time">10 min</span> {#righteousness-from-outside-ourselves}

*We hear where Paul's argument lands at the end of this first movement.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Romans 3:9–20 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes mention that Romans 3:10–18 strings together lines from Scripture, a "stringing pearls" technique tied to a Jewish sermon structure.[^9] As you listen, how many separate quotations can you hear? Why might Paul use Scripture's own voice, rather than his own, to say that every group has fallen short?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes conclude that "since all groups fall short of their respective standards, righteousness must come from outside human effort."[^10] If no group in the room can claim superiority, what would change in how you treat the person you find hardest to share a pew with?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The church in Rome was a blended family: Jewish believers who had been driven out, Gentile believers who had carried on without them, and new converts still learning the story. Each group had its own measuring stick and its own reason to feel superior. Paul takes each of them seriously and shows that every one falls short of its own standard. That is not the end of the argument but the setup for good news: righteousness comes from God, not from any group's achievement.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one measuring stick you are ready to put down?

</div>
