### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We traced the second-century break between Jewish and Gentile believers and what the church lost with it: its Jewish teachers, its interpretive framework, and much of its grip on the text.[^1]

**This week:** Persecution ends, Christianity becomes the empire's faith, and four councils define who Christ is. Marty asks what was gained and what was lost.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode covers the two centuries in which Christianity moved from a persecuted movement to an imperial religion. It begins with Constantine's vision before a battle, the Chi-Rho symbol, and his decision to legalize Christianity; later, under Theodosius, Christianity became advantageous and effectively the imperial religion.[^3] Marty calls the roughly 1,400 years from the fourth century to the French Revolution "Christendom," or "imperial Christianity."[^4]

Freedom from persecution brought new challenges: time for long-postponed debates, an influx of convenience converts, and Greek philosophy replacing the Jewish framework of relationship and narrative.[^5] Four councils (Nicea, Constantinople, Ephesus and Chalcedon) affirmed Christ's divinity, his humanity, his single personhood, and that he is fully God and fully human at once.[^6] The hosts honor those clarifications while asking whether they would have been needed if the church had kept its Jewish roots, and Marty describes the period as the start of a "downhill slide" in which orthodoxy became about affirming beliefs rather than living a particular way.[^7]

### From Persecuted to Powerful <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#from-persecuted-to-powerful}

*Constantine's conversion ended the threat of death for Christians and opened a very different chapter.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** According to Constantine's own testimony, he saw a vision of a shield bearing the Greek letters Chi and Rho and understood it to mean "In Christ, you will conquer." The notes list four ways historians read the story: genuine miracle, political genius, some combination of both, or a real vision that Constantine misunderstood.[^3] Which reading seems most likely to you, and what in the story moves you toward it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes distinguish Constantine making Christianity legal (ending persecution) from Theodosius making it advantageous and effectively the imperial religion.[^3] Why does that difference matter? What changes when a faith moves from tolerated to favored?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The early teaching manual called the Didache required those being baptized to swear off their old pagan way of life, dying to it and walking into a "brand new family, this Jewish walk." After legalization, many came into the church because it had become "convenient, efficient, effective, and advantageous," and research cited in the episode describes Christianity as a "fad among the Roman elite."[^5] What happens to a community's discipleship when following Jesus starts to cost nothing and gain something? Where do you see the same dynamic at work in churches today?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** If following Jesus in your setting costs little, what would a costlier, more Didache-like commitment look like in one specific area of your life this month?

</div>

### Four Councils and a Shift in Orthodoxy <span class="guide-time">35 min</span> {#four-councils-and-a-shift-in-orthodoxy}

*The councils asked precise questions about Christ's nature, and the way the questions were asked tells its own story.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes lay out the councils' progressive logic: Nicea (325) affirmed that Christ is God, Constantinople (381) that he is human, Ephesus (431) that he is one person rather than two, and Chalcedon (451) that he is fully God and fully man at the same time.[^6] The notes also explain that a Gnostic framework (spiritual good, physical not good) made saying Jesus was truly a man feel "dangerous and degrading."[^8] In your own words, what problem was each council trying to solve?

*Read Acts 15:22–29 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes point out that councils were not invented in the fourth century; the Jerusalem Council of Acts 15 gave a precedent, though the later councils were "set up for a rougher road" once separated from Jewish ways of interpreting.[^9] Compare the letter you just heard with the Nicene Creed's phrase that Christ is "of one substance with the Father."[^6] What kind of thing does each one ask of believers? What do you notice about the language, the tone and the goal of each?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty uses the familiar question to philosophy majors, "What are you going to do with that degree?", to describe highly educated Gentile converts bringing philosophical tools to questions the Bible was not written to answer in those terms.[^10] Where have you seen a good question get stuck because it was asked in the wrong framework?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Marty calls this period "the beginning of a downhill slide that I'm not sure we have ever recovered from," because orthodoxy came to mean affirming correct beliefs rather than living a particular way.[^7] The hosts still treat the councils' clarifications as significant.[^11] The traditions in our room relate to creeds and statements of faith in different ways, so approach this as a diagnosis rather than a verdict: in your own tradition, what tends to mark someone as a "real" Christian, what they affirm or how they live? What has that emphasis given you, and what might it have cost?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** If the people who know you best described your faith only by how you lived this past week, without hearing anything you believe, what would they say? What is one practice you want them to be able to name a month from now?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> This week the church stepped out from under the sword and into the palace. Persecution ended, crowds came in, and leaders finally had time to work through hard questions about who Jesus is. The councils gave answers the church still treasures, yet the hosts ask whether something was lost along the way: the Jewish framework of story, relationship and practice gave way to philosophical precision, and orthodoxy drifted toward believing correctly more than living faithfully. The Jerusalem Council had asked Gentile believers how to walk; the later councils asked them what to affirm. Both questions matter, and this week invites us to notice which one shapes us more.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, name one way you want your faith this week to be visible in how you live, not only in what you believe.

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 195 — AD 500–700](/guides/195)** (14:26): As Rome falls, the church moves from the "pointy end of the sword" to "the handle end of the sword," with forced conversions and anti-Jewish edicts. Yet the same era produces the monastic movements, which developed orthopraxy (right practice) and taught the church how to create space for God.[^12]
