### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Derek Vreeland's *Centering Jesus* put the slain Lamb of Revelation 7:17 at the center of everything, and asked whether our strong reactions point to the Lamb being challenged or to one of our idols.

**This week:** We return to the Psalms with Psalm 91, a psalm of bold, unhedged confidence, and ask what kind of protection it actually promises.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Reed Dent leads a close reading of Psalm 91, which he calls "the ultimate Mario star Psalm," after the video-game power-up that makes a player briefly invincible. The hosts begin honestly: each of them has a cynical streak that wants to argue with the psalm's absolute promises. They then walk through its mixed images (soft feathers beside hard shields and fortresses), two different Hebrew words for "dwelling," and a warning against treating the psalm as an amulet or magic formula.

The turning point is Matthew 4, where Satan quotes Psalm 91 to Jesus and leaves out a key line. Jesus refuses to use the psalm as a shortcut around suffering, and that refusal reframes the whole psalm: the real "Mario star" is resurrection life, which carries us through death rather than keeping us from it. The episode ends with Ruth and Boaz and a challenge: people who shelter under God's wings are called to become shelter for others.

### Feathers and Fortresses <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#feathers-and-fortresses}

*Psalm 91 piles up images of protection that do not obviously belong together.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Psalm 91:1–13 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes point out that the psalm mixes soft, intimate images (shelter, shadow, feathers, wings) with hard military ones (shield, rampart, fortress), and that it even promises rescue from the "fowler's snare" while picturing God as the bird[^1]. Look back over verses 1–13. Which images stand out to you, and what does putting the soft and the hard side by side say about how God protects?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Brent noticed that "dwells" in verse 1 and "dwelling" in verse 9 are different Hebrew words. Verse 1 uses *yashav* ("to sit"), a moment of resting under shelter. Verse 9 uses *ma'on* ("habitation"), a settled home, used both of God's dwelling in the temple and of an animal's den it returns to each night. Brent's reading: "If you want the true star power, you've got to *live* in this."[^2] What is the difference in your own life between sitting with God now and then and making God the place you keep coming back to?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Reed asked what it means to "make the Most High your dwelling." Marty answered with Sabbath, Reed with following Jesus wherever he leads. Then Marty added a key qualification: "The practice is not the refuge." Practices are containers that put us in contact with God's faithfulness, which is the real refuge[^3]. Our traditions value different practices (the Lord's Supper, Sunday worship, Scripture reading, prayer books, service). Without ranking them, which practice has most often been a "container" for you, and when have you caught yourself trusting the practice instead of the One it points to?

</div>

### The Star That Carries You Through <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-star-that-carries-you-through}

*Satan's use of Psalm 91 in the wilderness becomes the key to reading it rightly.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 4:5–7 aloud, then read Psalm 91:11–12 and 14–16 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Compare what Satan quotes with what the psalm says. Brent noticed that Satan leaves out "to guard you in all your ways," and the notes add that he also cuts out the relationship in verses 14–15: loving God, acknowledging God's name, calling on God[^4]. What do you notice about what is missing, and what does Satan's version of the psalm turn God into?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Reed cited Robert Alter's note that some have described Psalm 91 as an amulet, words recited to gain God's protection. Reed warned that the second half of verse 9, making the Most High your dwelling, turns it from a formula into a relationship[^5]. Where have you seen Scripture used "like a mantra" to make God do something, and what is the difference between that and praying a verse as an expression of trust?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Reed says the temptation is about how the kingdom will come. Satan offers a shortcut with no suffering, persecution or cross, and Jesus refuses because "the way in which the kingdom comes and the essence of the kingdom are one and the same thing."[^6] Why would a kingdom built on self-giving love be unable to arrive by a route where nothing bad ever happens? What does it change to read Psalm 91, as the notes urge, knowing that Jesus was crucified?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Marty sums it up: "This isn't the Mario star that keeps you from death. This is the Mario star... that carries you *through* it." He also calls the psalm "the game board on which it's being played," courage for walking God's way rather than leverage for our own[^7]. Some of us have prayed promises like these and still been hurt, so share only what you are comfortable sharing. Where in your life right now do you need courage to walk through something rather than a way around it?

</div>

### Growing Wings <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#growing-wings}

*The refuge Ruth found under God's wings came to her through people.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Ruth 2:10–13 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Boaz blesses Ruth, a Moabite foreigner, for taking refuge under God's wings, yet in the story that refuge comes through Boaz and Naomi[^8]. Reed's closing challenge: when outsiders see us, do they see shade from the heat and shelter from enemies? "Or do they see one more enemy?"[^9] Who are the outsiders in your community, and what do you think they see when they look at your church?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes link this to Isaiah 32:2, where each person becomes "like a shelter from the wind and a refuge from the storm," and to Marty's point that truth we receive changes us and then we embody it[^10]. Name one specific person or group for whom you could be "wings" this month. What would that look like on an ordinary Tuesday?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Psalm 91 does not promise that danger will disappear. It promises that fear loses its hold and that danger cannot ultimately reach the person who has made God their home, sitting under God's wing until sitting becomes dwelling. Practices help, but the refuge is God's faithfulness, given "because he loves me." In the wilderness Jesus refused to turn the psalm into an invincibility shortcut that skipped the cross, so the true Mario star is resurrection: it carries us through death rather than sparing us from it. And those who have found shelter under God's wings are invited to grow wings of their own[^11].
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** Going around the room in one sentence each, with no comments until everyone has spoken: what is one hard road this week where you want Psalm 91 to be your walk-up song?

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 386 — Psalms — The Whole Human Experience](/guides/386)** (45:33): Reed guides Marty and Brent through Psalm 139 and Psalm 51 side by side. A single Hebrew word, *damim* ("blood" or "bloodshed"), links the "bloodthirsty" enemies David prays against in Psalm 139 with the bloodguilt he confesses in Psalm 51 after Nathan's "You are the man." The hosts explore what it means that every one of us is, at different times, both victim and perpetrator, and how a God who sees all of us at once still answers with mercy[^12].
