### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Lamentations gave grief a shape, with honest lament directed at God and a passage of hope at the exact center of the book.[^1]

**This week:** Habakkuk does something most prophets do not: he questions God directly, climbs his watchtower, and waits for a better answer.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Habakkuk is a dialogue. The prophet complains that God tolerates violence and injustice; God answers that he is raising up the Babylonians; Habakkuk objects that a holy God should not use an even more wicked nation, and climbs into his watchtower to wait. God's second answer includes five woes against those who build empires on injustice, and Habakkuk responds with a prayer that ends in one of Scripture's most powerful declarations of faith.[^3]

The episode draws out three things: the holy audacity (*chutzpah*) of a prophet who believes God welcomes honest questions, the way God's charges against Babylon mirror Judah's own sins, and a faith that rejoices in God even when every field and flock has failed. The book even ends with a note for the director of music, so it was meant to be sung.[^4] The episode is short, so this guide spends extra time in the text itself.

### Complaint, Answer, Complaint <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#complaint-answer-complaint}

*Habakkuk refuses to accept the first answer he gets.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Habakkuk 1:1–11 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes summarize the first complaint as: why does God allow injustice and violence to prevail, and why does he tolerate wrongdoing when the wicked oppress the righteous? God's first response is that he is raising up the Babylonians, described by their fearsome military might.[^5] What words of Habakkuk's complaint stand out to you, and how would you describe the tone of God's answer?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say Habakkuk embodies a tension we met in Jonah and Nahum: God's patience ("Why doesn't God immediately judge the wicked?") and God's justice ("Will God do nothing about injustice?").[^6] Which of those two questions do you find yourself asking more often, and why?

*Read Habakkuk 1:12–2:1 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** In his second complaint Habakkuk acknowledges God's holiness but asks how a pure God can use a more wicked nation to punish Judah. Then he climbs into his watchtower to wait. The notes say the watchtower stands for persistence in seeking answers, waiting for God's perspective, and *chutzpah*, "bold faith that believes God welcomes honest questions."[^7] Our traditions may differ on whether questioning God is faithful or presumptuous. What do you make of a prophet whose argument with God ends up in Scripture, and what is the question you would carry up your own watchtower?

</div>

### The Vision and the Mirror <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#the-vision-and-the-mirror}

*God's answer about Babylon turns out to describe Judah too.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Habakkuk 2:2–4 aloud, then Habakkuk 2:6–20.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes say God tells Habakkuk to write the vision plainly because, though it lingers, it will certainly come, and that God then delivers five woes against those who build empires through injustice, violence, exploitation and idolatry.[^8] Go through the woes in verses 6–20. What specific behaviors are named, and who suffers from each one?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes call Habakkuk 2:4 ("the righteous person will live by his faithfulness") a verse that becomes foundational in later biblical theology, used in Romans, Galatians and Hebrews. In context, they describe it as living in covenant relationship and trusting God's character "even when circumstances argue against it."[^9] Our traditions talk about faith and faithfulness in different ways. What does the setting in Habakkuk add to how you hear this verse?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes observe that God's condemnation of Babylon mirrors his indictments against Judah: houses built through unjust gain, violence, idol worship, exploitation of neighbors, and empire built on oppression. They warn against "pointing out the sins of enemies while ignoring our own" and against "empire-building (whether national, corporate, or personal) through exploitation."[^10] When you complain about someone else's wrongdoing, how often does the same pattern show up in you, and what would it look like to let the mirror do its work this week?

</div>

### Though the Fig Tree Does Not Bud <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#though-the-fig-tree-does-not-bud}

*Habakkuk gets his answer, trembles, and chooses joy anyway.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Habakkuk 3:16–19 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes point out that Habakkuk's reaction to God's answer is fear, with his heart pounding, his lips quivering and his legs trembling. Then comes a list of total loss: no figs, grapes, olives, food, sheep or cattle, which in an agricultural society means complete economic collapse.[^11] What do you notice about the order of these verses, from trembling to loss to rejoicing, and what is Habakkuk's joy actually anchored in?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode reflects on why God sometimes reveals the future and sometimes does not: Job receives no explanation, Habakkuk receives a glimpse, and "sometimes knowing the future would prevent us from walking into it."[^12] When has not knowing what was coming turned out to be a mercy for you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes stress that this is "not prosperity gospel" but faith in the face of guaranteed calamity, and that God gives "strength for the journey, not escape from the journey," feet like a deer for impossible terrain. They add that the book was meant to be sung, because communities need shared expression of lament and hope.[^13] If you wrote your own version of verses 17–18 for your life right now, what would go in the "though" lines, and what would go in the "yet"?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Habakkuk asks the question every generation asks: why do the wicked prosper? He does not settle for the first answer. He climbs his watchtower, waits, and hears God speak woe over every empire built on blood and unjust gain, including, as he comes to see, his own people's. The answer terrifies him, yet he ends by rejoicing in God with every field empty. The righteous live by faithfulness, and they sing it together.[^14]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, share your own "though... yet" line for this week.

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 59 — Obadiah — Petra](/guides/59)** (31:27): Obadiah, the shortest prophetic book in the Hebrew Bible, is addressed to Edom, Judah's brother nation descended from Esau. The episode explores Edom's pride in its rock fortresses (Petra is the most famous), its escalating betrayal of Judah during the Babylonian invasion, and the claim that God holds every nation accountable for how it treats people.[^15]

**What to bring next week:**
- Read all of Obadiah (only 21 verses). Circle every verb describing what Edom did, or failed to do, on the "day" of Judah's trouble.
- Notice one place in your week where you were tempted to stand aloof from someone else's trouble.
