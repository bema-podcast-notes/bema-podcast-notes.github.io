### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** In 2 Corinthians, Paul defended his apostleship by boasting in weakness and suffering rather than credentials, insisting that his authority was "for building you up and not for tearing you down."[^1]

**This week:** Ephesians is a letter about who belongs in God's family. Paul's pronouns, his "new humanity," and even his instructions about marriage all serve the welcome of outsiders into the household.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode argues that Paul uses pronouns on purpose in Ephesians 1–2: "we/us" for the Jewish believers who first hoped in Christ, then "you" for the Gentiles who were included later. Read that way, the letter's central theme is family membership, the inclusion of Gentiles as full members of God's household rather than guests or second-class citizens.[^2]

The episode also sets the letter in Ephesus, a city devoted to the goddess Artemis, whose annual festival involved a ritual washing of her statue followed by a procession of sexual debauchery. The notes read Paul's instructions about marriage in Ephesians 5 as a specific, counter-cultural response to that setting.[^3] Since the traditions in our room read Ephesians 5 in different ways, we will treat that part of the conversation as a chance to understand one another, not to settle the question.

### Opening <span class="guide-time">10 min</span> {#opening}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-opening" markdown="1">

**Opening Question:** Think of a family, team or community you joined after its story was already well underway. What helped you move from feeling like a guest to feeling like you belonged, or what kept you on the outside?

</div>

### "We" and "You": Following the Pronouns <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#we-and-you-following-the-pronouns}

*Paul tells the story of God's people with pronouns that mark who was there first and who has been brought in.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Ephesians 1:3–14 aloud. As you listen, pay attention to every "we," "us" and "you."*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Go back through the passage and track the pronouns. Where does Paul use "we" and "us," and where does he switch to "you"? The notes point out that verses 3–12 describe those who "were the first to put our hope in Christ," and that verse 13 shifts deliberately: "And you also were included in Christ when you heard the message of truth."[^4] What changes in the passage at that switch, and who do you think the "we" and the "you" are?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes argue that the famous passages about predestination and grace in Ephesians "must be understood in their context of Jewish-Gentile relations, not abstract theological systems."[^5] Our traditions have different histories with words like "chosen" and "predestined." What does reading the "we" as Jewish believers and the "you" as Gentiles bring into focus for you, and what questions does it leave open?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes call this "the scandalous welcome of outsiders into God's covenant family."[^6] When have you been the "you" in someone else's story, included in something that began long before you arrived? What did it feel like to be told you were part of it?

</div>

### From Foreigners to Fellow Citizens <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#from-foreigners-to-fellow-citizens}

*Paul describes what the Gentiles once lacked and what they have now become.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Ephesians 2:11–22 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Make two lists from the passage. In verse 12, what were the Gentiles once without? In verse 19, what are they now? The notes quote both verses: Gentiles were "excluded from citizenship in Israel, and foreigners to the covenants of promise," and are now "fellow citizens with God's people and also members of his household."[^7] Which word on the second list stands out to you, and why?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes stress that Gentiles "are not merely added to Judaism" but become part of something new, "a family that transcends ethnic, cultural, and religious boundaries while honoring the Jewish foundation of God's covenant story."[^8] Paul calls it "one new humanity out of the two" (2:15).[^9] How can a family be genuinely new while still honoring the Jewish foundation it was built on? What would be lost if a church forgot either half of that sentence?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> *Read Ephesians 3:2–6 aloud.* Paul says this "mystery" was made known to him by revelation, and the notes connect it to his claim in Galatians 1:11–12 that his gospel came from Jesus and not from human teaching.[^10] Why do you think Paul treats the inclusion of the Gentiles as a revelation rather than something obvious?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes apply this passage by asking "what walls exist in their communities that need to be torn down," and add that the principle reaches "any divisions that prevent God's family from displaying the reconciling power of the Gospel."[^11] What wall do you see in your own church or community right now, and what is one small thing you could do to make someone on the other side of it a member of the household instead of a guest?

</div>

### A Different Story in the City of Artemis <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#a-different-story-in-the-city-of-artemis}

*The notes read Paul's household instructions against the religious life of Ephesus.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes describe the annual Artemisian festival: the statue of Artemis was paraded from her temple to the harbor and "ritually washed to 'restore her virginity,'" and the return procession "was characterized by rampant sexual debauchery."[^12] *Read Ephesians 5:21–33 aloud.* What language of washing, water and cleansing do you notice in the passage? Who is given the most instruction, and what are they told to do?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes argue that "submit to your own husbands" "isn't a universal gender hierarchy but a specific counter-cultural statement" in a city where Artemis worship involved women asserting sexual independence from their husbands, and that the command to husbands is "equally radical."[^13] The traditions in this room have read these verses differently for a long time. What has your tradition emphasized in this passage, what does it see as being at stake, and which part of the notes' reading do you find most helpful or most difficult?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes suggest that modern readers ask, "How do Christian marriages bear witness to the Gospel in our specific cultural context?"[^14] Widen that to every kind of household and friendship in the room. In your setting, what story does our culture tell about love, bodies and belonging, and what is one way your own relationships could tell a different one this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Ephesians is not mainly a letter about abstract systems. It is a letter about a family. Paul tells the story with "we" for the people who first hoped in Christ and "you" for the outsiders who were brought in, and then he tells those outsiders that they are no longer foreigners but fellow citizens and members of the household. That welcome reached into the most ordinary places, even marriages in a city shaped by Artemis. The question Paul leaves us is where our walls still stand and who is still waiting at the door.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: who is one person you want to treat as family rather than as a guest this week?

</div>
