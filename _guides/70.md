### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Malachi confronted a disappointed, apathetic remnant through a pattern of accusation and defensive questions, and asked whether we imagine new tomorrows or lie to ourselves about a better past. A faithful few answered by writing a scroll of remembrance.[^1]

**This week:** We turn from the returnees in Jerusalem to the much larger community that stayed in Persia. Esther gives them a story about living faithfully, wisely and courageously inside empire.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The notes point out that Esther is the only biblical book never to mention God's name explicitly and the only one not found among the Dead Sea Scrolls. It was written for the Jews who remained in Persia; the notes estimate that around 250,000 of 5–6 million Jews returned.[^3] Purim, the festival that comes from this book, is not one of the festivals commanded at Sinai, and its costumes reflect the theme of a God who is present throughout the story but "in disguise."[^4]

The episode draws heavily on Rabbi David Fohrman's *The Queen You Thought You Knew*. It traces deliberate parallels between Esther and Joseph, presents two traditional readings of how Esther became queen, follows her careful strategy before the king, and finds the deeper meaning of Purim in Esther's decision to "cast her lot" before God.[^5] Its central ethical teaching is that to remain silent in the face of injustice is to condone it.[^6]

### A Story Without God's Name <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#a-story-without-gods-name}

*God is never named in Esther, and the episode asks us to look for God anyway.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Esther 6:1–11 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes list a run of "coincidences" in the story, several of them in this chapter: the king cannot sleep, the reader happens to read the record of Mordecai's loyalty, Haman happens to arrive at that moment, and the king's question happens to fit Haman's narcissism perfectly.[^7] Read the chapter again slowly and count the moments where timing matters. What do you notice, and what effect does it have that the narrator never explains them?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes say the book's audience was the community that stayed in Persia, people living as a minority who had to maintain their identity while embedded in a foreign empire.[^8] They ask whether God's absence from the text might actually heighten awareness of God's presence through "coincidence" and providence.[^9] Why might a story with no mention of God be exactly the story that diaspora community needed?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Purim is celebrated with costumes, which the notes connect to the theme of God "in disguise," never mentioned yet unmistakably active.[^10] Where in your life right now does God seem silent or hidden, and what would it look like to watch for God in disguise there?

</div>

### Joseph's Path and a Shrewd Queen <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#josephs-path-and-a-shrewd-queen}

*Esther walks a path the Joseph story had already marked out, and she walks it with careful wisdom.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Esther 2:8–9 and 2:15–18 aloud, then Genesis 39:20–23 and Genesis 41:41–43.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes list parallels between Esther and Joseph: both enter a place of confinement, both "win favor" with the one in charge (the notes say the Hebrew phrasing is identical), both receive authority within that space, both are brought before the king, and both receive royal garments and symbols of authority.[^11] As you compare the passages, what parallels can you find for yourselves, and what does it suggest that Esther's story is told in Joseph's shape?

*Read Esther 5:1–8 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes describe Esther's strategy: she invites the king to a banquet, includes Haman, delays her request to a second banquet, frames herself as the one needing rescue, and keeps attention on Haman rather than on her own identity. The king cannot simply reverse his edict without losing face, so she creates conditions where he can save her people and keep his authority.[^12] The notes connect this to Jesus' teaching to be "innocent as doves but shrewd as serpents" (Matthew 10:16).[^13] Where do you see the difference between shrewd wisdom and manipulation in what Esther does?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The episode presents two traditional readings of how Esther became queen. In one, the contest was explicitly sexual, a year of training in "the art of lovemaking," and Esther, whose name the notes connect to Ishtar, won through it but was then providentially positioned to save her people. In the other, the dominant Jewish tradition, she won the king through her intellect, wisdom and knowledge of Torah.[^14] The notes say that either way, God can position us to make a difference "regardless of how we've arrived at our current positions," whether through wise choices or through "sketchy means."[^15] What is at stake in choosing between these readings, and what does the "either way" conclusion offer people with complicated pasts?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes suggest Esther models engaging power by understanding how it actually works, being patient about timing, creating conditions for change, and helping those in power save face while doing the right thing.[^16] Think of a system you live inside, such as a workplace, school, family or town. What would shrewd, faithful engagement look like there this month?

</div>

### If I Perish, I Perish <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#if-i-perish-i-perish}

*Mordecai presses Esther to act, and her answer gives the festival its deeper meaning.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Esther 4:10–17 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Listen to Esther's first reply, Mordecai's answer and Esther's final decision. The notes paraphrase Mordecai's argument: "You can act or remain silent, but understand that silence equals complicity."[^17] What do you notice about how Mordecai makes his case, and what changes in Esther between verse 11 and verse 16?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The surface meaning of "Purim" (lots) points to the lots Haman cast to choose a date for destroying the Jews (Esther 3:7). The notes say the deeper meaning is Esther's decision, "I will go to the king, though it is against the law, and if I perish, I perish": she "casts her lot" before God, and the festival celebrates that faithful risk, not Haman's plot.[^18] How does it change the story to hear Purim as a celebration of Esther's lot rather than Haman's?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes say the principle that silence equals complicity became foundational for Jewish ethics, and that it took on haunting resonance after the Holocaust with the question, "Where were the righteous who should have spoken?"[^19] They ask whether we will exercise courage and wisdom when the moment arrives, or remain "silent and safe."[^20] Where might you be positioned "for such a time as this," and what would it cost you to speak?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Esther was written for God's people who stayed in Persia, and it never names God, yet the story is full of timing that only makes sense as providence. Esther walks Joseph's path into the halls of power and uses careful, shrewd wisdom to save her people without asking the king to lose face. Mordecai tells her that silence would make her complicit, and she answers, "If I perish, I perish," casting her lot before God. Purim celebrates that courage, and the notes say such wisdom can cause empire to "crumble in light of goodness."[^21]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, name one place where you sense you may be positioned "for such a time as this."

</div>
