### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Elijah won the argument on Mount Carmel but changed no hearts. At Horeb, God met him in a still small voice and sent him to make a disciple, Elisha.

**This week:** Elisha meets Naaman, a foreign commander with leprosy, and the story ends with two surprising words. If Elijah taught us that God changes hearts through patient love, Naaman shows us what that patience looks like.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Naaman is an *ish gadol*, a "great man" whose presence fills a room, and he has leprosy. An Israelite servant girl points him toward the prophet in Samaria. The king of Israel panics; Elisha does not even come to the door, and tells Naaman to wash seven times in the unimpressive Jordan. Naaman is furious until his own servants talk sense into him, and he is healed.

Then the story gets messy. Naaman confesses that there is no God in all the world except in Israel, asks for two mule-loads of Israel's dirt to take home, and admits that he will still have to bow in the temple of Rimmon when his master leans on his arm. Elisha simply says, "Go in peace." The episode, which Marty credits as originally inspired by a Rob Bell sermon, treats this as permission: life is complicated, faith is a journey, and God is more patient than our religious expectations.

### A Great Man in a Muddy River <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#a-great-man-in-a-muddy-river}

*The most important man in the story has to learn from the least important people in it.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read 2 Kings 5:1–14 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** As you listened, which characters had the least status, and which ones offered the most wisdom? The notes point to the "young Israelite servant girl" who suggests the prophet, and to Naaman's servants who "reason with him."[^1] Among the questions they raise is "the value of marginalized voices."[^2] What do you notice about how the story moves forward, and through whom?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** Naaman is an *ish gadol*. *Ish* means man, and *gadol* carries "weight, significance, importance," connected to *kavod* (glory, weightiness).[^3] He arrives with horses and chariots, and Elisha "doesn't even come to the door." Naaman says, "I thought he would surely come out to me and stand and call on the name of the LORD his God, wave his hand over the spot and cure me." The notes add that the Jordan was "an unimpressive, dirty creek" compared to the rivers of Damascus.[^4] What exactly offended Naaman? What did he expect a healing to look like?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Naaman brings "10 talents of silver, 6,000 shekels of gold, 10 sets of clothing," and Elisha tells him to wash seven times. The notes read this as "6 (human effort/wealth) versus 7 (divine completion)," and describe "Naaman's journey from six to seven, from wealth to simple obedience" as "the journey from Empire to Shalom."[^5] Where else in our study have we met this contrast between six and seven? What does it add to this story?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Two people respond out of fear or pride. The king of Israel tears his robes ("Am I God?"), and the notes say "His insecurity makes him see threat instead of opportunity." Naaman storms off until his servants ask, "If the prophet had told you to do some great thing, would you not have done it?"[^6] The notes conclude: "Sometimes the pathway is simple obedience to small, humble instructions."[^7] Where in your life right now are you waiting for some great thing, when a small, humble step is already in front of you?

</div>

### Dirt, Rimmon, and Go in Peace <span class="guide-time">35 min</span> {#dirt-rimmon-and-go-in-peace}

*Naaman's new faith is real and incomplete at the same time, and Elisha lets him go.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read 2 Kings 5:15–19 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Listen for Naaman's three statements: what he confesses, what he asks for, and what he asks forgiveness for. The notes call his confession "a MASSIVE theological leap" in a world where gods were understood geographically, and they note that his request for dirt "reveals he still operates partially within the geographical god framework."[^8] What do you notice about how quickly Naaman moves from a breakthrough to a misunderstanding to a confession of compromise?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes list what we might expect Elisha to say: "Turn or burn," "Correct his theology before he leaves," "Give him a systematic theology course." Instead Elisha says, "Go in peace" (*Lech l'shalom*), which the notes render as "You've got a long walk home. You'll figure it out." Naaman is "not returning to a thriving Jewish community," with no synagogues or seminary, "Just a servant girl from Israel and a very complicated situation."[^9] Why do you think Elisha offered no correction? What does his answer assume about God?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes themselves raise a hard question: "How do we distinguish between dangerous syncretism (mixing false worship with true) and the messy process of growing in understanding?"[^10] They are also clear that this approach "doesn't mean abandoning truth or never speaking correction."[^11] Our traditions have drawn the line between faithfulness and compromise in different places, and each has reasons. Without settling it for everyone, how do you tell the difference between someone compromising and someone still on the journey?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes speak in two directions. To those who are stuck: "You've met God and you're doing your best - go in peace." To those helping others: "Sometimes people need permission more than instruction."[^12] Which of these do you most need to hear right now? Is there someone you love who needs to hear "go in peace" from you, and what makes that hard to say?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Naaman came as a great man expecting a great show, and he was healed by washing in a muddy creek on the advice of servants. His new faith was real: he confessed there is no God in all the world except in Israel. It was also incomplete: he wanted a load of dirt to take home, and he would still have to bow in the temple of Rimmon. Elisha did not panic, correct, or set conditions. He said, "Go in peace," trusting that the God who began the work would continue it on the long walk home. That is permission for us, and permission we can give others.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, where in your life do you need to hear "go in peace" this week?

</div>
