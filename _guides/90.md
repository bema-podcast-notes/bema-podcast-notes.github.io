**Last week:** We heard the birth of Jesus as a story of scandal and family rejection, met Magi who read Balaam's prophecy in the stars, and saw that from the first chapter God works outside the boundaries we draw.

**This week:** The episode finishes the birth narratives by placing the baby beside the two most powerful men of his world, Herod the Great and Caesar Augustus.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This episode argues that the Christmas story is deliberately political. Matthew frames Jesus's birth against Herod the Great, possibly the wealthiest person who ever lived, whose palaces and paranoia defined empire in the region.[^1] Jesus is born in Bethlehem, in the shadow of Herod's palace-fortress, the Herodium, and the news goes first to shepherds.[^2]

Luke frames the same birth against Caesar Augustus, whose propaganda called him "Son of God," "Prince of Peace," "King of Kings" and "Lord of Lords."[^3] Luke quietly hands those titles to the baby in the manger. The episode returns to BEMA's long-running theme of Empire versus Shalom and asks a hard question about our own Christmas: which kingdom do our celebrations actually belong to?[^4]

### Herod's Kingdom <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#herods-kingdom}

*Herod built mountains, and God answered with a baby born in poverty nearby.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 2:13–18 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes describe Herod's scale: Temple Mount stones so heavy that one weighs 260 metric tons, an artificial mountain built just so he could put a palace on it, and engineers forbidden to record their methods because he wanted "history to remember only his greatness."[^5] Yet he was so afraid of being overthrown that he built an escape route of three palaces.[^6] As you look at Herod's actions in this passage, what do you notice about what he fears and how he uses his power?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes say Jesus was born "in the shadow of the Herodium," among sheep dung and shepherds' fires, and call it "God's answer to Hellenistic greatness: not palace versus palace, but a complete inversion of what power and kingship mean."[^7] They list the questions this inversion raises: "What is real power?" "What is real wealth?" "Where does security come from?" and "Who is God, and what is God trying to save us from?"[^8] Which of those four questions does this contrast press on hardest for you, and why?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The original notes ask, "What does the context of a reference to scripture, like weeping Rachel, have on the meaning of the text we read?"[^9] Read Jeremiah 31:15 aloud, then glance at the verses around it if you have time. What does Matthew's quotation bring with it from Jeremiah's setting, and how does it shape the way you read the grief in Bethlehem?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes compare Herod's obsession with being remembered as "great" to "The contemporary obsession with personal branding, follower counts, and projecting an image of success."[^10] Where are you tempted to build a small Herodium of your own, something meant to say "look at me," and what is it protecting you from?

</div>

### Caesar's Titles <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#caesars-titles}

*Luke takes the emperor's titles and gives them to a baby in a feeding trough.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Luke 2:1–14 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes explain that when "Caesar's Comet" appeared, Octavian claimed it was Julius Caesar ascending to become a god, reasoning, "If Julius is now God, then I am the Son of God."[^11] Imperial propaganda then called Augustus "Son of the Most High," "Prince of Peace," "King of Kings" and "Lord of Lords," and an inscription from Priene proclaimed the "gospel" of Caesar Augustus.[^12] Listen to the passage again with that in mind. Which words would a first-century reader have recognized as Caesar's, and what would hearing them applied to Jesus have sounded like?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes warn about "The temptation to look for a 'better Caesar'" and stress that "This applies across the political spectrum; the issue isn't which party or candidate but the tendency to seek salvation through political power."[^13] Without naming parties or candidates, where do you notice that hope for a better Caesar in yourself, or in the churches you have belonged to?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The episode ends with a question the notes call convicting: "Would I even have noticed the King of the universe born in a stable, or would I simply have been looking for a better Caesar?"[^14] Be honest with yourself. What would you have been looking for, and what might you have walked right past?

</div>

### Out of Egypt <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#out-of-egypt}

*Matthew walks Jesus through Israel's story, and then the episode turns the question toward our own traditions.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 2:19–23 and Hosea 11:1 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes point to deliberate echoes: Joseph receiving guidance in dreams like Joseph son of Jacob, the flight to Egypt, and the return that fulfills "Out of Egypt I called my son."[^15] The original notes add parallels with Moses, who also lived in Egypt and was hunted by a king.[^16] What echoes of Israel's earlier story do you notice in these verses, and what does Matthew gain by presenting Jesus as a "new Israel"?[^17]

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes observe that modern American Christmas emphasizes "silver and gold," comfort and abundance, values that "align with Herod's kingdom, not Jesus's kingdom"; they quote the line "Our Christmas is tied up in the wrong kingdom."[^18] They also say "This isn't about being anti-celebration."[^19] What is your honest reaction to hearing that our Christmas traditions may not fit the Christmas story?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The original notes ask, "What are some small changes we can make over time to better align our own traditions with the story of Christ's Christmas that could impact our families and especially our children positively in the future?"[^20] Name one small, specific change you could try this year.

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Matthew and Luke both refuse to tell the birth of Jesus as a private, sentimental story. They set it beside Herod, who built mountains and killed children to protect his throne, and beside Caesar, who borrowed heaven's titles for himself. Against all of that grandeur, God sends a baby to a poor family, in the shadow of the Herodium, with shepherds as the first audience. Empire runs on fear, force and self-preservation; Shalom runs on invitation and self-giving love. The question the episode leaves us with is which of those two stories is shaping our lives, and our celebrations.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one place this week where you want to look for the King in the stable instead of a better Caesar?

</div>
