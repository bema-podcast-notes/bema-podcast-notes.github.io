### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** In our last guide (Ep. 252), John's prologue introduced the *Logos* (Greek, "word, reason"), a term John uses to speak to Greek and Jewish listeners at once, and the light and darkness motif that runs through the whole Gospel.[^1]

**This week:** Marty and Brent read John 2 in its Jewish setting: a wedding in a town that may have been a Zealot stronghold, and a temple cleansing that John places at the start of Jesus's ministry.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode reads the wedding at Cana as a story full of hints. The "third day" may point to Tuesday, the doubly blessed wedding day; Cana's name may come from the Hebrew word for zeal; and Jesus and Mary trade Genesis allusions about Jacob and Joseph before Mary wins the argument.[^3] The six stone jars may recall Elijah's water jars on Mount Carmel, but instead of the fury a Zealot audience might have wanted, Jesus brings an abundance of the best wine.[^4]

The second half looks at the temple cleansing: why John places it early, whether there may have been two cleansings, and why the Greek word *Ioudaios* is better heard as "Judeans" (the southern party tied to the temple authorities) than as "the Jews."[^5] The chapter ends with a puzzle about belief: many believed because of signs, yet Jesus would not entrust himself to them.[^6]

### A Wedding at the Foot of Carmel <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#a-wedding-at-the-foot-of-carmel}

*The episode treats the wedding at Cana as a conversation conducted in Scripture, set in a place charged with political hope.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read John 2:1–11 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes place the wedding on "the third day," which may mean Tuesday, the one day in Genesis 1 called good twice and so the traditional day for Jewish weddings. They also suggest Cana was Khirbet Qana at the base of Mount Carmel, with a name possibly drawn from *qanah* ("zeal").[^3] As you listened to John 2:1–11, which details did John slow down to give us (people, objects, numbers, words spoken)? Name one detail and say why you think John included it.

*Read Genesis 29:21 and Genesis 41:55 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The episode hears Jesus's "My hour has not yet come" as an echo of Jacob asking Laban for his wife, spoken "in the negative," as if Jesus were saying this is not the wedding he wants. Mary answers with "Do whatever he tells you," the words Pharaoh speaks about Joseph, whose story is about forgiveness and reconciliation with his brothers. The notes call Mary "a woman of the Text" and say she "ultimately wins the argument."[^3] What does this exchange suggest about the relationship between Jesus and his mother, and about how faithful people can challenge one another with Scripture?

*Read 1 Kings 18:30–39 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> In the episode's reading, Elijah had the people fill four jars three times, and for a Zealot audience living at the base of Mount Carmel the six stone jars at Cana would bring that scene to mind. Such an audience would have wanted "The wine of God's fury against their enemies." Instead Jesus brings 120 to 180 gallons of the best wine, saved for last.[^4] What do you make of the contrast between the wine they hoped for and the wine they received?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes suggest that Jesus's reluctance to embrace this crowd speaks to our own reluctance to embrace those we would prefer to avoid, such as "Political groups we disagree with," and they warn against expecting God to vindicate our side against theirs.[^7] Without naming anyone, where are you most tempted to want fury for the other side rather than abundance? What might it look like to bring "the best wine" into that place instead?

</div>

### Housekeeping in the Temple <span class="guide-time">35 min</span> {#housekeeping-in-the-temple}

*John's temple cleansing raises questions about order, authority, and what kind of belief Jesus trusts.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read John 2:12–25 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** John places the temple cleansing at the beginning of Jesus's ministry, while the other Gospels place it near the end. The episode offers three theories: John arranged it to follow a Genesis pattern, there were two separate cleansings, or this first cleansing was less violent, with the "whip with cords" possibly an idiom for winding together one's *tzitziot* (the tassels commanded in Numbers 15) and shaking them as a sign of disobedience.[^5] Looking closely at John's account, what do you notice about what Jesus does, what he says, and what the disciples remember afterward?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> One theory links two cleansings to the Leviticus laws about a moldy house: clean it, leave it, return later, and tear it down if the mold has come back. Read this way, Jesus cleared the corruption, gave time to repent, and the house fell in 70 CE. The notes add that "God gives space for repentance."[^8] The notes also trace a Jacob thread: Nathanael under the fig tree and Jacob's ladder at the end of John 1, then a wedding (as Jacob meets Rachel), then a temple (as Jacob names the place Bethel, "House of God").[^9] Which of these patterns helps you most in reading the chapter, and why?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes say translating *Ioudaios* (Greek, usually rendered "Jews") as "the Jews" in John "promotes antisemitic readings of the text" and hides the fact that Jesus, his disciples, and the early believers were all Jewish. "Judeans" refers instead to the southern party aligned with Jerusalem, the Sadducees and Herodian interests, so the demand for a sign comes from temple authorities questioning "this Galilean upstart."[^5] How has the phrase "the Jews" in John sounded to you over the years, and how might the way Christians (including our own traditions) have heard it shaped attitudes toward Jewish people? What changes when you hear "Judeans" instead?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** John closes the chapter by saying many believed when they saw his signs, but Jesus would not entrust himself to them, because he knew what was in each person. The notes ask "What kind of belief is genuine?"[^6] What do you think separates belief that Jesus can trust from belief that rests on being impressed, and what would it take for your own faith to move further in that direction this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> John 2 opens with a sign of abundance in a place that may have longed for fury. Jesus and Mary argue with Scripture, and Mary sends him toward brothers he might rather avoid. In the temple, Jesus confronts what has gone wrong, and the episode suggests he may be giving room to repent before judgment comes. Hearing "Judeans" instead of "the Jews" keeps us from turning a family argument into a weapon. And the chapter ends by asking what kind of belief Jesus can trust.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, where would you most like to see abundance rather than fury this week?

</div>
