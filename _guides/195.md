### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Constantine ended persecution, Christendom began, and four councils defined who Christ is, while Marty asked whether orthodoxy had become more about affirming beliefs than living a particular way.[^1]

**This week:** The church takes up the sword it once suffered under, and at the same time the monks quietly rebuild a way of life with God.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This short episode holds both darkness and light. As Rome falls, the empire of the Caesars gives way to an "imperial religious state" in which bishops act as civic leaders and doctrine can be enforced by force; under leaders like Justinian, people who refused the creeds faced sanctions or worse.[^3] The notes describe Christianity moving from the "pointy end of the sword" to "the handle end of the sword," and they record anti-Jewish edicts (which the notes attribute to Augustine) forbidding business and relationships with Jews and prescribing ham on Easter as an anti-Jewish statement.[^4]

The light comes from the monastic movements. Having refined orthodoxy (right belief) but set aside the Torah's practical framework, Gentile Christianity lacked orthopraxy (right practice), and the monks filled that gap with prayer, discipline, preserved texts, hospitals and community.[^5] Gregory the Great's liturgy brought both beauty and new channels for corruption.[^6] Through it all, the notes hold on to a hopeful principle: "if we create space, God will fill it," and there is always a remnant seeking God wholeheartedly.[^7]

### From the Pointy End to the Handle End <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#from-the-pointy-end-to-the-handle-end}

*Once the church held power, it began doing to others what had once been done to it.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes contrast the "Roman empire of the Caesars," an imperial political state, with an "imperial religious state," where popes replaced emperors as primary authorities, bishops became civic leaders, and "Christian doctrine became enforceable by imperial sword."[^3] Looking at those changes, which one do you think most altered what it meant to be a Christian?

*Read Genesis 18:1–8 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes say the anti-Jewish rules of this era cost the church "not just practice but basic human compassion and the Abrahamic call to hospitality."[^4] As you listen to Abraham at the tent, what do you notice about how he treats strangers he does not know? Report one detail. Then set it beside the edicts the notes record: forbidding business with Jews, forbidding Sabbath candles, prescribing ham on Easter, and forbidding relationships with Jews.

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes observe that a tradition many Christians practice innocently, ham on Easter, began as an anti-Jewish gesture, and they encourage us to "examine the origins of our traditions to understand what we're really affirming."[^8] What other customs might be worth examining this way, and how could we do it with honesty but without contempt for those who kept them?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes ask modern Christians whether they are "wielding influence like those holding the sword, or serving like those who suffered under it."[^9] Think about the places where you hold some power: at work, at home, in church, in your community. What would it look like this week to use that power the way someone who has been on the pointy end would?

</div>

### Orthodoxy, Orthopraxy, and the Monks <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#orthodoxy-orthopraxy-and-the-monks}

*The monks answered a question the councils had left open: how do we actually walk this faith out?*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes explain that by setting aside *miqsat ma'ase haTorah*, "the works of Torah" (kosher eating, tassels, the commandments), Gentile Christians "were missing the playbook on what it meant to walk the path of faith."[^5] The monks filled that gap. Of their contributions in the notes (preserving texts, hospitals and clinics, teaching on community, spiritual disciplines, liturgy), which surprises you most, and why?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Monastics are often criticized as "secluded and isolated," much like the Essenes, yet thinkers like Gregory of Nyssa and Basil the Great taught about community to counter isolation.[^10] What is the difference between withdrawing from the world and making space within it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Gregory the Great, called the "father of medieval spirituality," introduced large-scale liturgy and Gregorian chant. The notes call this a paradox: the same structure that gave beauty and order also "systematized corruption and power struggles," because institutional structures "amplify whatever spirit animates them, good or bad."[^6] Our traditions range from highly liturgical to very informal. Thinking diagnostically about your own tradition, what has its way of ordering worship amplified well, and where might the same structure amplify something unhealthy?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes ask, "Do we spend as much energy on how we live as on what we believe?"[^11] Picture your last month honestly. Where did your energy go, and what one practice would bring the two into better balance?

</div>

### Creating Space: The Remnant <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#creating-space-the-remnant}

*Even in a dark era, God kept finding people who made room for him.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 22:36–40 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes describe the remnant as those trying to follow the Creator "with all their heart, and all their soul, and all their mind," and say such a remnant exists "be they Jews, be they Christians, or pagans."[^7] As you listen to Jesus's answer, what do you notice about the two commandments and how they hang together? What does wholehearted love look like when the institutions around you are failing?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The key takeaways claim that "divine partnership doesn't require perfect theology or practice."[^2] Where do you find that encouraging, and where do you find it challenging?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q11:** "If we create space, God will fill it."[^7] What is one specific space (a time, a place, a habit) you will create for God this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> This week we saw the church take the handle end of the sword, forcing conversions and turning against its Jewish neighbors, losing the hospitality that Abraham once showed to strangers. In the same centuries, monks preserved texts, cared for the sick, and built a playbook of practices for walking with God, filling the gap left when orthodoxy outran orthopraxy. Their structures brought beauty and also new dangers. Through it all there was a remnant, people who loved God with heart, soul and mind and made space for God to fill.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what space will you make for God before we meet again?

</div>
