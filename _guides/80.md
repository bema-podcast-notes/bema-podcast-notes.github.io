### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We met the Zealots, whose fierce love for God's honor led them into the myth of redemptive violence, and we heard that God still has a place for the zealous.

**This week:** The last of the five groups is the Pharisees, who took the same zeal and poured it into devotion and obedience. Jesus spent most of his ministry among them.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The notes describe the Pharisees as descendants of the *Hasidim* ("pious ones") who rejected Hellenism without fleeing to the desert. They settled in the Galilean "triangle" of Chorazin, Capernaum and Bethsaida, built towns around the synagogue, lived communally, and channeled their zeal into "absolute devotion and obedience" to God's commands.[^1] The notes call them the Orthodox Judaism of Jesus's day and point out that after the Temple's destruction, Pharisaic Judaism became the foundation of Rabbinic Judaism.[^2] Their answer to Rome was simple: let God deal with Rome; our task is to become the people God asks us to be.[^3]

The episode argues that Jesus focused on the Pharisees because they had nearly everything God's people needed (devotion to the Text, passion for God, commitment to obedience, knowledge of Scripture) and lacked one thing: compassion.[^4] The notes take care to say that their harshness toward sinners grew from sincere theological conviction, not simple meanness.[^5] Most pointedly, the episode turns the lens on us: it is easy to read the Pharisees as "them," and the episode's discussion notes ask whether they are actually the group we are meant to see ourselves in.[^6]

**Opening Question:** Think of a community you have belonged to that took something very seriously: a team, a choir, a military unit, a church, a family tradition. What did that seriousness make possible, and what did it sometimes cost?

### Five Answers to Rome <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#five-answers-to-rome}

*Over five weeks we have met five answers to one question: what do you do about Rome?*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes review the five groups with a one-line answer each:[^7]

- **Sadducees:** "We partner with Rome"
- **Herodians:** "We get in bed with Rome"
- **Essenes:** "Rome? What's Rome?"
- **Zealots:** "We kill Rome"
- **Pharisees:** "You're asking the wrong question. Let God deal with Rome"

Read the five lines aloud. When you face a culture or a power you disagree with, which of these five instincts shows up first in you? Which one shows up in your church?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The Pharisees believed that "when they became the people God asked them to be, God would rescue them."[^8] The notes describe towns with the synagogue at the center and the *bema* at the center of the synagogue, practical buildings of rough-cut stone, and a "110% commitment to Scripture."[^9] What is admirable in this answer? What risks do you see in tying God's rescue to the people's obedience?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The Pharisees lived in *insulae*, multifamily dwellings around shared courtyards, which the notes contrast with the modern "your own house, your own car, your own washer and dryer" mentality.[^10] They believed they could be more obedient as a committed community. What would you gain, and what would you find hardest, about sharing daily life that closely? What is one small way you could live a little more "in common" with others?

</div>

### Everything but Compassion <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#everything-but-compassion}

*The episode explains both why Jesus spent so much time with the Pharisees and what he challenged in them.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes explain the Pharisees' view of sinners: if Israel would just be obedient enough, God would deliver them, so tax collectors and prostitutes were seen as the reason Rome stayed in power: "You're the reason God hasn't delivered us yet."[^11] The notes stress that "This wasn't mere judgmentalism but a genuine worldview" and that the Pharisees "didn't wake up wanting to be jerks."[^12] Why do you think the episode works so hard to understand the Pharisees' reasons? How does understanding the reasons change the way you read their conflicts with Jesus?

*Read Matthew 9:9–13 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Read the passage again. Who is at the table with Jesus? What question do the Pharisees ask, and to whom do they ask it? What does Jesus say in response, and which prophet does he point them to? In light of the Pharisees' theology of sin and deliverance, why would this meal have been so troubling to sincere, devoted people?

*Read Matthew 11:20–24 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Read the passage again and notice what Jesus says these towns failed to do. The notes observe that "The passage doesn't say they didn't believe the message"; the problem was that "the message didn't change the way they lived."[^13] They describe Tyre and Sidon as prophetic symbols of arrogance and Sodom as a city known for lack of hospitality and failure to protect the foreigner.[^14] What do these comparisons suggest about the specific failure Jesus is naming? Why might it be harder for devoted, knowledgeable people to repent than for others?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes call Capernaum "the 'Yale or Harvard' of the Pharisaic world" and say that when Jesus made it his base, he was intentionally engaging "the most educated and devoted segment of Judaism."[^15] What does it say about Jesus that he set up his ministry among the most serious students of Scripture rather than among the compromised or the withdrawn?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes say the Pharisees had "every piece God's people needed except one," and that without compassion, "everything else was ruined."[^16] They name a modern parallel: churches may emphasize "doctrinal correctness, biblical literacy, and moral standards" while failing in compassion toward "the marginalized, the sinner, the outcast."[^17] Where do you have knowledge, conviction or discipline but find compassion harder? Who is one person or group you find it difficult to feel compassion for?

</div>

### Cultural Herodians, Religious Pharisees <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#cultural-herodians-religious-pharisees}

*The episode closes by holding up a mirror.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The original notes put Marty's challenge bluntly: the church struggles most because "we are cultural herodians and religious pharisees." In other words, "We want to consume everything and live in all of the luxury," and "We also lack all compassion when it comes to our religion."[^18] The notes add that many of us have neither the Pharisees' devotion nor their compassion, which makes this "The Worst Cocktail."[^19] What is your honest reaction to that statement? Where does it ring true, and where would you push back?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode's discussion notes point to an addendum about "how we talk about pharisees and what that means historically" and ask whether we read the Pharisees as just "another group of people" or as the group "we are supposed to associate with."[^20] In the church where you first learned the Gospels, how were the Pharisees usually portrayed? How might that portrayal have shaped the way you think about Judaism, and the way you think about yourself?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q11:** The original notes capture the confession this way: "I have everything else but I lack compassion for those that God cares about most."[^21] The discussion notes ask what happens when we "start acting like God's gatekeepers."[^22] Where in your life are you most tempted to act as a gatekeeper? What is one specific act of compassion you could practice this week toward someone on the other side of that gate?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The Pharisees were not the villains we were often taught to imagine. They were the most devoted people in Israel: communities built around the Text, sharing life, pouring all their zeal into obedience and waiting for God to deal with Rome. Jesus spent his ministry among them precisely because they had nearly every piece God's people needed. What they lacked was compassion, and without it, everything else was ruined. The episode asks us to stop reading the Pharisees as someone else and to see ourselves in them. The question is not whether we know the message, but whether it is changing the way we live.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: which of the five groups have you seen most in yourself over these weeks, and what is one thing you want to do about it?

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 81 — Silent Years — Association](/guides/81)** (36:08): The final episode on the silent years asks why Galatians 4:4 says Jesus came at "the fullness of time," and suggests it was the moment when all five Jewish responses to Hellenism were in full conversation. Marty and Brent walk back through the Sadducees, Herodians, Essenes, Zealots and Pharisees, naming a positive contribution and a negative tendency in each, and invite listeners to recognize how they embody all five in different parts of their lives.

**What to bring next week:** Read Galatians 4:4–7. Make a simple five-row chart with one strength and one weakness for each group, and put a mark beside the strength you most want to grow in and the weakness you most see in yourself.
