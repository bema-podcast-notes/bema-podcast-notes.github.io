### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Elle Grover Fricks gave us six Hebrew words for the ways we fail to walk God's path, showed how archaeology illuminates Israel's entry into the land, and reminded us that "Philistine" means those who rolled in.[^1]

**This week:** Marty and his wife Becky talk about parenting through Paul's instruction, "Fathers, do not exasperate your children."[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Marty and Becky begin with the Roman household, where instructions always flowed upward (slaves to masters, children to parents, wives to husbands), and show that Paul's household codes were countercultural because they gave fathers obligations too.[^3] The Greek words matter: the opposite of exasperating or provoking a child is to nourish and nurture them toward maturity.[^4] Marty names three goals: that his children don't hate themselves, don't hate Jesus, and see their parents love each other honestly and vulnerably.[^5]

The conversation is candid about how fear and insecurity drive our worst parenting, and about how religious households can smother children "with goodness" until they can't breathe.[^6] Becky describes jars of pennies, one for each week until a child turns 18, and the weekly question, "Did I invest in my children well this week?"[^7] Not everyone in our group is a parent, and some of us carry hard memories of being parented. These questions are written so that anyone can join in through their own experience of being raised, mentoring, teaching or simply caring for younger people.

### A Household Code That Runs Both Ways <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#a-household-code-that-runs-both-ways}

*Paul's instructions to families were unusual in his world because they put obligations on the powerful as well as the powerless.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Ephesians 6:1–4 aloud, then Colossians 3:20–21.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes explain that Roman household codes "always flowed from bottom to top" and that there "was never reciprocal instruction going the other direction."[^3] As you listen to these two passages, who is addressed, and what is asked of each? What stands out to you about the instructions given to fathers?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes give the Greek behind the key words: *parorgizo* (exasperate, "to arouse to wrath, to provoke"), *ektrepho* (bring up, "to nourish to maturity, to nurture"), *erethizo* (embitter, "to stir up") and *athumeō* (discouraged, "broken in spirit"). The picture is of nourishing a child "like watering a plant, cultivating soil."[^4] How does the image of a gardener change the way you think about raising, teaching or mentoring someone?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty and Becky describe Christian parenting as "partnership," where "you're still the parent" yet "we're in this together," and where "the parent disciplines the child while also disciplining themselves."[^8] What does a parent, teacher or mentor model when they let a young person see them being disciplined too?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The goal, the notes say, is "to call out of them who they truly are, to help them see their God-given identity."[^8] Who are the younger people in your life right now (children, grandchildren, nieces and nephews, students, people you mentor), and what is one thing you see in one of them that you could name out loud?

</div>

### Parenting From Fear or Toward Who You Want to Be <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#parenting-from-fear-or-toward-who-you-want-to-be}

*Marty and Becky argue that fear and insecurity, not lack of knowledge, cause our greatest mistakes with children.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Marty's three goals are that children don't hate themselves, don't hate Jesus, and see their parents love each other "honestly and vulnerably," in a relationship "full of mistakes that fall into a net of humility." When children hear the name Jesus, the notes say, "they should think of love, acceptance, rest, and belonging - not exasperation."[^5] Which of the three goals surprises you most, and why might Marty rank it so high?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes list what children learn is "normal" if they see it at home: saying "I'm sorry" without hating yourself, critical thinking, saying "I was wrong," overcoming guilt and shame, going to therapy, and emotional health. Marty notes his parents' generation will probably never go to therapy because of stigma.[^9] Which of those did you see modeled growing up, and which did you have to learn later?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Marty observes that in some conservative and evangelical subcultures, parents exasperate their kids "in the name of Christ," with right beliefs and good intentions, smothering children with goodness until "they can't breathe." He is candid that one of his own biggest regrets is how often he breaks his son's spirit.[^10] Every tradition has its own good intentions that can turn heavy. Without pointing at anyone else, where might the good intentions of your own church culture risk becoming exasperation for children or young people? What would an invitation look like instead?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes urge parents to "parent into who you want to be, not away from what you don't want to be."[^11] In any relationship where you have influence, what would it mean for you to move toward who you want to be rather than react against what you fear?

</div>

### Counting the Weeks <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#counting-the-weeks}

*Becky's penny jars turn time into something you can see and hold.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** Each Sunday Becky moves a penny from each child's jar to an "investments" jar, asking, "Did I invest in my children well this week?" The notes add that "average is actually a parenting win," that wins generally outweigh losses, and that "God's grace covers the multitude of mistakes and tongue-slips."[^7] What do you notice about the mix of urgency and grace in that practice?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty sees Bar or Bat Mitzvah at 13 ("son/daughter of the commandment") as the age when his children will choose their faith for themselves. The goal is to model Torah so well that "the table was set with good things" and they want to come to it; there will be no bacon double cheeseburgers at home, but what they order with friends after 13 is their choice.[^12] What does it look like to set a table rather than force a meal, in faith or anything else?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q11:** If you had a jar with a penny for each remaining week of some relationship you care about, whom would it be for, and how do you want to invest this coming week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> This week Marty and Becky showed us that Paul's words to families ran in both directions: children are called to honor, and fathers are called not to provoke but to nourish. The opposite of exasperating a child is watering a plant. Fear and insecurity cause our worst mistakes, while seeing a young person's full humanity brings clarity. We are invited to set a table full of good things, model saying sorry, and trust that God's grace covers the weeks we wish we could take back.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one way you want to nourish a younger person in your life this week?

</div>
