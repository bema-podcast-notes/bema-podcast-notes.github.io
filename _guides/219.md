### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Rebekah watered ten camels for a stranger, her family's table was ruled by greed, and she chose God's purposes with three words: "I will go."[^1]

**This week:** Marty's wife, Becky, joins him for her first appearance on the podcast. Their conversation moves from biblical texts into daily life: marriage, parenting and the family's journey into Torah-observant Jewish living.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Marty and Becky describe marriage through the Hebrew phrase *ezer k'negdo* from Genesis 2, "the help that opposes," picturing two planks that lean against each other and hold each other up.[^3] They talk about parenting that is "dynamic and individualized" and that takes seriously the warning of Ephesians 6:4 not to exasperate children, and about Becky learning to give herself grace during the demanding toddler years.[^4]

They also describe their family's Torah observance as Jewish believers, grounded in being *kadosh* ("set apart") as Leviticus 20 describes, and the moment their daughter said, "Dad, I don't want to be different."[^5] Marty is clear that this path relates to Jewish heritage and is "not a model for Gentile believers to adopt."[^5]

*A note for our group:* not everyone here is married or raising children, and our traditions hold different views on marriage roles. Listen for what applies to any close relationship, and speak from your own experience.

### The Help That Opposes <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-help-that-opposes}

*The episode reads Genesis 2 as a picture of partners who complete each other through their differences.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 2:18–24 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes explain that *ezer* means "help" or "support" and *k'negdo* means "against" or "opposite," creating "the beautiful paradox of 'the help that opposes.'"[^3] Marty uses "the image of two planks leaning against each other - they oppose each other while simultaneously holding each other up."[^3] What do you notice in the passage that supports or complicates that picture?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The rabbis identify four *teshuqahs* (from a Hebrew word usually translated "desire"): rain's for the earth, God's for His people, a wife's for her husband, and evil desire's for Cain.[^6] The notes say the rabbis "intentionally reverse expected relationships" to teach that *teshuqah* "is not about want or lack, but about purpose and design."[^6] Why might the rabbis have chosen such surprising pairings, and what do they suggest about the idea that desire can be good when it is rightly oriented?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Marty describes how Becky's calm presence helps him with anxiety: "If she were identical to Marty, she couldn't provide this balancing support."[^7] Becky describes herself as a feeling empath, and Marty calls himself an "overextended thinker."[^8] In any close relationship you have, where has a difference you once found frustrating turned out to be the very thing that holds you up? What would it look like to thank that person for it this week?

</div>

### Set Apart Without Oppression <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#set-apart-without-oppression}

*The Solomons describe what being visibly different looks like for a Jewish family following Jesus, and where they drew the line.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Leviticus 20:22–26 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes say these laws were given not for health benefits or as arbitrary tests but "to set you apart from the nations to be my own," and that *kadosh* "fundamentally means 'different,' 'separate,' or 'set apart.'"[^5] What reasons does the passage itself give for the distinctions it describes?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** When their daughter Abigail said, "Dad, I don't want to be different," Marty recognized a boundary: different enough to be *kadosh* "but not so different as to be oppressive or alienating."[^9] When she asked why a neighbor was mowing on Saturday, they taught that "Sabbath is the Solomon family's practice, not a universal rule to judge others by."[^10] Why do you think the line between being set apart and becoming judgmental is so easy to cross?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty emphasizes that this path "is specifically for those with Jewish heritage living in covenant relationship with God through Jesus" and is "not a model for Gentile believers to adopt."[^5] Each of our traditions has its own ways of marking believers as distinct. What practices in your tradition set people apart in a healthy way, and when has a practice slid toward judging others?

</div>

### Grace for the Season <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#grace-for-the-season}

*Marty and Becky warn against a rigid faith that leaves children weary of Jesus, and they practice grace for seasons when little is possible.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Ephesians 6:4 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes say Marty sees this verse as "a critical warning against rigid religious methodology that associates Jesus with burdensome rules about music, media, behavior, and countless 'don'ts.'"[^4] The goal: "children who hear Jesus' name and respond with desire for more, not with weariness."[^4] What do you notice about the two halves of the verse, and what helped you (or would have helped you) associate faith with desire rather than weariness?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> When their children were toddlers, Becky "felt guilty about not maintaining prayer times, Bible reading, or other practices," and Marty helped her see "she was in a demanding season requiring grace, not guilt."[^11] Today she keeps regular morning prayer. What season are you in right now, and what would grace for that season look like?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The Solomons recite five family values before dinner (We Worship, We Bring Hope, We Create Memories, We Go All In, We Are Individuals) and use them to make decisions, which the notes call "structure without legalism."[^12] If your household, friendship group or small group had one value to recite together, what would you want it to be, and how would it change one decision you face this month?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Marty and Becky offered a rare look at how theology becomes daily life. In marriage, *ezer k'negdo* pictures two planks that oppose each other and hold each other up. In their family, being *kadosh* means being visibly different without becoming oppressive or judgmental, and Marty is clear that this path belongs to their Jewish calling. In parenting and in their own spiritual lives, they choose grace for each season over rigid rules that leave people weary of Jesus.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, where do you need to receive grace for the season you are in?

</div>
