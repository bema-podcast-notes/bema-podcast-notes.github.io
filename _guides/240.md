**Last week:** Reed Dent and Brent described faith as Abraham lived it, trust expressed by walking rather than agreement with a catechism, and they urged us to say "tell me what you mean" before we say "I disagree."[^1]

**This week:** Elle Grover Fricks joins Brent (Marty is away) for a Hebrew word study on sin and a tour of the archaeology behind Israel's entry into the land.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Elle organizes six Hebrew words for wrongdoing around the image of the path (*derech*). *Khata* is missing the mark, an unintentional step off the path; *avah* is twisting the path; *pesha* is breaking away from a covenant; *asham*, pictured as a weary camel, is sitting down on the path. Two more words, *ra'a* (evil) and *resha* (wickedness), describe noisy, shattering violence against wholeness.[^3] English often flattens all of these into one or two words.

The second half turns to archaeology. Elle describes Egyptian military control of the Canaanite city-states in the Late Bronze Age, the Sea Peoples who forced Egypt to withdraw and destroy its own fortifications, and distinctive new settlements that appear around 1207 BCE, which she connects to Israel's arrival.[^4] She also explains the name "Philistine" as "to roll in like a rock," and she models approaching archaeology "from a posture of confidence rather than fear," since it is an unfolding science full of vigorous debate.[^5]

### Six Ways Off the Path <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#six-ways-off-the-path}

*Elle shows that Hebrew has distinct words for different ways of failing to walk God's path.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes describe four path words: *khata* as "an unintentional step off the path," *avah* as "taking what God has asked and twisting it for one's own purposes," *pesha* as "explicitly rejecting the commitment to walk the path," and *asham* as "simply sitting down and stopping."[^3] Elle illustrates *pesha* with Boromir's attempt to take the Ring from Frodo.[^6] Which distinction is most new to you? Can you think of a biblical character or story that fits one of the four?

*Read Leviticus 4:13–14 aloud, then Leviticus 4:22–23.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** Elle connects *asham* to the offerings in Leviticus 4 for leaders and for the whole community, and describes the offering as saying, "We've been tired, we've stopped running the race, but here's how we start again."[^7] Look at who these verses address and listen for the word your translation uses when the community or leader realizes something is wrong. What does it suggest that God provided a way back specifically for leaders and whole communities?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> *Ra'a* and *resha* do not fit the path image. The notes say *ra'a* evokes "the sound of violent shattering," the opposite of *tov* (good), *shalom* (peace) and *tamim* (complete, whole), and that a wicked person, like a plate shattering, "enters a room seeking to get people agitated and disturbed."[^8] How does picturing evil as noise and shattering, rather than as a rule broken, change the way you think about it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Elle notes that *avah* carries weight because God calls his people to be upright (*yashar*), a word the notes find inside the name Israel.[^9] Which way of stepping off the path (missing, twisting, breaking away, or sitting down) is most familiar to you in this season? You are welcome to answer silently. What would getting back up and walking look like?

</div>

### A Land Recently Vacated <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#a-land-recently-vacated}

*Elle reads the conquest story against what the ground has revealed about the Late Bronze Age.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes lay out the evidence: the Amarna Letters from Canaanite kings to Pharaoh, Egyptian artifacts in the tel layers, a consistent ash layer from Egypt destroying its own fortifications as it withdrew, and distinctive elliptical settlements with storage holes, very large pottery and multifamily dwellings.[^4] The result, in the notes' words, was "a recently vacated territory where city-states had only residual militias."[^10] Which piece of that picture did you find most surprising or helpful, and why?

*Read 2 Samuel 5:6–8 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Elle tells how "a single olive pit beneath fortifications in Jerusalem" changed scholars' dating of a fortification and with it their view of 2 Samuel 5's account of David capturing the city.[^11] What does this story suggest about holding today's scholarly consensus, in either direction, with some humility?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes say the episode "models how to approach potentially challenging topics like archaeology and textual criticism from a posture of confidence rather than fear," recognizing that academic archaeology "involves vigorous debate and revision."[^5] Our traditions have handled questions about the Bible and history in different ways, and each way has its reasons. When you meet a claim from scholarship that seems to unsettle a biblical account, what is your usual first reaction? What would confidence rather than fear look like for you?

</div>

### Those Who Rolled In <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#those-who-rolled-in}

*Elle ends with a word about the Philistines that turns out to be a word about outsiders.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes explain the Hebrew *palash* as "to roll in like a rock" and suggest the name may have reminded Israel that the Philistines were outsiders (*gerim*) whom Torah calls Israel to care for, "a pointed irony given Israel's frequent conflicts with the Philistines."[^12] Why might a people's enemies and the strangers they are called to love turn out to be the same people?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** If every mention of the Philistines can work as a reminder of the call to love outsiders,[^13] who are "those who rolled in" in your own neighborhood, workplace or church? What is one way you could care for one of them this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> This week Elle gave us a richer vocabulary for how we fail. We can miss the mark, twist the path, break away from it, or simply sit down, and evil itself is a noise that shatters wholeness. God provided a way for tired leaders and communities to get up and walk again. Elle also showed us a land that had just been emptied of its occupying army when Israel arrived, and she modeled meeting archaeology with confidence rather than fear. Even the name of Israel's old enemy carries a reminder to love those who roll in from somewhere else.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, which of this week's Hebrew words will you carry with you, and why?

</div>
