### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** John wrote a funeral dirge for Rome at the height of its power and called God's people to come out of her. The battle was won by the word from the rider's mouth, the thousand years promised vindication to the faithful, and not one name was forgotten.

**This week:** We reach the last two chapters of the Bible. The episode argues that the story does not end with God's people leaving earth for heaven but with heaven coming down to earth, and with the garden of Eden open again.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The central theme of the episode is the reunion of heaven and earth, reversing the separation that began in Genesis 3. "God's dwelling place is now among the people," and creation's groaning in Romans 8 finds its answer.[^1] John is not inventing new theology; he weaves together promises from Isaiah, Ezekiel, Zechariah and Daniel to assure persecuted readers that God's ancient promises can be trusted.[^2]

The new Jerusalem is full of twelves (tribes, apostles, gates, foundations) and enormous, about fourteen hundred miles square, which the notes read as a message of abundance and inclusion: "there is enough of God's presence for everyone."[^3] There is no temple in the city, because God and the Lamb are its temple, and the tree of life, closed since Genesis 3, stands open again. In the episode's words, the end is revealed to be a new beginning.[^4]

### God's Dwelling Comes Down <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#gods-dwelling-comes-down}

*The last vision of the Bible moves in a direction many of us did not expect.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Revelation 21:1–5 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Pay attention to direction in these verses. What is coming down, where is it going, and who is moving toward whom? The notes say this "is not about believers escaping earth to go to heaven, but about God's dwelling place coming down to be among people."[^5] What do you notice when you read the passage with that in mind?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** Read Isaiah 65:17–19 and Romans 8:19–22. The notes say John draws on Isaiah 65's new heavens and new earth, with God wiping away tears, and that the reunion of heaven and earth fulfills Paul's picture of creation groaning for restoration. "Since the Fall, when soil and spirit were torn apart, the created order has been waiting for this moment of reconciliation."[^6] What does it add to hear Revelation's ending as the answer to promises and longings that run all the way back through the prophets and Paul?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say John's vision is "not a new creation ex nihilo," not something made from nothing, "but a restoration and fulfillment of what was always intended."[^7] Our traditions may have taught about heaven, the new earth and the end in different ways. Without settling those differences, how might you live differently this week if the story ends with creation restored rather than left behind?

</div>

### A City of Twelves <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#a-city-of-twelves}

*The new Jerusalem is measured in numbers that are meant to be felt more than calculated.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Revelation 21:9–21 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Count every twelve you can find in this passage, and notice what is written on the gates and on the foundations. The notes say the twelve tribes and twelve apostles together show "the unity of God's people across both Old and New Testament eras."[^8] The city measures twelve thousand stadia, about fourteen hundred miles, covering the whole known civilized world, which the notes read as "abundance and inclusion rather than literal architecture."[^9] What picture of God's people does this city paint?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Now read Revelation 21:22–27. John says, "I did not see a temple in the city." The notes explain that the whole new creation has become the temple, the place where heaven and earth meet, with God and the Lamb as its temple.[^10] The twelve precious stones correspond to the stones on the high priest's breastplate, so that "the entire new creation functions as the Holy of Holies, with all of God's people serving as priests." As they put it, "What was once mediated through a single high priest entering once a year is now the permanent reality for all."[^11] The gates are never shut, picturing nations streaming in without fear.[^12] What do you think it would have meant to first-century readers to hear that the whole city is the Holy of Holies, open to every one of God's people?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes put the point simply: "there is room for all peoples."[^13] Who do you find yourself quietly assuming there might not be room for, and what would it mean to picture them inside the open gates?

</div>

### Back to the Garden <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#back-to-the-garden}

*The final scene returns to the first one.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Revelation 22:1–5 aloud, then Genesis 2:8–10 and Ezekiel 47:12.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** List the echoes of Eden you hear in Revelation 22. The notes point to the tree of life, forbidden since Genesis 3 and now accessible; the river flowing from God's throne; light without the sun, as in Genesis 1 before the sun was made; the curse lifted; and people seeing God's face.[^14] They also trace the river and the healing trees to Ezekiel 47.[^15] Why do you think the Bible's last chapter reaches back so deliberately to its first?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say John's repeated "I am coming soon" spoke to his readers' immediate suffering, and that the promise to them "is not escape from suffering but the assurance that their faithfulness participates in God's ultimate victory."[^16] The episode's title sums it up: the end is a new beginning.[^17] Where in your life do you need an ending to turn out to be a beginning, and what would trusting that look like this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The Bible does not end with God's people escaping the earth. It ends with God's dwelling place coming down to be among them, heaven and earth reunited after the long separation that began in Genesis 3. The city is built of twelves, tribes and apostles together, so vast that there is room for everyone, with no temple because the whole of it is holy ground. And at its center is the garden again: the river, the tree of life, the curse lifted, and God's face seen. For persecuted believers, and for us, the end turns out to be a new beginning.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one image from Revelation 21–22 you want to carry with you into the final week of this session?

</div>
