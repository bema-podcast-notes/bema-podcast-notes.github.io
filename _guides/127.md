### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Jesus escaped three traps, telling his questioners to give Caesar his coin but not their worship, naming love as the weightiest commandment, and pushing the meaning of "neighbor" all the way to a Samaritan.

**This week:** Jesus pronounces a series of woes in Matthew 23. The episode shows how he affirms the Pharisees' devotion to Scripture while warning against righteousness performed for an audience, and how one extra woe turns Isaiah's warning about greed into a warning about self-righteousness.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode begins with Jesus' instruction to listen to those who sit in "Moses' seat," a physical seat in the synagogue for Scripture readers. Jesus affirms their devotion to the Text while warning against the way they walk it out. The Greek word behind "hypocrite," which the notes spell *hupocritos*, means "actor": someone performing for an audience, even if they do exactly what they say.

The heart of the episode is a comparison. Isaiah 5 contains six woes that describe a greedy person. Jesus gives seven, and the episode argues that this uses the symbolism of numbers (six for sinful humanity, seven for completion) to describe self-righteousness as "perfected sin." Marty applies this pointedly to his own generation and to himself. Because the subject is religious performance, every tradition in the room, and every person, can find themselves here. Please hear it as a mirror rather than a weapon.

### Moses' Seat and the Actor <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#moses-seat-and-the-actor}

*Jesus honors devotion to the Text and warns against turning it into a performance.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 23:1–12 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes explain that "Moses' seat" was a physical element of the synagogue where Scripture readers sat. Jesus affirms the Pharisees when they read and teach Scripture, but warns against their *halakhah*, "the way they walk out and interpret the Text in daily life." The notes paraphrase it this way: "Listen to their teaching because they teach on the Text, they are devoted to the Text, they read the Text."[^1] What do you notice in these verses that honors the Pharisees, alongside what warns against them? Why might it matter that Jesus begins with respect?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The episode says *hupocritos* literally means "actor." It is not mainly about being two-faced: "You can say something and do exactly what you say and still be a hypocrite," and "You can give 11% of your income as a tithe and be a hupocritos the entire time." The notes add that the Pharisees "were actually extremely consistent with their teachings and their halakhah"; Jesus' critique "is not about inconsistency but about performance."[^2] How does this definition change the way you have used the word "hypocrite"? What does it do to the stereotype many of us inherited about the Pharisees?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Brent shares that he once used Matthew 23:9 ("call no man father") as a reason to step away from Catholic faith. The episode clarifies that Jesus' point is "not about titles themselves but about how we use them," acknowledging that titles like "Father" can serve a helpful purpose and that "any title can be abused when it distinguishes rather than serves."[^3] What titles or roles operate in your church tradition? How can you tell when one is serving the community and when it is serving the person who holds it?

</div>

### Seven Woes <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#seven-woes}

*Jesus names seven ways devotion turns into performance.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 23:13–32 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** As you listen, try to count the woes and name each image. The notes list them: shutting the Kingdom's door, corrupt converts, blind guides on oaths, straining gnats and swallowing camels, clean outside and dirty inside, whitewashed tombs, and building the prophets' tombs.[^4] Which image stays with you most, and why?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe the oath loopholes, a hierarchy of swearing by the altar, the temple, or the gold of the temple that let someone break a lesser vow. They also mention a Jewish teaching that "if you actually start by cleaning the inside, you will clean the outside in the process."[^5] Where do you see the modern version of the loophole, "technically following the letter while violating the spirit"?[^6] What would it look like to start with the inside of the cup?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes describe evangelical believers who have their theology "tied up in a nice little bow," can "run circles in Bible study," and live exactly as they preach, yet do "everything for a show." Marty confesses, "I can struggle with this so easily in my life."[^7] Without naming anyone else, where in your own faith is it easiest to slip into performing?

</div>

### Six or Seven? <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#six-or-seven}

*One extra woe turns a portrait of greed into a portrait of self-righteousness.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 5:8–23 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Count Isaiah's woes as you listen. The notes say Isaiah's six woes describe a greedy person: adding house to house and field to field, running after strong drink, calling evil good, and building empires on the backs of the marginalized.[^8] What do you notice about the kind of person Isaiah is describing? How is it different from the person Jesus describes in Matthew 23?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The episode explains that in Jewish numerology six is "the number of sinful humanity" and seven "the number of completion or perfection." By adding a seventh woe, Jesus says in effect: "You have perfected the art of evil and sin." He follows it with "Go ahead then and complete what your ancestors started."[^9] The episode argues that self-righteousness is worse than greed because "at least people know greed when they see it," while self-righteous people put "a completely counterfeit version of God on display."[^10] What do you find persuasive, or not, in that argument? What makes self-righteousness so much harder to see than greed?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Read Matthew 23:37–39. After the woes, Jesus laments over Jerusalem and quotes Psalm 118, a phrase the notes flag for further study.[^11] What do you notice about the tone of these last verses compared with the woes before them?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** Marty warns his own generation, those who have rightly rejected the greed and oppression of earlier generations, not to move "from the greed of fundamentalism to the self-righteousness of progressive activism," missing "the pride that lurks just under the surface of our self-righteousness."[^12] The notes say the warning cuts both ways: fundamentalist communities can fall into external performance, and progressive ones can replace one perfected sin with another.[^13] Our traditions sit in different places on this map, so begin by asking which danger your own community is more prone to. Then ask the personal question: what sin of your spiritual ancestors are you most at risk of "perfecting"?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Jesus honors the Pharisees' devotion to Scripture and warns against the way devotion becomes a show. A *hupocritos* is an actor, and you can be perfectly consistent and still be performing for an audience. Isaiah gave six woes against greed; Jesus gave seven, describing a self-righteousness that puts a counterfeit God on display. Marty's warning is not aimed at someone else but at himself and at us. The episode's antidote is humility: "make sure that we see all people, help and serve all people, and pursue humility in our walk after God."
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one thing you could do this week with no audience at all?

</div>
