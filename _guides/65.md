### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Third Isaiah called the exiles "my servant" again and again, and read in that flow, Isaiah 53 explained their suffering as a path to healing for the generations after them. Jesus lived that calling perfectly to show us how to live it.

**This week:** Isaiah's fourth voice speaks hope to the remnant who survived the exile, offering grace that makes no human sense along with a call to become the people God always intended.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This episode begins with a review of every prophet covered so far, arranged by period: the pre-Assyrian prophets (Amos, Hosea, Micah, 1 Isaiah), the Assyrian period (Jonah, Nahum, Zephaniah, 2 Isaiah), the Babylonian period (Jeremiah, Lamentations, Habakkuk, Obadiah, Joel), and the exile (Ezekiel, Daniel, Job, 3 Isaiah).[^1] It then turns to Isaiah 55–66, which speaks hope to the remnant, the survivors who persevered through suffering.[^2]

Three moments anchor the episode. The famous line "My thoughts are not your thoughts" (Isaiah 55:8–9), often used to explain divine mystery, turns out in context to describe God's scandalous forgiveness.[^3] Isaiah 58 rebukes religious ritual divorced from justice and calls God's people "Repairer of Broken Walls," echoing the vineyard of 1 Isaiah.[^4] And the episode names Sabbath as a principle that frames the whole of Scripture.[^5] Our traditions think about justice, ritual, and Sabbath in different ways, which should make for a rich conversation.

### Scandalous Grace <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#scandalous-grace}

*After chapters of judgment and suffering, Isaiah's fourth voice opens with an invitation.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 55:1–9 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes say Isaiah 55 "shifts tone dramatically": an invitation to "come" for those who are thirsty and have no money, free wine and milk, an everlasting covenant connected to David, and a call to seek the Lord "while He may be found."[^6] As you listen, what do you notice about who is invited, what things cost, and what God asks the people to do?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes explain that "My thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways" is usually used to explain divine mystery. In context, though, it comes right after "Let the wicked forsake their ways... Let them turn to the Lord and He will have mercy" and is followed by assurance of free pardon. The point: "God's willingness to forgive completely doesn't make human sense."[^7] The notes credit this insight to Rachel Held Evans in *Faith Unraveled*.[^8] Read verses 6–9 again. How have you usually heard verses 8–9 used? What changes when you hear them as a statement about God's forgiveness rather than about mystery?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say this hope is "rooted in God's character, not wishful thinking," and that grace this complete "defies human logic."[^9] Where do you find God's forgiveness hardest to believe, whether for yourself or for someone else? What would it look like to let God's ways be higher than yours in that place?

</div>

### The Fast God Chooses <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-fast-god-chooses}

*Isaiah 58 shows that grace comes with a calling, and the calling is not new.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 58:1–12 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes set false fasting beside true fasting. False fasting looks righteous and eager to know God's ways, but it exploits workers, ends in quarreling and violence, and amounts to "Merely bowing heads and wearing sackcloth." True fasting looses the chains of injustice, sets the oppressed free, shares food with the hungry, shelters the poor wanderer, and clothes the naked.[^10] As you listen, list the actions on each side. What do you notice about who benefits from each kind of fast?

*Read Isaiah 5:1–7 aloud (we first met this vineyard in Week 19).*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes show that Isaiah 58 deliberately calls back to 1 Isaiah's vineyard. There, God expected *tzedekah* ("righteousness") but found *tze'ekah* ("cry of the oppressed"), and removed the hedge and broke down the walls as judgment. Here, God promises to rebuild "ancient ruins" and "age-old foundations," and calls his people "Repairer of Broken Walls."[^11] The notes conclude that God's expectations have not changed: "He still wants fruit of justice and righteousness."[^12] What does it mean that the same people whose walls were torn down are invited to become the ones who repair them?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes call Isaiah 58 a "Critique of religious performance divorced from compassionate action" and a "Challenge to examine if our 'righteousness' includes care for the marginalized."[^13] Our traditions emphasize different things when we talk about faithfulness: worship, doctrine, personal holiness, evangelism, service. None of those is in view as the problem here; the problem is ritual that leaves the oppressed where they are. In your own life, where does your practice of faith already reach the hungry, the poor, or the oppressed, and where might Isaiah 58 be asking you to let it reach further?

</div>

### Sabbath and the Remnant <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#sabbath-and-the-remnant}

*The chapter on justice ends with a call to rest, and the episode thinks that is no accident.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 58:13–14 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes identify Sabbath as a bookend of Scripture. Genesis 1 is structured around Sabbath rest, God's invitation to trust his goodness. 2 Chronicles, the last book of the Hebrew Tanakh, closes with Sabbath as a reason for the exile, because Israel forgot the invitation to rest.[^14] And Isaiah 58 ends its call to justice with Sabbath and "the inheritance of your father Jacob," the name given to the servant in 3 Isaiah.[^15] What do you notice about the promises attached to Sabbath in these two verses? Why might a chapter about justice end with rest?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe the remnant as the survivors who stay faithful after judgment, connected to Elijah's "7,000 who have not bowed to Baal," a "Small remaining quantity with which God can rebuild."[^16] The remnant's hope comes with a warning: restoration requires becoming the people God originally called Israel to be.[^17] What encouragement, and what challenge, do you hear in being part of a small remnant God can rebuild with?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes cite Walter Brueggemann's *Sabbath as Resistance*, which treats Sabbath as countercultural resistance in a culture driven by productivity and consumption: "Not formulaic obedience but heart posture of trust."[^18] Setting aside how our traditions define the Sabbath day, what is one concrete way you could practice that kind of trusting rest in the coming week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Isaiah's fourth voice speaks to the remnant, the people who made it through the exile. It opens with an invitation to come without money and with a forgiveness so complete that it makes no human sense. Then it reminds them what God has always wanted: not bowed heads and sackcloth, but chains loosed, the hungry fed, and broken walls repaired. And it ends with rest, the invitation that frames Scripture from Genesis to Chronicles. Grace, justice, and Sabbath belong together, and the remnant is invited into all three.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, name one broken wall you could help repair, or one way you will practice trusting rest, this week.

</div>
