### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Moses struck the rock in anger, reaching for Pharaoh's stick instead of the shepherd's voice, and failed to show the people a God who is different. How we lead, we learned, matters as much as where we lead people.[^1]

**This week:** In Deuteronomy, Moses' final teaching, Israel is told again and again to remember where they came from, and that memory becomes the reason to care for the foreigner, the orphan and the widow.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Deuteronomy is Moses' farewell before Israel enters the land. The episode notes that it follows the five-part shape of an ancient suzerain-vassal covenant (preamble, historical summary, stipulations, rewards and punishments, and a concluding song), a form its first hearers would have recognized, with God rather than a human king as the great ruler. The book's key words are *zakar*, "remember," which appears 14 times, and *shakach*, "forget," with 9 warnings not to forget.

Over and over the text says, "Remember that you were slaves in Egypt," and attaches that memory to the foreigner, the orphan and the widow: gleaning laws, a third-year tithe, and festivals that must include them. The episode describes a cycle in which remembering our own story makes us notice the vulnerable, and caring for them reminds us of our story. It closes with Moses' epitaph, which speaks of a man who knew God "face to face" rather than of the land he did not enter.

### Remember That You Were Slaves <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#remember-that-you-were-slaves}

*Deuteronomy ties Israel's memory of Egypt directly to how they treat the vulnerable.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Deuteronomy 24:17–22 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes say the phrase "Remember that you were slaves in Egypt" serves as the foundation for how God's people should treat marginalized groups.[^3] In this passage, who is protected, what exactly are farmers told to leave behind, and what reason is given? How many times does the reason come up?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe Deuteronomy as following the structure of ancient suzerain-vassal covenants, familiar to its original audience, while subverting the usual power arrangement by placing God, not a human ruler, as the suzerain. The 2017 notes add that the whole Torah can be viewed as one complete covenant structure.[^4] What changes when you read Deuteronomy as a covenant between partners rather than as a list of rules?

*Read Deuteronomy 16:9–12 and 14:28–29 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes call these laws not charity but justice: a structural system that wrote care for the vulnerable into the harvest, the tithe every third year, and the festivals, rather than leaving it to individual charitable impulses.[^5] They also apply the principle beyond ancient Israel: nations built by immigrants forgetting their immigrant story, individuals who knew poverty forgetting the poor, communities that received help forgetting to help, and churches that were once marginalized forgetting to welcome the marginalized.[^6] People in this room may see the political questions here quite differently, so start with the text. What is the difference between charity and the kind of structural care these passages describe? And which of the notes' examples comes closest to your own experience?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes describe a cycle: when we remember where we came from, we notice the marginalized, and when we care for the marginalized, we are reminded of our own origins. When we forget where we came from, we stop caring for those now in the positions we once occupied.[^7] What part of your own story, if you remembered it more often, would change whom you notice? Who might that be in your life right now?

</div>

### Face to Face <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#face-to-face}

*The Torah's last word about Moses is not his failure but his friendship with God.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Deuteronomy 34:1–12 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Last week we watched Moses lose the chance to enter the land. The notes say his epitaph emphasizes his intimate relationship with God ("face to face") rather than his failure to enter.[^8] As you listen, what does the text mention about Moses' death and life, and what does it leave unsaid? Which details stand out to you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes explain that the Hebrew expression *panim el panim* ("face to face") uses language normally reserved for intimate marriage, suggesting the closeness of lovers kissing. They add that Moses dies "by the mouth of the Lord," which may mean a divine kiss.[^9] We followed the marriage picture from the wedding at Sinai to the honeymoon in the desert. How does this image of Moses' death land with you, and how does it fit that larger story?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes draw out a redefinition of success: it is measured by relationship with God, not by achieving goals.[^10] Moses' greatest goal was the land, and he did not reach it, yet the Torah remembers him for knowing God face to face. What do you make of that? Where does this way of measuring a life challenge the way you measure your own?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes describe remembering as an active spiritual discipline, not passive nostalgia. In Deuteronomy, *zakar* involves communal storytelling, passing the story from one generation to the next, and connecting past experience with present behavior.[^11] What is one practice of remembering you could begin this week, and who would you want to do it with?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#closing}

> Deuteronomy ends the Torah with an urgent plea: remember. Remember that you were slaves in Egypt, and let that memory shape how you treat the foreigner, the orphan and the widow, through harvests left in the field, tithes shared, and feasts with room at the table. When we forget where we came from, we stop seeing the people standing where we once stood. And the book ends not with Moses' failure but with a man who knew God face to face, a reminder that the measure of a life is the depth of our relationship with God and the love that flows from it.[^12]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what part of your story do you most need to remember, and whom does it help you see?

</div>
