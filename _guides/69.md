### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Zechariah gave the remnant decades of pictures meant to give hope to people in the present, not timetables. His king on a donkey taught them to walk obediently within empire and wait for rescue through humility and God's providence.[^1]

**This week:** Malachi, whose name means "my messenger," speaks to a community disappointed by the return. God and the people trade questions and answers, and a faithful few respond by writing a scroll of remembrance.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The remnant had come home expecting the glory of the old kingdom, but they were still under Persian rule. The episode, drawing on Walter Brueggemann's *Out of Babylon*, describes Persia's control as longer, wider and more subtle than Babylon's: a cultural servitude that worked "from the inside out."[^3] Disappointed, the people slid into apathy. They offered blind and lame animals, held back their tithes and concluded that it was futile to serve God.[^4]

Malachi exposes this through a distinctive pattern: God makes an accusation, the people respond with a defensive "How have we...?", and God gives the evidence.[^5] The episode centers on a question from Rabbi David Fohrman, "Do we imagine new tomorrows or do we lie to ourselves about a better past?", and Marty shares his own struggle with nostalgia.[^6] Unlike many prophetic books, Malachi ends with a positive response: a remnant who fear the Lord, talk with each other and are called God's treasured possession.[^7]

### Opening <span class="guide-time">10 min</span> {#opening}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-opening" markdown="1">

**Opening Question:** Think of a season you call "the good old days," whether in a church, a job, a family or a town. What made it good, and what do you tend to leave out when you remember it?

</div>

### How Have We? <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#how-have-we}

*Malachi's dialogue shows a people who honestly cannot see their own drift.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Malachi 1:6–8 and Malachi 3:7–10 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes lay out Malachi's pattern: God makes an accusation, the people respond with a defensive question, God provides specific evidence, and the cycle repeats.[^8] Find each step in the two passages you just read. What do you notice about the people's questions, and what do they suggest about how well the people know themselves?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes contrast later Jewish practice, with its extreme care to offer spotless sacrifices, with the attitude of Malachi's audience: "A blind goat... who cares. A goat's a goat. Temple is a temple; path is a path."[^9] Brent points out that giving then meant gathering goods and animals and traveling to bring them, and the people's attitude became, "What? It doesn't even do any good, so why go to all this effort?"[^10] The notes say the root issue was not theological confusion but apathy. How does disappointment with outcomes turn into carelessness with practices?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes say Malachi 3:10, the well-known tithing passage, "is often misused as a prosperity formula," and that in context it is about the consequences of not believing God's promises and taking an apathetic approach to covenant faithfulness.[^11] Our churches have taught and practiced giving in different ways, and this verse may carry memories for some of us. How have you heard Malachi 3:10 used, and what changes when you read it as a word to disappointed, apathetic people?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q4:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Where has a disappointing outcome made you half-hearted about a practice that once mattered to you, such as prayer, giving, worship, Scripture or community? What might bringing your best, rather than a "blind goat," look like there this month?

</div>

### A Better Past or New Tomorrows <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#a-better-past-or-new-tomorrows}

*The people's apathy grows out of a story they tell themselves about the past.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Malachi 3:13–15 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** In this exchange the people say, "It is futile to serve God. What do we gain? Evildoers prosper."[^12] The episode pairs this with Rabbi Fohrman's question: "Do we imagine new tomorrows or do we lie to ourselves about a better past?" and with a fundamental truth: "You can't go backwards, you can only go forward."[^13] How does a romanticized past make the present feel futile, and what would it take for Malachi's audience to imagine a new tomorrow instead?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Marty describes friends who were miserable in an old setting, moved somewhere challenging and began remembering the old place as "the good old days." He admits he has felt the same pull toward "how it was four years ago," and says: "When I begin to believe this [nostalgia], I start to live much more poorly in the present. I get a lot more cynical, jaded, bitter and angry. I fall into bad attitudes and bad habits. I find myself beginning to fall into sins of the past."[^14] Where do you recognize that pattern, in yourself or in a community you love, and what helps break it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe Persia's control as cultural rather than physical: not robbing possessions but robbing identity as God's partners, enticing people into a system of control.[^15] The episode applies this to us: "It's harder to fight the idolatry present in our Western American culture than outright idolatry because it's more subtle and hidden and internal."[^16] What is one subtle way our culture shapes you "from the inside out," and how would you notice it?

</div>

### A Scroll of Remembrance <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#a-scroll-of-remembrance}

*Malachi's message lands, and a few people respond.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Malachi 3:16–18 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes say the faithful remnant heard the message, "Feared the Lord and talked with each other," wrote a "scroll of remembrance," and were called God's "treasured possession," echoing the language of the Exodus covenant.[^17] What do you notice about what this group actually does, and why might talking with each other be part of the response?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The episode closes with a theme it finds throughout Scripture, that small groups of committed people can change everything, pointing to the 7,000 who had not bowed to Baal in Elijah's day and to this remnant.[^18] It describes our moment as a culture "dominated by debate and argument and bifurcated worldviews" that "seems to be beyond hope," and says a few people putting their names on a scroll can still make a difference.[^19] What would it look like for this group to put our names on a scroll, and what would we be committing to?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes list the remnant's examples alongside Elijah's 7,000 as proof that a faithful few can matter.[^20] Who has been part of a faithful few for you, people who kept walking the path when others lost heart, and what did their faithfulness make possible in your life?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Malachi's people came home and found that the glory they expected had not arrived. Living under a quieter empire, they grew disappointed and then apathetic, offering blind goats and asking, "How have we...?" when God named it. The episode asks whether we will imagine new tomorrows or lie to ourselves about a better past, because we cannot go backwards. A small remnant chose to fear the Lord, talk with each other and write their names on a scroll, and God called them his treasured possession.[^21]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, name one thing from "the good old days" you want to stop going back to, or one new tomorrow you want to imagine.

</div>

Esther never names God, yet God seems present throughout, and the episode follows Rabbi David Fohrman in tracing Esther's parallels to Joseph, her shrewd wisdom before the king, and Mordecai's challenge that silence in the face of injustice is complicity.[^22]

**What to bring next week:** Read Esther 4. Notice Mordecai's argument to Esther and Esther's reply, and come ready to say which line strikes you most and why.
