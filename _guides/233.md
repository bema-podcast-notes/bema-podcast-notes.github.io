### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Elle Grover Fricks set Jephthah's story beside his daughter's: both were pushed out through no fault of their own, but only the father was sought and brought home.[^1]

**This week:** Josh Bossé revisits the story of Moses striking the rock at Kadesh and asks why Moses, of all people, did not enter the Promised Land.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The notes for this episode are brief, so this guide is shorter and stays close to what they record. The conversation begins with Episode 30, where Marty proposed a theory about why Moses (the notes call him Moshe) was kept out of the Promised Land; people got upset, and the notes stress it was "simply a theory." God says plainly that Moses did not trust him and did not show him as holy, but the text gives little clarity about how, and Ramban called the reason one of the great mysteries of the Torah.[^3]

Josh works through four puzzles in the story: why the people do not mourn Miriam, why their complaint is so strange, why God has Moses take the staff, and why Moses is so angry. His suggestion is that the people were eager to move on toward the land, that Moses's anger came from grief, and that God turns the word "rebels" back on Moses and Aaron.[^4]

<div class="guide-q guide-q-opening" markdown="1">

**Opening Question:** Think of a time when the people around you were ready to move on (from a place, a season, or a way of doing things) before you were. What made it hard for you to let go?

### Josh's Four Questions <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#joshs-four-questions}

*Josh reads the story slowly and lets its oddities raise the questions.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Numbers 20:1–13 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Josh noticed four problems in this story: the people do not mourn Miriam; they make a strange complaint (they wish they had died with the earlier generation, they dislike Kadesh, they have an odd "shopping list," and water comes last); God has Moses take the staff even though he was not supposed to strike the rock; and Moses, usually patient, is suddenly angry.[^4] Listen for each of these in the text. Which do you see, and what other questions does the passage raise for you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say people got really upset about Marty's earlier theory, and that Ramban considered the reason Moses does not enter the land one of the great mysteries of the Torah.[^3] Why do you think unresolved questions in Scripture can upset us so much? How do you personally hold a question the text leaves open?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Josh points out that the people have been at Kadesh before; it is where the last generation "tripped up" and then wandered for another generation. In his reading this is a new group of people, zealous to do God's mission and enter the land, and if they are guilty of anything, "it's of being TOO zealous."[^4] How does that change the way you hear their complaint? When have you mistaken someone's eagerness for rebellion, or had your own eagerness misread?

</div>

### Which Staff, and Who Rebelled? <span class="guide-time">35 min</span> {#which-staff-and-who-rebelled}

*Josh suggests the staff itself carries a message, and that the real rebels may not be the people.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Numbers 17:1–10 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Josh says the real question is not why the staff, but which staff. The notes say it is believed to be the staff that bloomed after the Korach rebellion, which God told Moses and Aaron to keep as a reminder of rebellious behavior. It produced almonds, which Josh connects with haste because almonds are one of the first crops of the year.[^5] What does the passage say happened to the staff, and why is it kept? What might it mean that Moses is told to pick up this particular staff at Kadesh?

*Read Numbers 20:23–24 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** In Josh's reading, there is no good reason for Moses to be angry with the people, but it is reasonable that he was angry because of his grief. When Aaron is "gathered to his people," the text uses the same word Moses used to call the people rebellious; the notes say God "seems to be turning Moshe's words around him," and conclude: "The people were not the rebellious one, it was Aaron and Moshe that were rebellious." The notes even ask whether "this rock" might be Miriam's headstone.[^6] What do you make of reading Moses's anger as grief? What does it change about how you see Moses and how you see God in this story?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Josh sees close similarities with the story of Nadab and Abihu: Aaron is told he cannot mourn his sons (as Moses and Aaron do not mourn Miriam), and something is wrong with both the fire and the timing. The notes add that God takes celebration seriously, that a priest sometimes eats from his own offering, and that "There is no VIP section in the Kingdom."[^7] Read Leviticus 10:1–3. What parallels do you notice with Numbers 20?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes say, "It seems like everyone BUT Moshe knows that it's time to move on," and they close with a question: "How could this story have been different if Moshe had acted differently?" adding, "If you're a leader, do NOT miss this question."[^8] Whether you lead anything or not, where in your life might grief or fatigue be making it hard to move on when God and the people around you are ready? Share only what you are comfortable sharing.

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Josh Bossé reads the story of the rock slowly and finds a people eager to leave Kadesh behind and a leader carrying fresh grief. The staff Moses picks up is the one kept as a reminder of rebellion, and in the end the word "rebels" turns back toward Moses and Aaron. Josh's reading is one proposal; the text still holds some of its mystery. But it invites us to ask what our own grief and weariness might be doing to the way we lead and follow, and whether we can recognize when it is time to move on.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one place in your life where you sense it may be time to move on?

</div>
