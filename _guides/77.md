### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We met the Sadducees, the priestly families who bent a God-given calling toward wealth and power, and we were careful to name who actually moved against Jesus.

**This week:** The Herodians were ordinary, non-priestly Jews who believed they could enjoy Greco-Roman culture without giving up their faith, and the episode suggests many of us live much the same way.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The Herodians were followers of Herod the Great who shared the Sadducees' comfortable approach to Hellenism without being priests.[^1] The episode visits Zippori (Sepphoris), a wealthy, mostly Jewish Herodian city three miles from Nazareth, where homes like the Nile House kept geometric patterns in public rooms but displayed Greek and Egyptian mythology in private ones, and where one floor known as the "Mona Lisa of the Galilee" contains 22.5 million mosaic pieces.[^2]

The episode refuses easy answers. The notes say Hellenistic things are not inherently sinful; the danger is the worldview behind them, which tells you that you are the center of the world.[^3] Herodians were dangerously close to idolatry, compromise and exploitation, yet also uniquely positioned to redeem their culture for God's mission.[^4] The episode ends by wondering what kind of home Jesus grew up in, since Nazareth sat near Zippori and Joseph was a *tekton*. The original notes advise listeners to "Hold these ideas loosely," and this guide tries to do the same: honest self-examination without heaping guilt on anyone.[^5]

### Opening <span class="guide-time">10 min</span> {#opening}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-opening" markdown="1">

**Opening Question:** Think of one thing you own or enjoy mostly because it is beautiful or comfortable: a favorite chair, a well-made tool, a piece of art, a streaming show. What does it add to your life?

### Inside the Nile House <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#inside-the-nile-house}

*Archaeology at Zippori lets us walk through a Herodian home room by room.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes summarize the Herodian outlook in one sentence: "I can have a little bit of Rome, and I can have a little bit of God and Judaism, and I can put the two together without sacrificing either."[^6] They describe a spectrum from enthusiastic to moderate to reluctant Herodians.[^7] Put that sentence in your own words. What makes it attractive? What makes it fragile?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** In the Nile House, public rooms had only geometric mosaics, avoiding images out of respect for the commandments. The private rooms showed Greek gods such as Pan, and the dining room's centerpiece told Egyptian Nile mythology as "dinner and a show."[^8] The original notes add that Brent pointed out "God defeated the gods of the Nile," which makes the choice of decoration odd.[^9] What do you notice about the difference between the public and private rooms? Why might a faithful person end up living that way without quite intending to?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> In another home, a husband commissioned the "Mona Lisa of the Galilee," likely a portrait of his wife, with 22.5 million mosaic pieces, yet its story culminates in Dionysus drinking Zeus under the table. The notes see "how even noble intentions could be compromised by cultural values."[^10] Where have you seen a good intention, such as honoring someone you love, get tangled up with values you would not choose on purpose?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes say the question "Is there anything wrong with this?" is not black and white. Theater, art, wealth and technology are morally neutral; "The story that wealth tells you becomes the problem."[^11] They give the modern version of the Herodian defense: "I still worship God," followed by appreciation for some cultural pleasure and the shrug, "Who doesn't? I don't worship that."[^12] Where would you place yourself on the spectrum from reluctant to enthusiastic Herodian, and what story do the comforts in your life tell you about who is at the center?

</div>

### Heroes at Drinking Wine <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#heroes-at-drinking-wine}

*The episode turns to the prophet Isaiah to name the dangers of the Herodian life.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 5:8–12 and Isaiah 5:20–23 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes quote two lines from this chapter: adding "house to house and field to field" and being "heroes at drinking wine and champions at mixing drinks."[^13] Read the passage again. Make a list of who or what each "woe" is aimed at. What do these woes have in common? What do they say about how comfort and injustice can be connected?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Looking at the elaborate Zippori homes, the notes ask, "Whose labor built these elaborate homes?" and raise questions of slave labor versus paid workers and fair wages versus exploitation.[^14] They apply the same question today: enjoying products and services "without questioning whose labor produced them."[^15] What makes that question hard to ask about our own lives? What is one place where you could realistically start asking it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes name a mindset of spiritual complacency: "I'll follow God all day long, just don't challenge my comfort."[^16] When has following God challenged your comfort in a concrete way? What happened?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The episode's discussion notes warn that "This conversation can go sideways really quick," because "We have been taught how to feel terrible about ourselves and need to be careful not to overdo it."[^17] They ask instead for steps that are both significant and sustainable. What is one significant but sustainable step you could take this month in response to what you have heard today?

</div>

### Positioned for Mission <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#positioned-for-mission}

*The episode insists that the Herodians had something to offer, and it wonders about Jesus's own surroundings.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The original notes ask: "If God wants to redeem theater, sports, education, healthcare? Who knows those things better than Herodians?"[^18] The notes add that God "loves Greeks and Romans as much as Jews" and wants to redeem their culture.[^19] How does this change the way you think about the Herodians? Who do you know whose deep familiarity with a field (media, sports, medicine, business, education) positions them for God's work there?

*Read Mark 6:1–3 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The word the townspeople use for Jesus is usually translated "carpenter." The notes say the word is *tekton*, more accurately "stone worker" in that context, and that a stone quarry owned by Herod lay between Nazareth and Zippori.[^20] The original notes say this leads Marty to "wonder" what kind of home Jesus grew up in, calling it "Something we cannot know until we meet Jesus again but it's curious to wonder about nonetheless."[^21] Read the passage again. What do the people of Nazareth know about Jesus, and what bothers them? What do you feel when you imagine Jesus growing up within sight of a city like Zippori?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q11:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes also report that Jesus uses the Greek theater word *hupokrites* ("actors," later "hypocrites") and suggest a parallel between his "hen gathers its chicks" saying and a line from Euripides' play *Trojan Women*.[^22] How would it affect your reading of Jesus if he knew Greek theater well enough to use its language?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q12:** The episode's discussion notes conclude that "All groups fall short and all groups have something to bring to the table."[^23] Think about the culture you know best, at work, at home or in your neighborhood. What is one specific way your familiarity with that world could be used for God's purposes this month?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The Herodians wanted a little bit of Rome and a little bit of God, and Zippori shows what that looked like: geometric floors in public, Pan and the Nile in private, and beautiful homes built by labor no one asked about. The episode does not call the art, the comfort or the technology evil. It asks what story those things tell us about who sits at the center, and whose good we forget along the way. It also reminds us that the people who know a culture best are the ones best placed to redeem it. The Herodian question is ours too, and we are invited to hold it honestly, without despair and without excuses.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one comfort you want to hold more loosely, or one cultural space you want to bring God's story into?

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 78 — Silent Years — Essenes](/guides/78)** (21:45): The Essenes were priests who rejected the corrupt Temple and withdrew to Qumran in the desert, devoting themselves to "knowing the path and walking the path." The episode describes their meticulous scribal process behind the Dead Sea Scrolls, the lengths they went to for "living water," and the hints that John the Baptist was connected to their movement.

**What to bring next week:** Read Isaiah 40:1–5, Jeremiah 6:16 and Luke 3:2–6. Think of one thing you have done the hard way on purpose because doing it right mattered to you, and be ready to share it.
