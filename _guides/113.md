### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Jesus "explained" his parables by retelling them, inviting the disciples to ask what kind of soil they were, and he warned against weeding the Kingdom: the sorting is his job, done by his angels at the harvest.

**This week:** Two very short parables, the treasure hidden in a field and the pearl of great price, read two ways: as our search for God, and as God's search for us.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This short episode slows down over two tiny parables. The hosts notice that the treasure and the pearl form a second pair of short parables in Matthew 13, matching the earlier pair of the mustard seed and the yeast, and that all of them describe a Kingdom that is hidden, small and easy to miss.

The familiar reading connects these parables to Proverbs 2, where wisdom is searched for like hidden treasure, so that we are the ones digging and selling everything. Marty proposes a second reading rooted in Ezekiel 16, where God finds an abandoned child in an open field and makes her beautiful like a jewel. On that reading God is the one who finds the treasure, and we are what he gave everything to acquire. The episode holds both readings together, and so will we.

### A Kingdom You Have to Dig For <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#a-kingdom-you-have-to-dig-for}

*The familiar reading: we are the seekers, and the Kingdom is worth everything we have.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 13:44–46 aloud, then read Proverbs 2:1–8 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The episode points out that these two short parables mirror the earlier pair of the mustard seed and the yeast, and that the traditional reading leans on Proverbs 2:4, where wisdom is searched for like hidden treasure.[^1] Listen to the two parables again. What do the man and the merchant do, step by step, and what feeling does the text name? Then look back at Proverbs 2:1–8: which verbs there sound like the treasure hunter?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe the Kingdom of Heaven as "weird" or counterintuitive. It does not arrive with trumpets and armies, as John the Baptist expected, but is small like a mustard seed, hidden like yeast worked into dough, and buried like treasure that has to be dug up.[^2] Why do you think Jesus kept describing the Kingdom in hidden, small images? What would it take for you to notice a Kingdom like that?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The episode links searching for treasure to the good soil of Matthew 13:23, which both hears and understands. Understanding, the notes say, is the result of digging, not surface-level engagement, and preparing soil means breaking up unplowed ground, removing rocks, burning thorns and being willing to dig.[^3] Which of those four kinds of work does your own soil most need right now? What might one week of that digging look like for you?

</div>

### The Treasure Who Was Found <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#the-treasure-who-was-found}

*Marty's alternative reading: the finder is God, and the treasure is God's people.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Ezekiel 16:1–7 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Marty's case starts with a pattern. In every other parable in Matthew 13 the main character represents God: the sower, the farmer who owns the field, the one who plants the mustard seed, and the woman who works yeast into dough.[^4] (The hosts note that a woman can represent God without difficulty, since the Psalms picture God as father, shepherd and nursing mother, and Jesus uses all three images in Luke 15.)[^5] If the pattern holds, who is the man in the field and who is the merchant? And who, then, is the treasure? What changes in how the parables feel when you hear them that way?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes identify Ezekiel 16:1–7 as the primary hint (*remez*, an allusion to another passage) behind these parables. God finds Israel thrown out into an open field, says "Live!", and makes her grow like a plant of the field. The notes add that the Hebrew phrase translated "entered puberty" more literally means something like "excellent ornaments" or "beautiful jewel," and that Hebrew's small vocabulary for precious stones lets "jewel" and "pearl" overlap.[^6] What words or images in Ezekiel 16:1–7 echo the field, plants and precious things of Matthew 13? Which connection seems strongest to you, and which seems more of a stretch?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** If God is the seeker, the notes say, we were found in a desperate state, God saw our value even when we were covered in blood and rejected, and he was willing to "sell everything" to acquire us. Yet the notes refuse to throw out the first reading: "Both truths coexist: we must pursue God diligently AND we are pursued by God passionately."[^7] Which of the two readings has been more familiar to you over the years? What might shift in your week if you lived from the other one?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#closing}

> Two parables, only a few lines long, open in two directions. Read with Proverbs 2, they call us to dig for the Kingdom like hidden treasure, to prepare our soil, and to count everything else as loss. Read with Ezekiel 16, they show God walking into a field, seeing a rejected child, and saying "Live!", then giving everything he had for what he found. The Kingdom is hidden either way, and it rewards those who keep digging. And underneath our searching is the searching of a God who decided we were worth it.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: which picture will you carry this week, the one who digs or the treasure that was found, and why?

</div>
