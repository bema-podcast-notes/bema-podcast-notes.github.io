### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** The parable of the workers in the vineyard asked whether we can celebrate God's generosity toward people we think do not deserve it, and Jesus redefined greatness as servanthood on the road to Jerusalem.

**This week:** Jesus arrives in Jerusalem. The episode reads his entry as a deliberate collision between two kingdoms and follows him into the temple, past a fig tree, and into a confrontation over his authority.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode pictures Passover week with two processions. Pontius Pilate entered Jerusalem from the west on a white stallion with soldiers and banners, while Jesus came from the east, from the Mount of Olives, on a donkey with his disciples. The crowds recognized Zechariah 9:9 but missed verse 10, and their palm branches and shouts of "Hosanna" carried hopes for a violent revolution that made Jesus weep. Matthew's second, riderless donkey becomes an invitation to choose.

Jesus then clears the temple's Court of the Gentiles, the one place non-Jews could worship, which the priestly leadership had turned into a marketplace. He curses a fig tree, which the episode reads as a judgment on corrupt leadership rather than on Israel, and answers a challenge to his authority by pointing to John the Baptist. The episode was released on July 4th, and it raises questions about national loyalty that people in our traditions may answer differently.

### Two Entries, Two Kingdoms <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#two-entries-two-kingdoms}

*Two processions enter the same city from opposite sides.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes describe Pilate's yearly procession from Caesarea Maritima, with soldiers, trumpeters, banners, and a white stallion symbolizing military conquest. Its message was "Don't even think about revolting." Jesus entered from the east on a donkey with his *talmidim*. The notes set the two side by side: stallion and donkey, soldiers and disciples, fear and favor, chaos and order, empire and *shalom*.[^1] Which contrast in that list strikes you most, and why?

*Read Zechariah 9:9–10 aloud, then read Matthew 21:1–11 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The episode says the crowds immediately recognized Zechariah 9:9 when Jesus mounted a donkey, but "failed to read the next verse," which describes a king who takes away chariots and warhorses and proclaims peace to the nations.[^2] What do you notice when you hear verse 10 alongside verse 9? Why do you think the crowd held onto one verse and not the other?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The crowds waved palm fronds and shouted "Hosanna" (*Hoshana*, "Lord save us") from Psalm 118, which was traditionally sung at Sukkot, not Passover. The notes say palm fronds were a symbol of the Zealot party, and that the crowd was "packing palm frond heat," ready for revolution. Jesus wept over them (Luke 19:41–42) because they wanted violence when he came to bring peace.[^3] The notes add the irony that churches reenact Palm Sunday with children waving the very thing that made Jesus cry. How does that land with you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Matthew mentions two donkeys, though Jesus could ride only one. The episode reads the riderless donkey as a rabbinic invitation: "Which kingdom do you want? Will you ride the donkey of peace with Jesus, or do you want the stallion of military power?"[^4] Marty says, "I think far too much of our American culture has us standing at the west side of Jerusalem, not the east." The notes are clear that this "doesn't mean rejecting gratitude for freedom or denying genuine blessings," but it does mean examining where our ultimate allegiance lies.[^5] People in our traditions may react to this differently, and that is worth talking about honestly. Where do you feel the pull of the stallion in your own life, whether in politics, work, church, or family?

</div>

### Tables, a Fig Tree, and a Mountain <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#tables-a-fig-tree-and-a-mountain}

*Jesus confronts a corrupt system and tells his disciples what faith can do against it.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 21:12–17 aloud, then read Isaiah 56:7 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes say the money changers and animal sellers were set up in the Court of the Gentiles, "the only space where non-Jews could worship," in a marketplace called the "Booths of Ananus." The priests raised the animals, set the prices, decided which sacrifices were acceptable, and profited from the whole system.[^6] Jesus quotes Isaiah 56:7 about a house of prayer "for all nations." What do you notice about who was being pushed out? What does that suggest about the target of Jesus' anger?

*Read Matthew 21:18–22 aloud, then read Proverbs 27:18 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The episode explains that in Jewish literature olive trees represent the people of Israel, while fig trees represent spiritual leaders and rabbis, drawing on Proverbs 27:18. Sitting under a rabbi was like eating sweet fruit from a fig tree. So the fruitless tree symbolizes "corrupt spiritual leadership," particularly the priesthood, and the notes stress that this is "not Israel as a whole."[^7] Why does it matter whether we read the fig tree as Israel or as corrupt leadership? How might the first reading have shaped Christian attitudes toward Jewish people?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> From the Mount of Olives, Herodium would have been visible, the mountain-fortress Herod built by moving earth. The episode suggests Jesus pointed at it when he spoke of telling "this mountain" to throw itself into the sea: if Herod's wealth can move mountains, a little faith can take down a corrupt system.[^8] What system or situation in your life feels too entrenched to move? What would a mustard seed of faith look like there?

</div>

### By What Authority? <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#by-what-authority}

*Jesus answers a trap with a question about John.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 21:23–27 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The leaders ask by what authority, or *s'micha* (rabbinic ordination), Jesus acts. The notes explain that *s'micha* came through the traditional school system, where rabbis laid hands on students, and that Jesus had not come that way. His question about John's baptism "reveals that Jesus considered John the Baptist his rabbi, the source of his authority," and it traps the leaders between the crowd and their own unbelief.[^9] What do you notice about how Jesus responds to a question asked in bad faith?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Jesus' authority did not come from the credentials his questioners recognized. Think about whose voice has shaped your faith most. Where did that person's authority come from, and what does that suggest about the kind of authority you want to carry for others?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Two processions entered Jerusalem that week. Pilate came from the west with soldiers and a stallion, and Jesus came from the east on a donkey, fulfilling a prophecy about a king who breaks the weapons of war. The crowds waved palms for revolution, and Jesus wept. He cleared a marketplace out of the only space where outsiders could pray, cursed a fruitless tree that stood for corrupt leadership, and pointed to John as the source of his authority. Matthew leaves a second donkey without a rider, and the episode hears it asking each of us which kingdom we really want.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one way you could stand on the east side of Jerusalem this week?

</div>
