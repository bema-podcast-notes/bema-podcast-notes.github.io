### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We wrestled honestly with the conquest stories in Joshua, noticed that the book lingers on rescue stories like Rahab and the Gibeonites, and heard God, Joshua and the people tell each other to be strong and courageous.

**This week:** The episode steps back to a 10,000-foot view and asks why God worked so hard to give Israel this particular piece of land.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This episode wraps up Joshua by looking at geography instead of battles. The land of Canaan was mostly desert and rock, but it sat at the crossroads of the ancient world, the only land bridge between Africa, Asia and Europe, with the Via Maris highway running through it.[^1] If Israel was to be a kingdom of priests and bless all nations, they needed to be where the nations passed by.[^2]

The episode then reads the land itself as a teacher. The *shephelah*, the hill country between Israel's mountains and the coastal plain, becomes a picture of engaging culture rather than retreating from it.[^3] Ancient cities on their mounds, with gates where provision was handed out to the poor, give fresh weight to Jesus's words about a city on a hill.[^4] The episode closes with the tribe of Dan, who found their assignment next to the Philistines too hard and moved north, with serious consequences.[^5]

### Crossroads and Shephelah <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#crossroads-and-shephelah}

*God put his people in the busiest place in the world, on purpose.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 12:1–3 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes say the land was "the most valuable real estate in the ancient world" not because of its resources or beauty but because of its location, and they call it "the turnstile of the ancient world."[^6] They draw a parallel: "Just as the tabernacle was positioned in the center of the Israelite camp where all tribes could access it, Israel as a nation was positioned in the center of the civilized world where all nations could access them."[^7] Look at the promise in verse 3. What does the location of the land have to do with that promise?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes describe the land in zones, like a BLT sandwich (an image from teacher Aaron Couch): the coastal plain where Philistines and Phoenicians lived, the Judah mountains where Israel wanted to settle, and between them the *shephelah*.[^8] The word *shephelah* "literally means 'to bow down' or 'kneel'" and describes how the mountains bow down to meet the coastal plain.[^9] The notes read it as "the intersection point between two different cultures" and add that "Living in the shephelah means not retreating to safety but engaging the mission."[^10] What do you think made the mountains so attractive to Israel, and what would it have cost them to live in the hill country instead?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes apply this to "Christian subculture tendencies to create separate music, schools, language, entertainment, etc." They are careful to say "these things aren't inherently wrong," while warning that "the danger is disengagement from the mission."[^11] Our traditions have built different kinds of Christian institutions for good reasons. Without judging any of them, how can you tell when something is helping people engage and when it is helping them hide?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes name two dangers: staying in the mountains (the "holy huddle") and losing your identity on the coastal plain.[^12] Which way do you naturally lean, and what is one place in your life that functions as your *shephelah*?

</div>

### A City on a Hill <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#a-city-on-a-hill}

*Ancient cities sat on hills of their own history, and their gates were where hope was handed out.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 5:13–16 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes explain that a tel is a mound built up by city after city on the same site, so every tel "was a literal city on a hill, visible from miles around."[^13] The wealthy lived in the center, the middle class lived in apartments built into the walls (Rahab's house in Joshua 2:15 was one), the poor lived outside the walls in unprotected villages, and the most destitute lived near the burning trash dump.[^14] Picture yourself as someone living outside the walls, looking up at that city. What would you feel, and what would you hope for?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes say the city gate was where "The wealthy from inside the city would bring their abundance to the gates, where it was rationed and given to those in need from outside the walls."[^15] So when Jesus says "you are the light of the world, a city on a hill," the notes hear him "saying they are to be the hope of the earth."[^16] Read verses 14–16 again with the gate in mind. What changes in how you hear "let your light shine"?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes connect the trash dump, with its smoldering fire and "constant weeping and gnashing of teeth," to the imagery Jesus uses for *gehenna*.[^17] Our traditions hold a range of views about final judgment. Setting those debates aside, what does it add to know that this image came from a real place where the poorest people lived?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes say that "The watching world (those 'outside the walls') should see the church as a source of hope, generosity, and shalom rather than judgment or irrelevance."[^18] If someone outside the walls of your church described it honestly, what do you think they would say, and what is one thing you could do to make your own "gate" a place of provision?

</div>

### Dan's Allotment <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#dans-allotment}

*Every tribe received land and a mission, and one tribe walked away from both.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Judges 18:1 and Judges 18:27–29 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** If you looked at a map before tonight, share what you saw. The notes describe Dan's original allotment as a small L-shaped territory northwest of Judah, "directly bordering the Philistines," with the mission to "bring shalom to that chaotic pagan culture" and Judah, the largest tribe, right beside them for support.[^19] Instead, Dan moved to the far north. What do you notice in these verses about what Dan was looking for and what they did to get it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes list the consequences: loss of protection, loss of community, loss of identity, and absence from the list of tribes in Revelation 7, with the sobering implication that "abandoning God's mission can result in losing your part in the story altogether."[^20] They conclude: "The mission wasn't given to make Dan's life hard - it was their purpose and calling, and abandoning it proved far more dangerous than fulfilling it would have been."[^21] Why might running from a hard calling be more dangerous than staying in it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q11:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes point out that Samson came from Dan, and that when Samson was in trouble with the Philistines, Judah appeared in the story.[^22] Who is your "Judah," the people God has placed beside you for support, and how often do you lean on them?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q12:** The notes suggest each person, family and church has something like a tribal allotment, and that "The challenging aspects of your calling (like Dan facing the Philistines) aren't reasons to abandon mission but the very reason you're positioned where you are."[^23] What is the Philistine border in your own allotment right now, the hard part you are tempted to move away from?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> God did not hide his people in a remote valley. He placed them at the crossroads of the earth, where every trader and army had to pass, so that a kingdom of priests could bless all nations. He asked them to live in the shephelah, where the mountains bow down to the plain, and to be like cities on hills whose gates hand out provision to those outside the walls. Jesus picked up the same picture when he called his followers the light of the world. Dan shows the other path: a hard assignment, a strong neighbor for support, and a choice to leave for somewhere easier that turned out far more dangerous.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: where is your crossroads this week, the place God has put you where others pass by?

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 36 — The Redemption Cycle](/guides/36)** (32:36): The book of Judges. The episode challenges the familiar "sin cycle" reading of Judges and reframes it as a "redemption cycle" that puts the spotlight on God's patience with a people learning to follow him. It begins with a generation that forgot the story and asks what kind of failure God endures and what kind he must confront.

**What to bring next week:** Read Judges 2:10–19 and write down the steps of the repeating pattern in your own words. Next to each step, note who is doing the action: Israel or God.
