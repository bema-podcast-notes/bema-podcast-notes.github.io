### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We watched Paul's ministry grow from broadcasting the gospel widely to making about twelve committed disciples, and we asked whether we are making *mathetes* (students) or *talmidim* (life-long followers who imitate their rabbi).[^1]

**This week:** We return to the Damascus road and ask what Paul himself thought happened there: a change of religion, or a prophet's call.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This short episode settles a question first raised back in Episode 138. Was Paul's experience on the Damascus road a "conversion" from one religion to another, or something else? Marty argues that Paul understood it as a prophetic calling, the kind Jonah, Ezekiel, Jeremiah and Isaiah received, and that Paul stayed Jewish while his understanding of God was corrected.[^2]

The evidence comes from comparing two tellings of the same event. When Paul retells the story to King Agrippa in Acts 26, he adds details (the goads, a light brighter than the sun, the command to "stand on your feet") that echo the call stories of the Hebrew prophets. The episode reads these additions as intentional signals, a Jewish way of reading called *remez* ("hint"), rather than slips of memory.[^3] Because this guide leans on several short passages, there is extra time for reading them aloud.

### Two Tellings of One Story <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#two-tellings-of-one-story}

*We compare Luke's account of the Damascus road with the version Paul tells years later.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Acts 9:1–9 aloud, then read Acts 26:12–18 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The episode's argument starts with a simple comparison: Paul's Acts 26 testimony contains details that are not in the Acts 9 account.[^4] As a group, list what you hear in Acts 26 that you did not hear in Acts 9. Which additions stand out most to you, and why?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes say Paul "deliberately embellished details in his Acts 26 testimony to King Agrippa for theological purposes, not due to faulty memory."[^5] When you have noticed two biblical accounts of the same event that differ, what has your instinct usually been? What would it look like to ask first, "What is the writer trying to tell me by telling it this way?"

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> According to the notes, the apostles believed that embedding God's words inside their own speech carried power "beyond human reasoning," even when listeners did not consciously catch the allusions.[^6] Why might Paul tell his own story in the language of Scripture before a king? Where in your own life do you reach for borrowed words (a verse, a hymn, a line from a prayer) to describe what has happened to you, and what do those words carry that your own words cannot?

</div>

### Standing in the Prophets' Line <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#standing-in-the-prophets-line}

*We read the call stories Paul seems to be echoing and look for the pattern.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Ezekiel 1:28–2:3, Jeremiah 1:4–8, and Galatians 1:15–16 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes list several elements that recur in Old Testament call stories: a divine appearance with brilliant light or fire, the command to "stand on your feet," a mission to a rebellious or stubborn people, a promise of protection, a calling "from the womb," and a mission to the nations (Isaiah 49:6).[^7] Which of these do you hear in Ezekiel and Jeremiah? Which do you hear in Paul's own words, in Acts 26 and in Galatians 1?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode also connects Paul to Jonah. Jonah resisted his call (he "kicked against the goads," in the notes' phrase) and spent three days in darkness inside a fish; Paul hears about kicking against the goads and spends three days blind until something like scales falls from his eyes.[^8] What do you make of Paul echoing the most reluctant prophet in the Bible? What does it suggest about how Paul saw his own past?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** In the notes, Paul sees himself as continuing the tradition of Hebrew prophets who were called "to bring light to the Gentiles and confront God's people."[^9] Israel's prophets spoke as insiders who belonged to the people they challenged. What is the difference between challenging your own community as someone who loves it and attacking it from the outside? Where might you be called to speak that way in your own family, church or workplace?

</div>

### Calling, Not Conversion <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#calling-not-conversion}

*We consider why the word we use for Paul's experience matters.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes argue that calling Paul's experience a "conversion" from one religion to another feeds replacement theology, while seeing it as a prophetic calling keeps the continuity between Judaism and the early church in view.[^10] How does the picture change when Paul is a Jew whose understanding is corrected rather than a man who leaves Judaism behind? What might a Jewish neighbor hear differently in each version of the story?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Our traditions use different words for the beginning of a life with God: being saved, being baptized, being confirmed, being called, coming to faith. In your tradition, what language is used, and what does the idea of a "calling" add to it or press on it? Where do you sense God still correcting your understanding rather than replacing who you are?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> On the Damascus road, Paul did not trade one God for another. He met the God of Israel more truly and was sent, like Ezekiel, Jeremiah, Isaiah and even Jonah, to carry light to the nations and speak honestly to his own people. When he later told the story, he told it in Scripture's words so that those with ears to hear would recognize a prophet's call. The question for us is not only what happened to Paul, but what kind of story we are living inside when God interrupts us.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one thing you believed that God has corrected rather than replaced?

</div>
