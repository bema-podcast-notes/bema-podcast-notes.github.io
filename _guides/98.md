### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** The *hupocritos* turned out to be an actor, someone doing the right things for an audience. At the center of the Lord's Prayer, and perhaps of the whole sermon, Jesus added one line that invites us into the work of forgiveness.

**This week:** A single Hebrew idiom about the eye ties together a run of teachings that often feel disconnected: treasure, worry, judging, pearls, and asking, seeking, and knocking.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

When Jesus says "the eye is the lamp of the body," the episode argues he is not talking about eyesight. He is using a rabbinic idiom: a person had either a "good eye" (*ayin tovah*), seeing the world generously and with trust in God's abundance, or a "bad eye" (*ayin ra'ah*), seeing it through scarcity and fear. The hosts trace a chain from what we treasure, to how we see, to whether we worry, to whether we are generous or judgmental.

The episode makes a striking claim: we judge people because we worry. It separates the judging Jesus forbids (deciding the worth of a person) from discernment, which is a necessary task. It also reads "dogs and pigs" as a Jewish figure of speech for Gentiles and warns against holding people to a covenant they never agreed to. That last point touches parenting, friendships, and public life, so expect some honest disagreement.

### A Good Eye, a Bad Eye <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#a-good-eye-a-bad-eye}

*An idiom about the eye turns out to be about generosity, trust, and where the story starts.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 6:19–34 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes describe *ayin tovah* (good eye) as seeing possibilities and goodness and being generous, and *ayin ra'ah* (bad eye) as seeing scarcity and fear, connected to "stinginess and hoarding." The notes add that the NIV footnotes capture this: "healthy" eyes imply generosity, while "unhealthy" eyes imply stinginess.[^1] As you listened to the reading, where did you hear the language of generosity and stinginess underneath the language of eyes, light, and darkness?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The episode traces a chain: treasure determines where your heart is, your heart shapes your eye, your eye fills you with light or darkness, and that determines your master, God or money.[^2] Follow the chain through the passage. Which link in it feels most true to your experience? Which one feels like a stretch?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** According to the notes, a good eye is "rooted in trusting God's story from Genesis 1 (that the world is fundamentally good)," while a bad eye is "rooted in starting the story at Genesis 3 (the fall)."[^3] Many of us were taught to begin the gospel story with sin. How might starting the story at Genesis 1 or at Genesis 3 shape the way a person, a church, or a tradition sees the world around it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q4:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode calls worry the primary obstacle to generosity: worry assumes "there won't be enough" and reasons "if I give, I'll lack." Brent says he identifies with the Herodians here, who prioritized security, wealth, and comfort, and the notes observe that "many modern Christians are functionally Herodian despite different theological commitments."[^4] Where do you notice worry about having enough quietly shaping your generosity with time, money, or attention?

</div>

### Why We Judge <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#why-we-judge}

*Not all judgment is the same, and the kind Jesus forbids may grow out of worry.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 7:1–5 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes list three kinds of judgment: civil judgment by courts; discernment between right and wrong, holy and common; and value judgments about the worth of people. On discernment the notes quote the rabbis: "In the beginning, God separated light from darkness and that is forever our task as well." Only the third kind is what Jesus forbids: "Only God can judge the value of a person."[^5] Why do you think these three kinds of judgment get blurred so easily? What happens in a community when they do?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The hosts propose that "we judge people because we worry." We don't forgive because "we worry offenders won't get what they deserve," and we fear that forgiving means what they did was "okay."[^6] Think back to last week's forgiveness line in the Lord's Prayer. How does this connection between worry and judgment help explain why forgiveness is so hard?

*Read Matthew 7:7–12 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The episode reads "ask, seek, knock" as part of the same thread, not a standalone promise: when we are worried that someone won't get their comeuppance, we give that to God and trust that our Father knows how to give good gifts.[^7] The original notes put it this way: "Maybe it's about praying for what God wants to do in our lives rather than praying for what I want God to do in my life."[^8] What changes when you read these verses right after the teaching on judging? What would it look like this week to hand God one of your worries that someone will not get what they deserve?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The hosts say both sides of today's political spectrum are "bathed in worry," each fearing what the other will do, and that when we set worry aside we discover others are "more human than we realized."[^9] Without debating politics, where have you seen worry about "the other side" turn into judgments about people's worth? Where have you seen that change?

</div>

### Pearls and Covenants <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#pearls-and-covenants}

*A puzzling saying about dogs, pigs, and pearls becomes a question about whose standards we hold others to.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 7:6 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes explain that "dogs" and "pigs" were Jewish figures of speech for Gentiles, and that "pearls" in rabbinic thought refers to teachings, wisdom, and Torah. The warning, as the episode hears it: "We can't impose Torah/biblical morality on those who never signed up for that covenant." Instead, "we should let our good deeds shine, bringing shalom to chaos."[^10] Our traditions have different instincts about how Christians should engage the wider culture. What does your tradition tend to emphasize, and how does this reading confirm or challenge it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode applies this to parents of adolescent and college-age children, who often react against religious platitudes by "pushing harder in the opposite direction," and to students asking about friends: the key question is "Are they Jesus followers?"[^11] Think of someone close to you who does not share your faith. What would it look like to stop throwing pearls and start building openness through relationship?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Jesus' teachings on treasure, worry, judging, pearls, and prayer are not a pile of separate sayings. They are one thread. What we treasure shapes how we see; a bad eye sees scarcity, scarcity breeds worry, and worry makes us stingy and quick to judge the worth of others. A good eye starts the story in Genesis 1 and trusts the God who feeds birds and clothes flowers. The notes sum it up: "Trusting this story produces ayin tovah, which produces generosity, which reduces worry and judgment."[^12]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each: where is one place this week you want to practice seeing with a good eye?

</div>
