### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Wesley called people to practical holiness, Protestant freedom splintered the church, and after the French Revolution Western culture would no longer "blink Christian" by default.[^1]

**This week:** American Christianity responds to its new culture of freedom with revival, restoration movements, social concern and, finally, a culture war.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This episode is close to home for our group. It begins with the Second Great Awakening, whose camp meetings (the first widely recognized one in Kentucky in 1801) drew denominations together in search of revival and the Holy Spirit. It then describes the clash between European "colonial denominationalism," with its creedal requirements, and America's culture of freedom and independence. Out of that clash came several movements, among them the Stone-Campbell Restoration Movement, which sought to restore the faith of the early church in Acts. Marty, who was trained and pastored in that movement, says of it: "I often hate its application, but I love its ideals that it stood for."[^3]

The episode also covers the Social Gospel of William Booth, a Methodist pastor who founded the Salvation Army; the challenges of Darwin and Marx; the Scopes Trial of 1925 and the rise of fundamentalism; and the Scofield Bible, which Marty bluntly says "wrecked Christian theology in the modern era." The summary reminds us that all people bear the image of God and that categorical thinking often obscures truth. Several of us belong to the traditions named here, so this guide asks us to look at our own histories with honesty and affection, diagnosing rather than condemning.[^4]

### Camp Meetings and a Plant in the Wrong Climate <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#camp-meetings-and-a-plant-in-the-wrong-climate}

*Revival and independence pulled American Christianity away from the patterns it had brought from Europe.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The Second Great Awakening's camp meetings were outdoor gatherings where denominations came together for fellowship, worship and preaching, with an emphasis on personal experience, confession, repentance and the Holy Spirit. The notes say it answered the question, "Where's the art? Where's the beauty? Where's the Holy Spirit?" in a Christianity that had become overly focused on systems and academic debate. Brent describes attending a Baptist camp meeting recently, where the same practices continue.[^5] What experiences of revival, camp, retreat or mountaintop worship have you had? What were they giving you that ordinary church life was not?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Immigrants brought their national churches with them: German Lutherans, Dutch Reformed, Scottish Presbyterians and others, often requiring members to affirm confessions like the Westminster Confession before receiving communion. Marty compares this to "trying to plant some kind of plant that wasn't going to grow in that climate." Brent adds: "If you're in England and you don't like what the Anglican Church is doing, where are you going to go? If you're in America and you don't like what whoever is doing, you just shift over to the west a little bit. There's plenty of room."[^6] How has that freedom to "shift over" shaped the way people choose, change and leave churches today, including in your own story?

</div>

### Restoring the Church in Acts <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#restoring-the-church-in-acts}

*The Stone-Campbell movement began with a refusal at the communion table and a hope to recover the church of Acts.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Acts 2:42–47 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes trace the Stone-Campbell movement's origin to Barton Stone, a Presbyterian minister who was appalled when asked to sign the Westminster Confession before receiving the Eucharist. Its core conviction was to restore the faith experience of the early church in Acts rather than perpetuate denominational traditions, and it rejected creedal requirements for communion and fellowship.[^7] As you listen to this description of the early community, what do they actually do together? What do you notice is present in the passage, and what is absent that later churches added?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The movement first succeeded in uniting believers from different denominational backgrounds, as Stone's work and Thomas Campbell's parallel work came together. Later, the notes say, it "lost this beauty and splintered itself." Marty, who pastored in this movement, puts it this way: "I often hate its application, but I love its ideals that it stood for."[^8] Whether or not you come from this movement, which of its ideals do you most value? Where have you seen a gap open between a tradition's ideals and its application, in your own tradition as well as others, and what do you think causes that gap?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes group several movements that arose in this era of new American freedom: the Restoration Movement, the Latter-day Saints, the Seventh-day Adventists, the Jehovah's Witnesses and others. All of them capitalized on "a newfound culture of freedom and independence, to start a new revived expression of a faith that they felt was corrupted and dying." Marty's own family includes Reformed, Restoration, Jewish and Latter-day Saint branches, and the summary warns that categorical thinking (cult versus orthodox) often obscures rather than illuminates truth.[^9] What starting impulse do these movements share, and how might recognizing it change the way we talk with people from traditions very different from our own?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Barton Stone's turning point came at the communion table, when a creed stood between him and the Eucharist.[^10] Who might feel that something stands between them and the table in our churches today? What is one practical way you could help widen the welcome in your own congregation?

</div>

### Social Gospel and Culture War <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#social-gospel-and-culture-war}

*The church tried to recover care for the poor and then turned its energy toward fighting secular ideas.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> William Booth championed care for the poor and marginalized as industrialization widened the gap between rich and poor, drawing on biblical themes of the alien, orphan and widow and the *mamzer* (a Hebrew term the BEMA notes use for the marginalized outsider). The notes acknowledge that the "social gospel" carries negative connotations in some conservative circles, yet Marty affirms: "There is very much a social component to the Gospel from Genesis to Revelation."[^11] Why do you think care for the poor became a contested label, and what is at stake in recovering it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** After World War I, Marty observes, "War always seems to get in the way, every time." American Christianity turned toward fighting secular ideals, and the Scopes Trial in Dayton, Tennessee, in 1925 became a flashpoint for fundamentalism and a culture-war mentality. The notes compare this to the church's reaction to Copernicus and Galileo, potentially misidentifying the enemy and the truth to be preserved, and say that splintering made it harder for Christianity to learn from its mistakes.[^12] Where are you tempted to see an enemy where there might instead be a neighbor or an honest question? What would it look like this week to treat that person or question differently?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Between 1800 and 1925, American Christianity tried again and again to recover something it felt it had lost. Camp meetings went looking for the Spirit, beauty and art; Barton Stone and Thomas Campbell went looking for the church of Acts and a table without creedal walls; William Booth went looking for the poor. Each recovery carried real treasure, and each also carried the seeds of new division. By 1925 much of the church had turned to fighting a culture war, perhaps against the wrong enemy. Marty's invitation is to love the ideals of those who came before us, learn from their mistakes, and remember that every person we meet bears the image of God.[^13]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, name one ideal from your own tradition that you want to live out more faithfully.

</div>
