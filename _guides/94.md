### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Jesus moved into Herod's backyard, called fishermen who had been sent home from school, and opened the Sermon on the Mount by announcing God's favor on people the world considers unblessed.

**This week:** Jesus turns from "those" to "you" and tells his disciples who they are: the salt of the earth, the light of the world, a city on a hill.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode begins with a small grammatical shift. Through most of the Beatitudes Jesus speaks of "those" who are blessed, but in verse 11 he turns to his disciples and says "you," warning them that announcing this kingdom will bring opposition.[^1] He then calls them salt and light, images the episode unpacks through the ancient city on a hill: a mound built up over generations, with the wealthy inside the walls, the poor in unprotected villages outside, and the city gate serving as courthouse, town hall and place where provision was handed out to those in need.[^2]

The episode returns to a theme from Session 2: God places his people at the crossroads of the earth to bring shalom into chaos.[^3] It also explains *Gehenna*, the word often translated "hell," as the Valley of Hinnom outside Jerusalem, a smoldering trash dump where the poorest people lived.[^4] Its central claim is that Jesus plans to be the hope of the world through his followers.[^5]

### From Those to You <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#from-those-to-you}

*The blessing turns personal, and so does the warning.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 5:3–12 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes point out that Jesus uses third-person language through the Beatitudes and then, in verse 11, makes "a dramatic shift to second person."[^6] Listen for exactly where the shift happens. What changes in the tone of the passage at that point, and what is Jesus telling his disciples about their own future?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe Matthew as "a former tax collector and thus a 'mamzer' (outsider) himself," and say his Gospel aims to show that those considered outsiders "are actually insiders in God's Kingdom."[^7] They also connect Jesus's move to Capernaum with Isaiah 9 and the Gentiles, saying "Matthew wants his Jewish audience to understand that their mission has always included the nations, even though they've forgotten this calling."[^8] Read Isaiah 9:1–2 aloud. How does Matthew's own story shape the way he tells this part of Jesus's teaching?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes say "The persecution isn't theoretical - it's a promise," and that disciples who announce God's favor on outsiders "will face opposition from those who believe Kingdom looks different."[^9] They suggest an honest self-check: if you never face resistance for your faith, "examine whether you're truly proclaiming Kingdom values or just conforming to cultural Christianity."[^10] What kind of discomfort, if any, has following Jesus actually brought you, and what does that suggest?

</div>

### Salt, Light, and a City on a Hill <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#salt-light-and-a-city-on-a-hill}

*For the poor living outside the walls, the city on the hill was the only hope in sight.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 5:13–16 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes explain that salt "preserved food, added flavor, and was valuable enough to be used as currency," and that at night a city on a hill "would be visible for miles, its lamps creating a beacon of hope for travelers seeking shelter, protection, and rest."[^11] Look at what Jesus says about salt, a lamp and a city. What is each one for, and what happens when it stops doing its job?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes describe the layers of an ancient city: the wealthy inside the walls, the middle class in apartments built into the walls, the poor in "daughter villages" outside, and the destitute at the dump.[^12] The city gates "functioned as the courthouse, town hall, and welfare distribution center," the place where "the abundant resources of the wealthy were distributed to those in need."[^13] Read verse 16 again with the gate in mind. What do you hear differently in "let your light shine"?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say good deeds "aren't for self-glorification but to reveal what God is like," and suggest asking before acting: "Will this reveal what God is like? Will this cause people to glorify the Father in heaven?"[^14] How can you tell the difference between doing good so that God is seen and doing good so that you are seen?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes say "Today's church should function like ancient city gates," and they offer two audit questions: "Are we distributing abundance to those in need, or hoarding it? Are we known in our communities as sources of hope, or are we invisible?"[^15] How would you honestly answer those questions for your own household, and for your church?

</div>

### Outside the Walls <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#outside-the-walls}

*The word we translate "hell" pointed to a real place just outside the city.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 5:29–30 aloud, noticing the word translated "hell."*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes explain that *Gehenna* comes from the Hebrew *Gei Hinnom*, the Valley of Hinnom outside Jerusalem, where the city's trash and sewage were dumped and burned.[^16] The fire smoldered continuously, and the place was associated with "weeping and gnashing of teeth" because "the absolute poorest were forced to live there, scavenging for survival."[^17] When you hear Jesus use this word with that place in mind, what picture comes to you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say Jesus's audience "wouldn't envision the medieval concept of underground eternal torment," and that Jesus "often warns the religious elite that they might 'find themselves at Gehenna'" despite assuming they were safely inside.[^18] Our traditions teach about final judgment in different ways, and this question is not meant to settle that. What does this first-century background add to how you read Jesus's warnings, and what questions does it raise for you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes suggest that "salvation might be better understood as being welcomed inside, participating in God's abundant provision and protection," and encourage us to "Focus less on avoiding hell as fire insurance and more on entering the Kingdom."[^19] Who lives "outside the walls" in your community, and what is one concrete way you could open a gate to them this month?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> After announcing God's favor on the unblessed, Jesus looks at his disciples and says "you." You will be opposed, and you are the salt of the earth and the light of the world. In Jesus's day a city on a hill was where the poor outside the walls looked for hope, and its gate was where abundance was handed out to people in need. God has always placed his people at the crossroads of the earth for exactly this purpose. Jesus does not plan to be the hope of the world without us; he plans to be the hope of the world through us.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: who is one person who might find a little hope through your "gate" this week?

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 95 — Abolish or Fulfill](/guides/95)** (37:55): The episode tackles one of the most misunderstood lines in the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus's claim that he came not to abolish the Law and the Prophets but to fulfill them. It explains what "fulfill" and "abolish" meant in the rabbinic world and introduces the idea of teaching authority, which sets up Jesus's "You have heard it said, but I say to you."

**What to bring next week:** Read Matthew 5:17–20 and Matthew 7:28–29. Write down, in a sentence or two, what you were taught that "Jesus fulfilled the law" means.
