### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** In blue-collar Thyatira, guild feasts and a prophetess John called "Jezebel" pressed believers toward compromise. The episode showed John writing in two layers at once and gave us two questions for every passage: "What's the culture?" and "What's the Text?"

**This week:** Sardis had a reputation for being alive, a mountain full of graves, and a history of falling to its enemies because its guards fell asleep. Jesus tells this church to wake up.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The notes describe Sardis as a city of two mountains, an acropolis and a necropolis ("city of the dead"), reflecting a reputation for life alongside a fascination with death.[^1] It fell twice, to the Persians and centuries later to the Seleucids, because its guards fell asleep, which makes "wake up" and "I will come like a thief" pointed reminders of the city's history.[^2] A catastrophic earthquake in AD 60 split the acropolis into thirds, and the notes connect it to earthquake imagery later in Revelation.[^3]

The episode also explores the largest synagogue found in the ancient world, built inside the city's gymnasium, and asks whether it shows missional engagement or compromise.[^4] Finally, it describes the festival of the goddess Cybele, in which worshipers in white robes sought to stain them with blood, a practice that makes the promise to those who have not soiled their clothes "extraordinarily powerful."[^5] Some of this background is graphic; this guide refers to it briefly and focuses on what the letter offers.

### The City That Fell Asleep <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-city-that-fell-asleep}

*Sardis believed itself impenetrable, and its history said otherwise.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Revelation 3:1–3 aloud, then Obadiah 1–4.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes say John uses a "text-to-context" approach, applying the Hebrew Scriptures to the Greco-Roman world. For Sardis he draws on Obadiah, the only Old Testament book besides Revelation to mention Sardis (as Sepharad), along with Isaiah and Hosea.[^6] Read Obadiah 1–4 and then Revelation 3:1–3. What images or ideas in Obadiah seem to fit a proud city on a height?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes tell the story twice. When Cyrus besieged Sardis, a Persian soldier watched a Lydian guard fall asleep and drop his helmet, then saw him retrieve it through a secret passage; the Persians entered at night and "found the entire city asleep."[^7] Four hundred years later a Seleucid soldier noticed vultures perching on an unguarded section of wall, and again the city was taken at night.[^8] The notes call "wake up" "a devastating cultural burn," not "metaphorical advice for generic spiritual alertness."[^9] How would you have heard "Wake up!" and "I will come like a thief" if you had grown up in Sardis?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Jesus tells Sardis that it has a reputation for being alive but is dead, and the notes point to the city's two mountains, one for the living and one for the dead, and suggest further study of "reputation versus reality" in spiritual communities.[^10] What makes the gap between reputation and reality so easy for a church, or a person, to miss?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes apply the warning this way: "What are the specific areas where our communities have historically 'fallen asleep'?" and add that "The call to wake up is always contextual and specific, not generic."[^11] Where in your own life do you sense the same kind of blind spot showing up again and again, and what would "strengthen what remains" mean there?

</div>

### Engaged but Not Absorbed <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#engaged-but-not-absorbed}

*Sardis raises a question every faithful community faces: how to live inside a culture without being swallowed by it.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Revelation 6:12–17 and Revelation 16:18–19 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes list earthquakes in Sardis in AD 17, 19, 21, 24 and 29, and a catastrophic one in AD 60 that broke the acropolis into thirds: one third fell backward, one third fell forward and buried over 300 acres, and one third stood.[^12] Look at the two Revelation passages you just read. What details sound different when you picture people who had lived through that earthquake? The notes say these passages were "immediate, culturally relevant allusions to recent traumatic history."[^13]

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes report that the first Lydian king was named Gugu, whom the Hebrews called "Gog," followed by a king the Hebrews called "Magog," and conclude that these names "actually refer backward to historical rulers of Sardis, not forward to future events."[^14] They add that this "doesn't preclude secondary application to future events."[^15] The traditions in this room read prophecy about the end in different ways. Without trying to settle which reading is right, how do you hold a text's first meaning and any later meaning together?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes describe the synagogue in Sardis, the largest found in the ancient world, built inside the city's gymnasium (its university). Evidence for faithfulness includes no animal images on the floor, an ornate Torah closet and Moses seat, and a *mikvah* (ritual bath) fountain that doubled as a free public fountain. Evidence for possible compromise includes lions sacred to Cybele at the entrance, Roman eagles on the Torah reading table, and its location inside the gymnasium.[^16] The Harvard team, the notes say, sees missional living, a community trying to be "a light to the Gentiles" while keeping its core identity.[^17] Weighing that evidence, where would you draw the line between engaging a culture and being absorbed by it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes connect Sardis to the *shephelah* ("lowlands") tension discussed earlier in BEMA, which the notes describe as "living at the intersection of chaos and shalom without compromise." Their answer "isn't withdrawal but intentional presence with clear boundaries."[^18] What is one place in your life, a workplace, school, neighborhood or institution, where you are called to that kind of presence, and what boundaries help you stay yourself there?

</div>

### Robes Kept White <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#robes-kept-white}

*The letter closes with a promise that answered the most disturbing festival in the city.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Revelation 3:4–6 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes describe Cybele as the patron goddess of Sardis, whose annual forty-day festival drew more than a million visitors. Participants wore white robes, and priests taught that getting blood on those robes from devotees who mutilated themselves in her honor counted as one's own offering.[^19] Against that, the notes call the promise to those who "have not soiled their clothes" and will "walk with me, dressed in white" "a powerful counter-narrative."[^20] What does it mean to you that Jesus answers this practice with a promise rather than only a warning?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> In the ruins of Cybele's temple stands a small circular building from the third or fourth century, and the notes report a theory that it was a Christian medical clinic caring for people injured in Cybele worship. "Whether historically accurate or not," they say, "the image is powerful."[^21] The notes ask, "Where are the 'Cybele temples' in our communities - places of destruction and exploitation?"[^22] What would it look like for your church, or for you, to build a redemptive presence in a place like that while keeping your robes clean?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Sardis looked alive. It sat between a mountain of the living and a mountain of the dead, and twice in its history it fell because no one was awake to see the enemy coming. Its Jewish community built a synagogue inside the gymnasium, trying to serve the city without losing itself. Its streets filled every year with white robes deliberately stained in a goddess's honor. Into that city Jesus speaks a wake-up call and a promise: strengthen what remains, and those who have not soiled their clothes will walk with him dressed in white.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one thing you want to wake up to this week?

</div>
