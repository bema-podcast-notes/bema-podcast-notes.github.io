### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Micah pictured God as a judge who actively sets things right, reread Bethlehem as a sign of God working through the small, and summed up God's requirements as justice, mercy and humility[^1].

**This week:** The last of the pre-Assyrian prophets, First Isaiah, speaks to Judah about worship God will not accept and about a vineyard where every wall matters[^2].

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode introduces the idea that the book of Isaiah contains several prophetic voices from different periods, possibly carried forward by disciples trained in Isaiah's tradition, much as Elisha carried forward Elijah's[^3]. This week focuses on the first voice, which the notes place in Isaiah 1–12, during the reigns of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz and Hezekiah[^3]. Its focus is overwhelmingly on justice rather than idolatry: Judah's worship is technically correct, yet God rejects it because it sits alongside greed and neglect of the vulnerable[^4].

The central image is the terraced vineyard (*gan*, "terraced garden") of Isaiah 5. Each family tends its own terrace, but no one can see their own retaining wall from above; only the neighbor below can. If one wall fails, the terraces beneath it wash away[^5]. The episode uses this picture to challenge Western individualism, and it closes with a glimpse of hope in Isaiah 4[^6].

### Worship God Will Not Accept <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#worship-god-will-not-accept}

*Judah has the forms of worship right, and God still turns away.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 1:10–17 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes point out that Judah was bringing correct Levitical offerings, using the proper priesthood, keeping festivals, new moons and Sabbaths, and praying[^4]. As you listen, list what God says he is weary of, then list what he asks for instead. What do you notice when you set the two lists side by side?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes say the issue is not Judah's worship practices but their failure to seek justice, defend the oppressed and care for the fatherless and the widow[^4]. Why would correct worship fail to please God when it exists alongside injustice? How does this echo what we heard from Amos, Hosea and Micah?

*Read Ezekiel 16:49 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Isaiah calls Judah's leaders "rulers of Sodom." The episode, drawing on earlier BEMA teaching, says the sin of Sodom was not primarily sexual immorality but social injustice, and cites Ezekiel 16:49, which describes Sodom as "arrogant, overfed and unconcerned"[^7]. Our traditions may have taught Sodom's story with different emphases. Without deciding between them, what does reading Ezekiel's description alongside Isaiah 1 bring into focus, and why might Isaiah have chosen this comparison?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q4:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> "Unconcerned" is a quiet word for a serious failure. Where in your own life is it easiest to be unconcerned, and what is one need nearby that you could choose to notice this week?

</div>

### The Terraced Vineyard <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#the-terraced-vineyard}

*A hillside farm becomes a picture of how a community stands or falls together.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 5:1–7 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes list the owner's preparations: choosing a fertile hillside, clearing stones, planting *soreq* (the finest vines), building a watchtower and cutting a winepress. Yet the vineyard produced *betushim*, bad grapes that droop to the ground without care[^8]. Verse 7 contains a wordplay: God looked for *mishpat* (justice) and found bloodshed, for *tzedakah* (righteousness) and heard *tze'akah* (cries of distress)[^9]. What do you notice about where the failure lies, and what does the near-rhyme of the last pair suggest?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes describe how terraced vineyards worked. Each family inherited a terrace. Owners could not see their own retaining walls from above, but the neighbor below could, and one failing wall could wash out every terrace beneath it in a heavy rain[^5]. The episode showed photographs of a maintained hillside and, across the same valley, a ruined one that was only a pile of rocks[^10]. What does this picture teach about personal responsibility and communal health?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The episode tells of a modern neighborhood dispute over a neglected retaining wall and names the Western instinct to say "mind your own business"[^10]. The notes ask how communities can practice mutual accountability, "watching each other's walls," without becoming legalistic or invasive[^11]. What makes it hard for you to let someone see your wall, and what would make it safe?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Who in your life is positioned to see a wall you cannot see, and what might you invite them to tell you?

</div>

### A Glimpse of Hope <span class="guide-time">10 min</span> {#a-glimpse-of-hope}

*Even First Isaiah leaves a door open.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 4:2–6 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes say these promises (a cloud by day, fire by night, shelter and shade) recall the wilderness journey from Session 1, suggesting that exile will become a new desert season where God's presence is known again[^6]. What images in the passage stand out to you, and what do they suggest about the purpose of judgment?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> When has a hard season in your life turned out to be a place where you found God's presence in a new way?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Judah did everything right in the sanctuary and still heard God say he was hiding his eyes. The problem was not their liturgy but their neighbors: the poor, the widow and the orphan left uncared for. Isaiah's vineyard shows why it matters. No one can see their own wall, every terrace depends on the ones above it, and one neglected wall can wash out a whole hillside. God is still listening for righteousness, and the hope of Isaiah 4 is that he will shelter a people willing to be a vineyard again.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, whose wall will you help watch this week, or whom will you invite to watch yours?

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 52 — Jonah — Potential](/guides/52)** (51:09): We move from warnings before the Assyrian threat to a book written for a people living in its aftermath. Jonah is read as a deliberately backwards story about the "worst prophet," God's compassion for Israel's enemies, and the potential God sees in all people, which is why Jewish communities read it on Yom Kippur[^12]. It asks who we would rather God not spare.

**What to bring next week:** Read the book of Jonah in one sitting (four short chapters). Note anything that seems strange or backwards.
