### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Josh walked us through Psalm 23 with fresh ears, following the sheep all the way to God's table, where enemies are reconciled by a shared meal rather than by the sword.[^1]

**This week:** Elle Grover Fricks joins Brent and Reed for a slow reading of Psalm 8, a psalm of wonder at how small humans are and at the dignity and responsibility God gives them anyway.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The hosts work through Elle's own translation of Psalm 8. They ask what the heading "upon the *gittith*" means, why verse 2 seems to interrupt the flow, whether "angels" is the right word in verse 5, and what kind of "rule" verse 6 has in mind. Along the way they note that several familiar English wordings come from the Septuagint, the Greek Old Testament, rather than from the Hebrew.[^3]

One tension runs through the whole conversation: people are dust, and yet the universe was made for them; they are not essential to the cosmos, and yet God crowns them with glory and honor. The hosts argue that holding both truths forms humble, attentive, servant-hearted leadership over creation. Some of what the psalm offers cannot be reproduced in a classroom, they add, so read it outside, under the stars.[^4]

### Opening <span class="guide-time">10 min</span> {#opening}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-opening" markdown="1">

**Opening Question:** Elle compares the feeling of praise to standing in front of a huge mountain.[^5] Describe a moment when you felt very small in front of something vast: a night sky, a mountain, an ocean, a storm. If you read Psalm 8 outdoors this week, tell the group what you noticed.

</div>

### The View from the Night Sky <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#the-view-from-the-night-sky}

*Psalm 8 steps back from personal distress to reflect on the nature, purpose and destiny of humanity.*
{: .guide-note}

*If possible, dim the lights. Read Psalm 8 aloud slowly.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Elle describes the psalm as one long camera shot. It starts in the heavens with the moon and stars, pans down to humanity, and then moves to everything under human feet. Reed notes that most psalms are first-person reports of distress or deliverance, while this one is contemplative.[^6] Trace the camera's movement in the psalm you just heard. What do you notice about where it starts, where it pauses, and where it ends?

*Read Psalm 8:1 and 8:3–4 aloud, skipping verse 2. Then read verses 1–4 again with verse 2 included.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** Brent noticed that the psalm flows without verse 2, which raises the question of what verse 2 is doing there. The heading *al ha-gittit* may point to a winepress and a harvest festival, or to Gath, Goliath's city. Elle suggests the "infant" in verse 2 may be young David, the smallest person on the battlefield, through whom God laid a foundation of strength; afterward he looks up at the stars, asking *who even am I?* Reed offers a broader reading: God brings strength out of weakness.[^7] What does verse 2 add when you hear it in place? Which reading do you find most persuasive, and why?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Reed's small group once took about 20 young people to a dark field lit by tea lights, where for 45 minutes they simply sat under the stars while he read Psalm 8 every so often, with no commentary.[^8] When have you received something from Scripture without any explanation attached? What would it take to create that kind of space for yourself or someone else this month?

</div>

### Not Essential, and Yet <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#not-essential-and-yet}

*Being small is not the same as being worthless.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Reed quotes Abraham Joshua Heschel: "He who wants to enter the holiness of the day must learn to understand that the world has already been created and will survive without the help of man." His point is that being non-essential does not mean being insignificant. Elle adds that praise is not God acting like "a creepy narcissist"; people find their deepest meaning and delight when they remember how big God is and how small they are.[^9] Why might it be a relief, and not only a humbling, to hear that the world does not depend on you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The Hebrew of verse 5 says humanity was made a little lower than *Elohim*, a word that can mean God or divine beings but is not the usual word for angels. The rendering "angels" comes from the Septuagint, which Hebrews 2:7 later follows. "Glory and honor" are words Scripture usually uses for God himself, and Reed notes that humans rule over "the works of your hands," the same handiwork that made them feel so small.[^10] Many of us learned "a little lower than the angels" and may be attached to it. What changes for you if the comparison is to God rather than to angels? What might make translators hesitant to say it that way?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The rabbinic "two pockets" teaching, traditionally attributed to Rabbi Simcha Bunim of Peshischa, says to carry a note in each pocket. One reads *"I am but dust and ashes."* The other reads *"For my sake the world was created."* You have to hold both at once, which produces both healthy self-worth and healthy humility.[^11] Which pocket do you tend to reach into more often? What happens to you, or to people around you, when you live out of only one?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Elle mentions a finding that when you zoom out in your imagination from your room to your street, city, region and beyond, your sense of time slows. The notes suggest it as a practice: zoom out to the cosmos, then return to the psalm.[^12] Try it together now for two minutes in silence, then read Psalm 8:3–4 aloud once more. What did you notice, and where might you use this practice in an ordinary week?

</div>

### Ruling by Seeing <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#ruling-by-seeing}

*The psalm's kind of rule looks less like domination and more like attention and service.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 1:26 aloud. Then read Psalm 8:6–8 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Genesis 1:26 uses *radah*, which carries the image of treading down. Psalm 8:6 uses a different verb, *mashal*. Elle reads Psalm 8's ruling as creation being drawn into humanity's mission to bless all nations and love God and neighbor, "harnessed under our yoke" rather than crushed underfoot. The psalm could have said "all living things" and stopped; instead it names each group, which makes the reader see each creature before deciding how to rule it, the way God "comes down" to see humanity before acting.[^13] What do you notice about the list in verses 7–8? Why might seeing come before ruling?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** Elle describes her parenting: shouting "Stop wrestling in the living room!" rarely works, so she kneels, holds her three-year-old's hands, looks him in the eye, and asks what is going on. Reed notices that "ruling over my kids" feels different when he names each one. The episode connects this with Jesus in Mark 10:42–45: the Gentiles lord it over others, "not so with you," and whoever wants to be great must be a servant.[^14] Where do you hold some authority, at home, at work, at church, or over land or resources? What would ruling by seeing look like there this week?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say ruling the earth well rules out "pillaging" it, and that ruling over creation does not justify oppressing other people, who are also crowned with honor.[^15] What is one place, creature or resource you use regularly that you could stop and actually look at before deciding how to use it?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Psalm 8 asks us to step outside, look up, and feel how small we are. The world was finished before we arrived and will go on without us, and yet God is mindful of us and crowns us with glory and honor. Both truths are needed together: dust and ashes in one pocket, the world created for my sake in the other. That balance is what makes us fit to rule, and ruling in Psalm 8 looks like kneeling to look a child in the eye, naming each person, and seeing each creature before we act.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, say which note, "dust and ashes" or "for my sake the world was created," you most need to carry this week.

</div>

They talk about communal grief, memory after loss, the dignity of refusing to perform pain for those who caused it, and why praying our hate honestly may be a necessary first step toward real forgiveness.[^16] This session touches grief, abuse and violence, and sharing will always be optional.
