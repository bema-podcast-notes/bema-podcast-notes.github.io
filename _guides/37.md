### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We reread Judges as a redemption cycle, watched a generation forget the story, and heard that God has endless patience for people honestly trying to follow him and does not abandon his people even at their lowest.

**This week:** The book of Ruth zooms in on the same dark era and finds pockets of faithfulness: a Moabite widow and an Israelite landowner who actually live out God's commands.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Ruth opens "In the days when the judges ruled" and shows that not everyone in Israel had forgotten God's commands.[^1] The episode reads the book as more than a romance. It is a love story between God's people and their God, shown through obedience to the commands that protect the alien, the orphan and the widow.[^2] Ruth herself fits all three categories, and she comes from Moab, a nation Deuteronomy excluded from the assembly.[^3]

The episode walks through Naomi's bitterness and its two possible meanings, the gleaning laws and Boaz's generosity, the threshing floor scene read as a call to covenant obligation, and the kinsman-redeemer transaction at the city gate.[^4] It ends with the genealogy: Ruth becomes the great-grandmother of David, and by New Testament reckoning, part of Jesus's family line.[^5]

### Bitter and Loyal <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#bitter-and-loyal}

*The book opens with a famine, three funerals, and two very different responses to loss.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Ruth 1:1–22 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes say Elimelech's move to Moab during a famine can be read two ways. It may have been disobedience, trusting Moab's gods, or obedience, deliberately avoiding Egypt, the usual refuge in famine, because God had warned against going back.[^6] "The text leaves this ambiguous, allowing readers to wrestle with the complexity of decision-making during crisis."[^7] What details in verses 1–5 could support each reading?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** Naomi asks to be called *Mara*. The notes explain that the Hebrew root "can mean both 'bitter' and 'rebellious,'" so her words could express anger at God for her losses, or guilt and shame over a choice she now believes God is punishing.[^8] The notes say "This ambiguity makes Naomi's character relatable to anyone who has experienced both anger at God and self-blame during suffering."[^9] Listen again to verses 20–21. What do you hear in Naomi's voice?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Ruth's words in verses 16–17 show covenant faithfulness, self-sacrifice and lifelong commitment, even though Deuteronomy 23:3 excludes Moabites from the assembly of the Lord.[^10] The notes call this "a powerful irony: a pagan Moabite woman demonstrates greater faithfulness than many Israelites."[^11] Why do you think the Bible so often lets the outsider show the insiders what faithfulness looks like?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** In seasons of loss, some of us lean toward anger at God and some toward blaming ourselves. Without sharing more than you want to, which voice have you heard more in your own hard seasons, and what did you need from the people around you, the Ruths in your life?

</div>

### Gleaning in Boaz's Field <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#gleaning-in-boazs-field}

*Torah built care for the vulnerable into the harvest itself, and Boaz goes further.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Deuteronomy 24:19–22 aloud, then Ruth 2:1–17.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** If you did the preparation, share what you marked. The notes list five ways Boaz shows covenant faithfulness, including obeying the gleaning laws and telling his workers to deliberately drop extra grain for Ruth (Ruth 2:16).[^12] Compare what Deuteronomy requires with what Boaz actually does. Where does he meet the law, and where does he go past it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes point out that Ruth knew about Israel's gleaning practice even while she was in Moab, "suggesting Israel's obedience had created a visible witness."[^13] They also describe gleaning as "dignified participation in economic life" rather than charity.[^14] What is the difference between charity and dignified participation, and why might that difference matter to someone like Ruth?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes connect Ruth to Jesus's words, "If you love me, you will obey my commandments," and suggest examining "which of God's commands we might consider optional, particularly those regarding economic justice, care for marginalized populations, and inclusion of outsiders."[^15] Our traditions emphasize obedience in different areas. Which of God's commands do you think churches in general find easiest to treat as optional, and why?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Who are the people gleaning at the edges of your "field" right now: at work, in your neighborhood, at church? What would it look like for you to leave the corners, or even drop a little extra on purpose?

</div>

### The Threshing Floor and the Gate <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#the-threshing-floor-and-the-gate}

*Ruth makes a bold request, and Boaz takes it to the city gate.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Ruth 3:1–13 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** Notice who speaks, who acts, and who makes the requests in this chapter. The notes read the scene through its cultural symbols: Ruth's request to "Spread your garment" is a betrothal symbol, so she is asking to be a wife, not a servant.[^16] They also take uncovering Boaz's feet as a euphemism pointing to his circumcision, the sign of his covenant, so that Ruth reminds Boaz of "his covenant obligation to care for aliens, orphans, and widows."[^17] What do you notice about Ruth's boldness and Boaz's response in verses 9–13?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** Boaz calls Ruth's act a kindness, *hesed* ("covenant loyalty"), greater than her care for Naomi, because she could have pursued younger, wealthier men but instead called Boaz to keep his covenant obligations.[^18] At the gate, the closer relative, the first *go'el* ("kinsman-redeemer"), agrees to redeem the land but backs out when he learns he must also marry Ruth the Moabitess.[^19] What does the contrast between Boaz and the unnamed relative show about what covenant love costs?

*If time allows, read Ruth 4:13–17 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q11:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes trace the line from Ruth to Obed to Jesse to David, and note that the elders' blessing names Perez, born from Judah and Tamar, "another story of questionable legitimacy and unconventional inclusion."[^20] What do these "irregular" names in David's family tree say about the kind of family God is building?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q12:** The notes observe that Boaz and Ruth "didn't wait for society to improve; they lived out God's values regardless of surrounding failures," and that "Ruth couldn't have known she'd be David's ancestor."[^21] In a season when the culture around you feels like the days of the judges, what is one act of quiet faithfulness you could keep doing even if you never see where it leads?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> In the days when the judges ruled, when Israel kept forgetting the story, a famine sent one family to Moab and grief brought two widows back. Naomi came home bitter. Ruth came home loyal, an alien, a widow and as good as an orphan, and went to glean in a field owned by a man who kept God's commands and then went beyond them. At the threshing floor and the city gate, covenant love became concrete: a redeemer willing to pay the cost and an outsider brought all the way in. The book is a love story, and the deepest love in it is love for God shown in how the vulnerable are treated.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: who is one person you could show *hesed* to this week, and what might that look like?

</div>
