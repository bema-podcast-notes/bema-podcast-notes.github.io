### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Michael Burns asked us to hear faith as allegiance to Jesus as King, to read Romans 12 and 13 together, and to treat every person as an image-bearer before any label.[^1]

**This week:** We return to *The Chosen*, this time to its pilot, a short film that tells the birth of Jesus in Luke 2 through the eyes of shepherds.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The pilot began in 2017 as a 22-minute short film for Dallas Jenkins' church, made with a small budget and crew.[^2] Marty and Brent sort history from creative choice. They note that the opening text has priests reading messianic prophecies, which the priesthood of the day (Sadducees, who accepted only the Torah as inspired) would not have done; the Pharisees would have. They also caution that messianic hope was real but not as universal as Christians often assume, and that we should avoid oversimplifying it "in supersessionist ways."[^3] They appreciate the film's birth setting, the lower floor of a house where animals were kept, which still leaves Mary isolated and on the margins.[^4]

The film centers on Simon, a shepherd with a disabled leg who is mocked by the other shepherds and dismissed by a religious inspector, yet is the one Joseph chooses to hold the baby first.[^5] The hosts draw an Advent lesson: the Messiah came "under people's noses," the outcasts saw and believed, and most people missed him.[^6] As always with *The Chosen*, the show is a doorway; Luke's text is the main event.

### Clearing Away the Christmas Card <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#clearing-away-the-christmas-card}

*The hosts ask what the scene actually looked like, and why it matters.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Luke 2:1–7 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Read slowly and notice what Luke actually tells us about the place of Jesus's birth and the circumstances around it. What details are there, and which familiar ones (a stable, a barn, an innkeeper) are not? The notes describe the film's choice: not a wooden barn with golden straw, but likely the ground floor of a house where animals were kept.[^4] How does that picture change the scene for you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The hosts insist that even in a house, Mary "was not 'surrounded by loving community' during birth"; the lower animal area was still "a setting of rejection and hardship," dirty and unpleasant. They also appreciate that the film shows a genuinely newborn baby, bloody and dirty, in dim light, which "avoids sanitizing the Christmas story."[^7] Why do you think we tend to clean up the nativity? What do we lose when we do?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty and Brent correct the film's opening text: the priests (Sadducees) accepted only the Torah as Scripture and had no interest in messianic expectations, while the Pharisees were the ones reading the prophets. They add that messianic hope was strongest among fringe groups and the desperately oppressed, not "a dominant or monolithic Jewish expectation."[^3] Why might it matter, for how we read the Gospels and for how we speak about Jewish people, to get this kind of detail right?

</div>

### Simon and the Shepherds <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#simon-and-the-shepherds}

*The film builds its story around the people Luke says heard the news first.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Luke 2:8–20 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Who receives the announcement, where are they, and what do they do afterward? Notice the verbs in verses 15–20. What do you notice about how the people who hear the shepherds respond, and how Mary responds?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The hosts explain that male shepherds near Bethlehem are plausible because the priestly house raised sheep there for temple sacrifice; these shepherds were still lower class, though not as marginal as the child shepherds typical elsewhere.[^8] The film then makes its central character Simon, who has an untreated, disabled leg, is mocked by the other shepherds, and shows kindness to Mary and Joseph by giving them water.[^5] Why do you think the filmmakers chose a disabled outcast among outcasts as the one to follow? What does that choice draw out of Luke's story?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> In the film, Joseph chooses Simon to be the first to hold the baby, and Mary bandages his wounded arm. The notes call this a reversal that "shows the kingdom pattern: the last shall be first, the outcasts are welcomed, the broken are valued."[^5] Where have you seen that kind of reversal, in Scripture or in life, and what did it teach you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The religious inspector in the film blames Simon: "Messiah hasn't come because of people like you with your stains."[^9] (The episode is critiquing a character, not the Pharisees as a whole, and the film's historical mix-ups are noted above.) Blaming the vulnerable for what is wrong is a temptation in every tradition, including ours. Where do you see communities, or yourself, quietly treating certain people as the reason God seems absent? What would it look like to treat them as the first to see him?

</div>

### Don't Miss Him <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#dont-miss-him}

*The hosts close with an invitation for Advent and for every season.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 35:3–6 aloud. (In the film, Isaiah 35 is heard as Simon wanders with his lamb.)*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The film uses Micah and Isaiah 35, 7 and 9 as voiceover, and the notes say the technique "provides ironic counterpoint to what's happening visually."[^10] What does it add to hear these words about the lame leaping and the weak made strong while watching a limping shepherd? What does it suggest about where prophecy finds its fulfillment?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The other shepherds are crude: crass jokes, mockery of Simon, nothing romantic about them. The hosts value this because it shows "God's kingdom comes to imperfect people," and after the angels appear, the lead shepherd hugs Simon.[^11] What does it mean to you that the first witnesses were not admirable people cleaned up for the occasion?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The Advent invitation is that the Messiah came "under people's noses" but most missed him, while outcasts and unexpected people saw; the notes say the story "calls us to pay attention to where Jesus appears."[^6] Where might Christ be showing up in your life or community right now in a place you would be likely to overlook? What will you do to pay attention this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> This week a short film sent us back to Luke 2. Marty and Brent cleared away some Christmas-card assumptions: a birth in the dirty lower floor of a house, a newborn who looked like a newborn, priests who would not have been reading the prophets, and a hope that was real but not universal. At the center of the film is Simon, a limping shepherd mocked by his peers and dismissed by a religious official, who is handed the Messiah first. The people on the margins saw and believed while most of the town slept through it. The invitation is to watch for where Jesus shows up now, especially among the people we are most likely to overlook.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, name one place or person where you will look for Christ this week.

</div>
