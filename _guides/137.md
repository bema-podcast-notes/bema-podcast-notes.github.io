### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** In Acts 3–6 the Kingdom became visible in healing, shared possessions and care for widows, and opposition followed each time. Isaiah 32 pictured a king whose rulers are each a shelter, and Peter's shadow became that shade.

**This week:** The opposition turns deadly. Stephen dies forgiving his killers, the scattered believers carry the message outward, and an Ethiopian official finds an answer in Isaiah.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode asks two questions of Acts 7–8: how does the Kingdom advance, and who does it include? On the first, Marty reads Jesus' teaching in the Sermon on the Mount (turn the other cheek, give your cloak, go the second mile) as nonviolent resistance: not passive submission and not violence, but public action that exposes injustice. Stephen lives this out, praying for those who stone him, and the episode notes that he sees Jesus standing at God's right hand. Later martyrs such as Polycarp follow the same path. The episode openly presses on modern attitudes toward self-defense.

On the second question, the episode follows the message to the Samaritans and then to an Ethiopian eunuch, a man excluded twice over. The notes suggest that Philip's conversation likely continued from Isaiah 53 into Isaiah 56, which promises eunuchs "a memorial and a name better than sons and daughters." The eunuch's real question, the episode says, was whether there was a place for someone like him.

### The Other Cheek <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#the-other-cheek}

*Before Stephen's death, the episode revisits the teaching that shaped how the early church faced violence.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 5:38–42 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Look closely at the specific details Jesus gives: which cheek, which garment, how far. What do you notice? Then hear the episode's explanation:[^1]

- A strike on the right cheek was a backhanded blow used to demean a lower-class person. Turning the left cheek forces the striker to treat you as an equal.
- The cloak lawsuit was an unjust demand that violated Deuteronomy. Giving everything exposes the injustice publicly.
- Roman soldiers could legally force civilians to carry their pack one mile (*angaria*). Volunteering a second mile puts the soldier in the awkward position of breaking Roman law.

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say the early church "used non-violent resistance rather than pacifism or redemptive violence to confront injustice," and that Jesus' teaching was "about exposing injustice through public demonstration, not passive submission."[^2] How does this differ from what you were taught "turn the other cheek" means?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Think of an injustice you face or see regularly, large or small. What might it look like to respond in a way that neither returns harm nor quietly accepts it, but brings the injustice into the light?

</div>

### Stephen and the Standing Jesus <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#stephen-and-the-standing-jesus}

*Stephen's death becomes the model for how the early church met persecution.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Acts 7:54–60 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes point out that Luke, like Josephus, follows narrative with long speeches, and Stephen's speech in Acts 7 is one of them.[^3] Now look at how the speech ends. What do you notice about what Stephen sees and what he says as he dies? The episode calls this "the only place in Scripture where Jesus is depicted standing rather than sitting at God's right hand," suggesting Jesus paying tribute to Stephen as the first disciple to fully embrace the cross-bearing call.[^4] What do you make of that image?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode names later Christians who chose death over denying Christ, including Philip of Hierapolis, who watched his daughters crucified, and Polycarp of Smyrna, who chose burning at the stake.[^5] It also notes that persecution "led to the scattering of believers, which accelerated the spread of the Gospel."[^6]

*Read Acts 8:1–8 aloud.*

What do you notice about what the scattered believers do? What does that suggest about how the Kingdom advances?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes say the early church "consistently chose martyrdom over self-protection as their method of Kingdom advancement," and that the episode "challenges contemporary Christian attitudes toward self-defense and gun ownership."[^7] People of faith in this room may hold different convictions about this, and the goal is not to settle it for anyone else. Where does this teaching press on you? What do you find yourself trusting for safety, and how does Stephen's example speak to that?

</div>

### A Place for the Dry Tree <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#a-place-for-the-dry-tree}

*The message moves outward to people who had been kept at the edges.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes trace a progression in Acts 8: first the Samaritans, "ethnic and religious outsiders," then the Ethiopian eunuch, "doubly excluded (foreign and sexually altered)."[^8] Why do you think Luke places these stories right after Stephen's death and the scattering?

*Read Acts 8:26–35 aloud, then Isaiah 53:7–8.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Notice what the eunuch was reading and the question he asks Philip. The episode argues that his question "wasn't about messianic identity but about inclusion," and that Philip's conversation likely went on to Isaiah 56.[^9] Why might a man in his position linger over this particular passage?

*Read Isaiah 56:3–5 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** What words does Isaiah put in the eunuch's mouth to describe himself? What does God promise instead? The notes highlight the promise to eunuchs who keep covenant of "a memorial and a name better than sons and daughters" within the temple.[^10] Imagine hearing this passage as someone who had been told, in many ways, that he did not belong. What would it mean to you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes put the eunuch's question this way: "Is there a place in God's family for someone like me?"[^11] Who is asking that question of churches today? What would it take for them to hear Isaiah 56 in the way our community treats them?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Jesus taught a third way between violence and passivity: actions that expose injustice without returning harm. Stephen walked that way to his death, praying for his killers, and Luke shows Jesus standing to receive him. The scattering that followed carried the message to Samaria and to a eunuch on the Gaza road. In Isaiah's words, the man who called himself a dry tree is promised a name better than sons and daughters.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each: who is one "dry tree" you could help find a place this week?

</div>
