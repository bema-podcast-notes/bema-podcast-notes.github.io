### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Paul argued from Abraham's story that righteousness came by trust before circumcision, and described the law as a guardian protecting an heir until the promise could be claimed.[^1]

**This week:** Paul turns Abraham's two wives into an allegory about two kinds of covenant relationship, and names God-fearing Gentiles as children of promise.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This episode completes Galatians 4. Paul remembers how warmly the Galatians first received him during an illness, warns about misdirected zeal, and then builds a rabbinic allegory from Abraham's two wives. Hagar, the slave woman, stands for Mount Sinai and "present Jerusalem"; Sarah, the free woman, stands for "the Jerusalem above" and the covenant of promise.[^2]

The notes explain how covenants layer in Scripture: the covenant with Noah for all creation, the covenant with Abraham based on faith and promise, and the Sinai covenant with the descendants of Jacob. Paul's revolutionary claim is that Gentiles can be *b'nai Abraham* ("children of Abraham") through faith, not merely *Ben Noach* ("son of Noah"). The allegory is aimed at the circumcision party pressing Gentiles to convert, so this guide gives care to reading it without turning it against Jewish people.[^3]

### Hospitality and Zeal <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#hospitality-and-zeal}

*We hear Paul's personal appeal before he makes his argument.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Galatians 4:12–20 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** What does Paul remember about how the Galatians first received him? What has changed since then, and how does Paul describe his own feelings now?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes say the Galatians first showed "Abraham-like hospitality" to Paul during his illness, but had since changed their attitude. They also stress: "Zeal itself is not wrong - God called zealots as disciples - but zeal must be directed toward God's inclusive purposes rather than exclusionary practices."[^4] What turns good zeal into something that shuts people out? What do you think Paul wants the Galatians to be zealous for instead?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Think back to the zeal you named in the opening. Where is your zeal right now aimed at drawing people in, and where might it be aimed at keeping people out?

</div>

### Two Women, Two Covenants <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#two-women-two-covenants}

*We work through Paul's allegory and the covenant story underneath it.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 16:1–4 and Genesis 21:8–12 aloud, then read Galatians 4:21–31 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Make two columns, one for Hagar and one for Sarah. Using only Galatians 4:21–31, list everything Paul connects with each woman. What do you notice about which details Paul draws from Genesis and which he adds?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes lay out the covenant layers: Noah (with all creation), Abraham (based on faith and promise), and Sinai (with the descendants of Jacob who stood at the mountain). They observe that even today a Gentile attending synagogue can identify as *Ben Noach* but "would not be welcomed claiming to be a child of Abraham."[^5] With that in mind, why would Paul calling God-fearing Gentiles "brothers" and "children of promise" have sounded so startling? What does it say about the Abrahamic promise that it was always meant to reach the nations?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes identify the "circumcision party (Shammai Jews)" with the Hagar side of the allegory, and say those "seeking justification through law-keeping are actually choosing slavery over freedom, contrary to their intentions."[^6] Allegories like this have often been read as God rejecting the Jewish people. How does knowing that Paul's target is a specific group pressing Gentiles to convert change how you read verses 29–31? How can we read this passage without slipping into contempt for Judaism?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The allegory turns on the difference between being born a slave and being born free as a child of promise. Where in your own walk with God do you still live like a slave trying to earn a place rather than a child who already has one?

</div>

### Spiritual Checkpoints <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#spiritual-checkpoints}

*We turn the question toward our own communities.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes warn that we "often create requirements for Gospel access similar to the circumcision party," and that churches sometimes adopt "a 'we are in and you are out' mentality."[^7] Each of our traditions has practices it holds dear. Without accusing anyone, what checkpoints might a newcomer run into in our churches before feeling they truly belong? Which of those come from Scripture and which from our culture or habit?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes insist Paul's inclusive gospel "doesn't mean universalism or that truth is relative," but that God "has always intended to bless all nations through Abraham."[^8] How do you hold welcome and conviction together in your own relationships? Name one person you could welcome more fully this week without letting go of what you believe.

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Paul remembers a people who once welcomed him like Abraham welcomed strangers, and he grieves that their zeal has turned toward shutting others out. Through Hagar and Sarah he argues that the promise to Abraham, older than Sinai, was always meant for the nations. God-fearing Gentiles are not guests on the edge of the family; they are brothers and children of promise. The challenge for us is to aim our zeal at God's open table rather than at our own checkpoints.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one checkpoint you want to stop placing in someone's way?

</div>
