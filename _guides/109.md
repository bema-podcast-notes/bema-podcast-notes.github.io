### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Jesus answered the charge that he worked by Beelzebul, pointed to good fruit and the sign of Jonah, and redefined his family as all who do the Father's will.

**This week:** Matthew 13 begins Jesus's parables. The first one is usually called the Parable of the Sower, but its real question may be about the soil.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode begins BEMA's journey through the parables by renaming the first one. If God is the sower, the variable in the story is the condition of the soil, so the notes call it the Parable of the Soils.[^1] Jesus uses a four-part pattern that rabbis commonly used to describe kinds of students, a pattern that does not need to ask "Which one are you?" because the structure asks it for you.[^2]

The episode also faces Jesus's surprising explanation of why he teaches in parables, quoting Isaiah 6, and contrasts an Eastern rabbi's goal of transformation with a Western teacher's goal of clarity.[^3] Finally, it follows Jesus's hints back to the prophets, where each poor soil has a remedy: generosity for hard ground, deep roots for rocky ground, and a circumcised heart for thorny ground.[^4]

### A Farmer Went Out to Sow <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#a-farmer-went-out-to-sow}

*Jesus sits in a boat and tells a crowd a story about seed and ground.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 13:1–9 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Read the story slowly and notice what stays the same and what changes from one part to the next. Who sows, what is sown, and what is different each time?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes argue the parable "is misnamed in Western tradition" and should be the Parable of the Soils, because "God is the sower, not us."[^5] They describe a common reading, "Go sow seed everywhere - some people will receive it, some won't," and say it "Makes us the sower instead of God" and "Focuses on others rather than self-examination."[^6] How does changing the title change who the parable is talking to?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe a familiar rabbinic model of four learners: the sponge, which keeps everything; the funnel, which loses everything; the strainer, which keeps only the bad; and the sieve, which keeps only the good.[^7] "The template itself does the work, provoking self-examination and reflection."[^8] Which of the four learners have you been in different seasons of your life, and what made the difference?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes say the parable's purpose "is to challenge and transform the listener, not to reaffirm what they already believe."[^9] When you hear a familiar Bible story, what helps you listen for a challenge rather than for confirmation of what you already think?

</div>

### Why Parables? <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#why-parables}

*The disciples ask Jesus why he speaks to the crowds in stories, and his answer is not what they expect.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 13:10–17 aloud, then Isaiah 6:9–10.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** In your own words, what reason does Jesus give for teaching in parables? Report what you notice in verse 15 about why the people do not see or hear.

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes say that by Western standards Jesus would be considered a "horrible teacher," because "His measuring stick for success is transformation, not information transfer."[^10] They contrast Western teaching, which prizes "Clarity and accessibility," with Eastern rabbinic teaching, which prizes "Transformation over information."[^11] What do you think is gained, and what is risked, when a teacher makes understanding depend on the learner's willingness to dig?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Reading Isaiah 6, the notes suggest that "Understanding would require repentance and change" and that "Many prefer comfortable ignorance to challenging truth."[^12] Why might understanding something sometimes feel more costly than not understanding it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Think about a truth from Scripture you understood only after wrestling with it. How did that change you differently than something you were simply told?

</div>

### Preparing the Soil <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#preparing-the-soil}

*Jesus explains the parable, and the notes hear the prophets behind each soil.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 13:18–23 aloud. Then read Hosea 10:12 and Jeremiah 4:3–4.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes match each poor soil with a prophetic remedy. For the path, Hosea 10:12 calls for breaking up unplowed ground through *tzedekah*, "righteousness" or "generosity." For the thorns, Jeremiah 4:3–4 calls for circumcised hearts and the removal of idolatry.[^13] After reading those two passages, what do you notice that connects each remedy to the problem Jesus describes in verses 19 and 22?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> For rocky ground, the notes point to Isaiah 5:24 and to deep roots grown through *hagah*, "meditation" or "dwelling" in the law of the Lord, like the acacia tree from Session 1 that looks dead in drought but lives by its deep roots.[^14] For the thorns, they recall Session 2's teaching that Israel's core idolatry was the "lust for empire," pursuing power, wealth and security through worldly means.[^15] Which image, the acacia's roots or the lust for empire, speaks most to where you are right now?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q11:** The notes put the central question plainly: "which soil are you, and what work do you need to do to prepare your heart?"[^16] Which soil best describes you in this season, and what is one specific step you could take this week to prepare the ground?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The sower in this parable is God, and God sows generously. The question is the ground. Jesus used a pattern his listeners knew, four kinds of learners, and let the structure ask the question for him. He taught in parables because he wanted transformation, not just information, and transformation requires digging. Behind each soil the prophets offer a remedy: generosity breaks hard ground, dwelling in God's word grows deep roots, and a circumcised heart clears away the thorns of empire.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one thing you will do this week to prepare your soil?

</div>
