### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Two sources tell the history of Israel's kings: Samuel/Kings, "agenda-driven headlines from Israel," and Chronicles, a "documentary perspective from Judah" written centuries later. Both are accurate, and both are prophetic history meant to move us.

**This week:** We put the two sources side by side for David and Solomon and discover that they disagree about where the story turned. That disagreement is the point.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Samuel/Kings tells the story with an agenda of "Immorality and Idolatry": David's sin with Bathsheba and Solomon's many wives are the turning points. Chronicles, written with centuries of hindsight, has an agenda of "Empire and Injustice." It leaves out Bathsheba altogether and points instead to David's wish to build God a temple, to Solomon breaking the kingship laws of Deuteronomy 17, and to the forced labor that made Solomon "a new Pharaoh."

The episode's big claim is that lust for Empire leads to moral failure, not the other way around. It does not set the two sources against each other; both are inspired and true. Many of us learned these stories mainly as lessons in personal morality, so this session invites us to hold that lesson alongside a second one about the systems and ambitions we live inside.

### Two Tellings of David <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#two-tellings-of-david}

*Chronicles tells David's story without the scene most of us know best.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes summarize each source's agenda in a phrase: Samuel/Kings, "Immorality and Idolatry"; Chronicles, "Empire and Injustice." In Samuel/Kings, the Bathsheba incident is "the definitive turning point," but Chronicles has "No mention of the Bathsheba incident at all."[^1] What is your first reaction to a biblical writer leaving out the story many of us were taught most often? Why might a writer with centuries of hindsight make that choice?

*Read 1 Chronicles 17:1–6 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** In Chronicles the turning point is this request. The notes put David's reasoning this way: "Why should I live in a palace of cedar while my Lord lives in a tent?" They paraphrase God's reply as "I like my tent, thank you very much" and "When did I ever ask for a house?" The tent "signifies mobility and presence with the people," while a fixed temple "implies a fixed, institutionalized God."[^2] As you listened, what did you notice about what God says he has and has not done? What does God seem to value about the tent?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The episode's central claim is that "lust for Empire leads to moral failure, not vice versa." After the temple request, the notes say, David "shifts from *kiddush hashem* to conquest and accumulation."[^3] (*Kiddush hashem* means hallowing God's name.) Many of us learned David's fall mainly as a story about one sin. How does reversing the order, Empire first and moral failure second, change the way you think about how good people go wrong?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q4:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes observe that "Empire thinking is harder to identify than moral failures because it often comes with good intentions," since "David genuinely wanted to honor God with a temple."[^4] Where might a good intention in your own life, or in a community you love, be quietly building something God never asked for?

</div>

### The Mirror Passages <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#the-mirror-passages}

*Two accounts of the same census, with one striking difference.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read 2 Samuel 24:1–3 aloud, then read 1 Chronicles 21:1–3 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** If you compared these passages this week, share what you found. Listen especially for who incites David, what Joab says, and the order of the place names. The notes point out that in Samuel "The anger of the LORD burned against Israel, and he incited David," while in Chronicles "Satan rose up against Israel and incited David," and Joab asks, "Why should he bring guilt on Israel?" Even the geography flips: "Dan to Beersheba" in Samuel, "Beersheba to Dan" in Chronicles.[^5] What does each version seem most concerned about?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes resolve the tension with "Both/And Thinking": "Personal morality matters AND systemic justice matters."[^6] Every tradition, and every person, tends to lean toward one of these. Which emphasis comes more naturally to you, and what would it take for you to hold both together?

</div>

### Solomon and the Price of Empire <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#solomon-and-the-price-of-empire}

*The chronicler builds Solomon's story to echo a warning Moses gave long before.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Deuteronomy 17:14–20 aloud, then read 2 Chronicles 1:14–17 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes say the first chapter of 2 Chronicles "deliberately shows Solomon breaking every kingship law from Deuteronomy 17": accumulating horses, importing horses from Egypt ("you are not to go back that way again"), taking many wives, and amassing silver and gold.[^7] Which of the warnings in Deuteronomy did you hear echoed in the Chronicles passage? Why might the chronicler open Solomon's reign this way?

*Read 1 Kings 12:1–15 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** When Solomon dies, the people beg his son to "loosen the yoke." The notes conclude that "Building an Empire always comes at a price - paid by the oppressed," and that Solomon "becomes a new Pharaoh, using slave labor for his building projects."[^8] Rehoboam chooses the advice to double down.[^9] How does a people who were once slaves in Egypt end up running forced labor within a few generations? What makes that slide so hard to see from the inside?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes apply this to church life, comparing the temple project to building campaigns, budgets, and "counting attendees." They are careful to say "Church buildings and budgets are not inherently wrong (amoral)," but that there is "a persistent temptation to build Empires rather than embody Shalom."[^10] Without pointing at any particular church, what questions could help a congregation tell the difference between building for Shalom and building for Empire?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes call us to "continually remember their humble origins," the "field shepherding sheep" and being "slaves in Egypt," because "Forgetting these origins leads to becoming Pharaoh ourselves."[^11] What is the humble beginning in your own story that you most need to keep remembering, and what helps you remember it?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Samuel/Kings tells us that David fell with Bathsheba and Solomon fell through his many wives, and that is true. Chronicles, looking back across centuries, tells us the story behind that story: a king uncomfortable in his palace of cedar who wanted to give God a house, and a son who built an Empire on horses, gold, and forced labor. God preferred the tent, mobile and present with the people. Both sources are inspired, and together they ask us to watch our morals and also the ambitions and systems that shape them. The persistent invitation is to remember where we came from and choose Shalom over Empire.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one "tent" in your life that you are tempted to turn into a "temple"?

</div>
