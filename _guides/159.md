### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** In Ephesians, Paul's pronouns traced God's story from the Jewish believers who first hoped in Christ to the Gentiles who were brought in, no longer foreigners but "members of his household."[^1]

**This week:** We read the warmest letter Paul wrote, sent from chains to a small, poor and generous church. It is a letter about joy, partnership, humility and contentment.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode describes Philippians as reading like a "gushing love letter" next to Paul's sharper letters to the Galatians and Corinthians. Paul writes from imprisonment, likely in Rome, to a church that was neither large nor wealthy but that supported his ministry when other churches would not.[^2]

Four threads run through the episode: joy that circumstances cannot take away; the hymn in Philippians 2 about Christ refusing to cling to divinity; Paul's call to imitate him, which the notes connect to the Jewish rabbinic way of forming disciples; and the famous line "I can do all things through Christ who strengthens me," which the notes place back in its setting of learning contentment.[^3] This is a short episode, so we will spend more of our time in the text itself.

### A Love Letter from Prison <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#a-love-letter-from-prison}

*Paul writes in chains, and the letter overflows with affection.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Philippians 1:3–26 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes contrast this letter with Galatians, where Paul was "astonished" and "furious," and with Corinthians, where he was "confrontational and prophetic."[^4] As you listen to chapter 1, what words of affection, gratitude and joy do you hear? Call them out and let someone keep a list. What do they tell you about this relationship?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> In verses 21–26 Paul wrestles with whether he would rather "die and be with Christ" or "remain in the flesh," and concludes that staying would serve the Philippians more.[^5] What does the way he reasons through that choice reveal about what he values most?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Paul thanks God for the Philippians' "partnership in the gospel from the first day until now." The notes describe this *koinonia* ("fellowship" or "partnership") as "more than financial support," a shared participation in the gospel mission that creates deep bonds.[^6] Who has been that kind of partner to you in your faith, and who might you become that kind of partner for?

</div>

### The Mind of Christ and the Way of Imitation <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-mind-of-christ-and-the-way-of-imitation}

*Paul holds up Jesus as the pattern, and then dares to hold up himself.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Philippians 2:5–11 aloud, slowly.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Trace the movement of the hymn. Where does Christ begin, what does he let go of, and where does he go, step by step? The notes say the passage describes Christ's willingness to "not consider divinity as something to be held on to," with the Greek word suggesting "grasped" or "clung to."[^7] What does Jesus refuse to cling to, and what happens after he lets go?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** *Read Philippians 3:17 and 4:9 aloud.* The notes summarize Paul's call as "imitate me as I imitate Christ" and link it to the rabbinic tradition, where students learned "not just through instruction but through observing and copying their teacher's way of life." They contrast Greek schools, which emphasized "the transmission of ideas," with the Jewish model of "behavioral imitation and practical living."[^8] What is the difference between learning about Jesus and learning Jesus by watching someone live? Why do you think it is harder for most of us to say "watch me" than to say "listen to me"?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say the hymn provides "a model for how believers should relate to power, status, and privilege."[^9] *Read Philippians 2:1–4 aloud.* What do the verses just before the hymn ask of the community as a whole? What would it look like for a church, not just an individual, to have this mind?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** If someone followed you around for a week the way a student followed a rabbi, what would they learn about Jesus from your life? Name one thing you are clinging to (a status, a reputation, a right) that the mind of Christ might ask you to hold more loosely.

</div>

### Contentment, Not Conquest <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#contentment-not-conquest}

*The most quoted verse in the letter sounds different in its own paragraph.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Philippians 4:10–19 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Find verse 13. What comes right before it, and what comes right after it? The notes say this verse is "specifically about contentment in various circumstances, not a general promise of empowerment for any endeavor," and that it has been applied inappropriately to "success theology or personal achievement."[^10] Where have you seen this verse used, and what changes when you read it next to "well fed or hungry"?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Paul writes that "not one church shared with me in the matter of giving and receiving except you only," and that "even when I was in Thessalonica, you sent me aid more than once." The notes conclude that "generosity is more about heart posture than financial capacity."[^11] What do you make of a poor church being the one that gave when others did not?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** *Read Philippians 4:4–8 aloud.* The notes call verse 8 practical guidance for "mental and spiritual health," focusing on what is "true, noble, right, pure, lovely, and admirable."[^12] Look at the circumstance you brought from this week where contentment was hard. What is one thing that is true and lovely within it that you could choose to dwell on?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Philippians is a letter from a prisoner who cannot stop rejoicing. Paul writes to a small, poor church that gave when others would not, and he calls them partners. He holds up a Christ who would not cling to divinity but let it go, and he asks them to learn that way of life by watching him. Even his most famous promise turns out to be a testimony about contentment, whether full or hungry. Joy here is not a mood that depends on circumstances. It grows from partnership, humility and trust.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one thing you will choose to dwell on this week, from Paul's list in Philippians 4:8?

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 160 — Colossians — Gnostic Heresy](/guides/160)** (23:14): Paul writes to a small church in Colossae threatened by teaching rooted in Platonic dualism, which treated the spiritual as pure and the physical as inferior. The episode shows how Paul answers with Christ, in whom "all the fullness of the Deity lives in bodily form," and with the reconciliation of "all things," and it asks how much our own picture of heaven owes to Plato.[^13]

**What to bring next week:** Read Colossians 1:15–23 and count how many times Paul says "all things." Bring one song, saying or idea you grew up with about heaven, and be ready to share it.
