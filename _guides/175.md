### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Revelation is apocalyptic literature written to churches under Roman persecution, asking whether faithfulness was worth the cost. In chapter 1, John pictures Jesus with images drawn from Daniel, Zechariah, Ezekiel and Isaiah, holding the seven stars Rome thought ruled destiny and walking among his lampstands.

**This week:** The first of the seven letters goes to Ephesus. The church is praised for its discernment and endurance, then confronted with a single charge: it has forsaken the love it had at first.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode sets the letter to Ephesus against the twenty-five years since Paul wrote to the same city. In that time, the notes say, the eruption of Vesuvius was read as the anger of Israel's God over the destroyed temple, and Domitian ran a fear-based campaign against the Jews and their God, centered in Ephesus.[^1] The order of the seven letters follows the postal route through Asia, starting from Ephesus, the second-largest city in the empire and the enduring center of the Christian movement.[^2]

The Ephesian church tested false apostles and hated the practices of the Nicolaitans, who taught that one could take part in pagan worship physically while keeping the spirit pure.[^3] Yet something had cooled. The hosts admit uncertainty about what "first love" means and offer three possibilities from Paul's letter: love for Christ, love for one another, and love for the Jew-Gentile inclusion Paul celebrated in Ephesians 2.[^4] The episode is short, so this guide gives extra time to reading Revelation 2 alongside Ephesians.

**Opening Question:** Think of a time you worked hard to get something right, at work, at home or at church, and later realized you had lost some of the joy that started you down that road. What happened?

### Ephesus, Twenty-Five Years Later <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#ephesus-twenty-five-years-later}

*Ephesus had changed a great deal since Paul wrote to it, and the letter makes more sense once we see how.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Revelation 2:1–3 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes place Paul's letter to the Ephesians around 62–65 AD and Revelation in Domitian's reign, most likely the late 80s or early 90s, a gap of about twenty-five years.[^5] They report that the Oracle of Delphi read the AD 79 eruption of Vesuvius as the God of Israel's anger over the destroyed temple, and that Domitian exploited this with what the hosts call a "fear-based political campaign," promising to destroy the Jews and their God.[^6] Look at the words Jesus uses to commend this church in verses 2–3. What do they suggest about what the last twenty-five years had demanded of them?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** Ephesus was a port city and the second-largest city in the empire, and the notes call it the more enduring center of the Christian movement, "the head of the snake" in the eyes of its opponents.[^7] The seven letters follow the actual postal route, beginning with Ephesus, which reminds us that Revelation "was a real letter to real churches facing real circumstances."[^8] The notes add that Timothy, Paul's protégé, pastored this church, the "little young punk named Timothy," as Marty calls him.[^9] How does knowing these people were real, and under real pressure, change the way you hear the praise in verses 2–3?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say the hosts are introducing their method for reading Revelation slowly, layering one principle at a time across the seven letters, and that Ephesus is "not the best church letter" for showing the full method because many details remain unclear.[^10] How comfortable are you with a study that admits uncertainty this openly, and what does that honesty invite from us as readers?

</div>

### The Church That Could Not Be Fooled <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#the-church-that-could-not-be-fooled}

*Ephesus was good at spotting compromise, and the episode takes its praise seriously.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Revelation 2:2–6 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes describe the Nicolaitans as promoting full engagement with pagan Roman culture, influenced by Gnostic ideas, so that one could practice pagan rites physically while keeping the spirit devoted to God. The hosts compare it to crossing your fingers behind your back while offering incense and saying, "I'm not truly worshiping those gods; God knows what's in my heart."[^11] The notes add that the issue was right practice more than right belief.[^12] What makes that kind of reasoning so attractive, then and now?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes observe that contemporary believers often use the same logic: "God knows my heart" becomes a justification in business ethics, entertainment choices, relationships and more.[^13] Where do you notice the temptation to keep faith in one compartment and practice in another, in our culture or in your own life?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes suggest the Nicolaitans may be related to, or even the same as, the false teachers addressed in Jude, 2 Peter and Colossians, though the relationships are unclear.[^14] Thinking back over the general letters we have studied, what common pressure do you see behind these warnings?

</div>

### The Love You Had at First <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-love-you-had-at-first}

*After all that praise comes one charge, and the hosts are honest that its meaning is not certain.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Revelation 2:4–7 aloud, then Ephesians 2:11–22 and Ephesians 3:14–19.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes offer three possibilities for the love Ephesus had forsaken, each rooted in Paul's earlier letter: love for Christ, since Paul prays they will know "the love of Christ that surpasses knowledge"; love for one another in a diverse community under pressure; and love for the inclusion of Jews and Gentiles.[^15] Look at Ephesians 2:11–22 and notice how Paul uses "we" and "you." The notes say the pronouns distinguish Jews who had the ancestral faith from Gentiles being invited into it.[^16] What do you notice about how Paul describes the two groups and what Christ has done between them?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes explain that a low wall in the Jerusalem temple, only two or three feet high, separated the Court of the Gentiles from areas reserved for Jews, with signs warning that Gentiles who crossed would face death. Paul's claim that Christ tore down "the dividing wall of hostility" announced a new reality.[^17] Which of the three possibilities seems most likely to you for what Ephesus lost, and how might the three be connected?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes name the irony plainly: while fighting for right practice, "something in the Ephesian believers died," and they present this as "a cautionary tale about the possibility of being 'right' while losing what matters most."[^18] Every tradition in this room has convictions it has fought to keep. Without pointing at any one church, where have you seen a community win an argument for truth and lose some of its love along the way?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The letter closes with a promise to the one who is victorious: the right to eat from the tree of life in the paradise of God.[^19] If your own "first love" has cooled in some way, what is one concrete step you could take this week to return to it?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The church at Ephesus had been through twenty-five hard years. It tested false apostles, refused the Nicolaitan bargain of a pure heart and compromised hands, and kept going under Domitian's pressure without growing weary. Jesus sees all of it and says so. Then he names what they lost, whether love for him, love for each other, or love for the radical welcome that tore down the dividing wall. Ephesus reminds us that a church can be right and still need to repent.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one thing that rekindles your love for God or for people when it starts to cool?

</div>
