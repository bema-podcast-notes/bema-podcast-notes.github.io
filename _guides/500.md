### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Reed described Scripture as "God-breathed" in two senses, coming from God and giving life, and showed that poetry, satire, story and history can each carry real truth. He also left us with a test for any way of reading: does it produce fear, or love?[^1]

**This week:** The four-pillars series pauses for BEMA's 500th episode. Rabbi Dr. David Rudolph traces how the early church went from relying on the Jewish community to set the date of its central yearly celebration to deliberately cutting that tie at the Council of Nicaea.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

For its 500th episode, BEMA paired a celebration (the announcement of its first live recording) with a sobering history lesson. David Rudolph, a Messianic Jewish New Testament scholar, uses one question, when to celebrate *Pascha* (the early church's name for Passover and, later, Easter), as a window into the "parting of the ways" between church and synagogue. The story runs from Polycarp asking the Jewish community when Nisan 14 fell, through the Bar Kokhba revolt and a bitter dispute between East and West, to Constantine's letter after Nicaea, which is openly antisemitic.

Rudolph's aim is honest reckoning, not condemning the church. Nicaea made decisions many of us treasure, and it also made a decision that has fed antisemitism for 1,700 years. Our traditions relate to Easter, the church calendar and the early councils in different ways, so this guide invites each of us to describe what we were taught and notice what this history adds, rather than to defend or accuse.

### A Shared Date <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#a-shared-date}

*The earliest Gentile believers celebrated differently from their Jewish neighbors but chose to celebrate on the same day.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes describe early Gentile *Pascha* (from the Hebrew *Pesach*, "Passover") as mainly a celebration of the Lord's Supper, with leavened bread and no week of avoiding leaven. It was not a Seder. Yet the Quartodecimans (Latin *quartodecimani*, "fourteeners") kept it on Nisan 14, the same date as the Jewish community. Brent sums it up: the practice was different, but the date was shared, and that shared date was a deliberate sign of unity.[^3] Polycarp kept this date because he had seen the Apostle John do it, and to keep it he had to ask the Jewish community when it fell. When he and Anicetus of Rome disagreed about the date, they agreed to disagree.[^4] What do you notice about the kind of relationship a shared date created, and about the way Polycarp and Anicetus handled their disagreement?

*Read Acts 15:1–21 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes explain that the Jerusalem Council decided Gentile believers did not have to become Jews or take on the full "yoke of the Torah," yet many still worshiped alongside Jewish believers and probably timed their observance to match.[^5] As you listen to the passage, what does the council ask of the Gentiles, and what does it leave open? How might that combination have made room for the choice Polycarp later made? (If you read Acts 15 this past week, include the detail that surprised you.)

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes compare Polycarp's situation to an immigrant family that relies on relatives back home to know when a festival falls, so the calendar becomes a reason to stay in touch.[^6] What calendar, habit or shared rhythm in your own life keeps you connected to people you might otherwise drift away from?

</div>

### From Dependence to Distance <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#from-dependence-to-distance}

*Over about two centuries, a shared date turned into a deliberate separation.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes trace several turning points. After the Bar Kokhba revolt (c. 132–135 CE), Rome expelled all Jews, including Jewish believers, from Jerusalem, and the church lost its Jewish "mother congregation." Around 190 CE, Victor of Rome tried to excommunicate the churches of Asia Minor for keeping Nisan 14. The Councils of Arles (314) and Nicaea (325) then made Rome's method official: the Sunday after the first full moon after the vernal equinox, always after Jewish Passover, a method that needed no input from the Jewish community.[^7] Walking through these steps together, at which point does the relationship seem to change most, and what made that moment so decisive?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Rudolph compares the second-century dispute to how upset people would be today if someone moved Easter. This was the most important day of the church's year, and sharing it was a large part of what held the church together.[^8] What practice in your church or tradition would cause that kind of outcry if someone changed it, and what does your answer reveal about what holds your community together?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Constantine's letter after Nicaea, preserved by Eusebius, calls on the church: "Let us then have nothing in common with the detestable Jewish crowd, for we have received from our Savior a different way." It also repeats the charge of deicide.[^9] Rudolph's main point is that Nicaea's decision on Christ's divinity has echoed for 1,700 years, and so has its antisemitically motivated decision on the calendar. The notes compare the honesty he is asking for to nations that keep days of remembrance for their own historical wrongs, honoring what was good while still naming what was wrong.[^10] Our traditions hold Nicaea, its creed and the church calendar in different ways. Without defending or condemning any of them, how do you hold together gratitude for what the church got right and honesty about what it got wrong?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Rudolph says the "nothing in common" mindset still shows up today in ideas such as: Christians shouldn't visit synagogues, Messianic Jews should assimilate into the church, or the "Christian God" is different from the God of Israel.[^11] Where, if anywhere, have you heard echoes of these ideas, and what would it take to notice and gently correct them in your own circles?

</div>

### Doing Jewish Things with Jewish People <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#doing-jewish-things-with-jewish-people}

*Rudolph closes with lessons that move this history into the present.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read John 10:17–18 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Rudolph's first lesson is to put the deicide charge to rest. He notes that Vatican II's *Nostra Aetate* rejected the idea that all Jews are guilty of Jesus' death, that Romans and others took part, and that those involved did not represent all Jews for all time. He then points to John 10:18 and to Jesus dying for everyone's sins.[^12] What do you notice in these verses about who has power over Jesus' life? How does that change the way the question "who killed Jesus?" ought to be asked?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Rudolph is concerned about Gentile-only Seders in some Hebrew Roots circles, seeing them as a new form of worship cut off from any dependence on the Jewish community. He raises a question about the Seder's covenantal language: can a Gentile say "He brought *us* out of Egypt"? His recommendation is to join a Messianic Jewish congregation's community Seder or a Jewish friend's Seder, since having guests is a traditional part of the meal.[^13] Some of us may have attended or hosted a church Seder that meant a lot to us. What does Rudolph's concern help you see about those experiences, and what would change if Jewish people were at the table?

*Read Ephesians 2:11–22 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes summarize Rudolph's lessons as rejecting the deicide charge, fighting antisemitism actively, and following the principle "do Jewish things with Jewish people." They also offer practical examples: noticing each year how the Western, Orthodox and Jewish dates line up, looking for a community Seder at a local Messianic synagogue, and correcting antisemitic tropes online with good information.[^14] In light of Ephesians 2, what is one concrete step you could take this year toward the interdependence Rudolph describes?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> What looks like a technical question, when to celebrate Easter, carries the story of the church's relationship with the Jewish people. At first, Gentile believers gladly relied on their Jewish brothers and sisters to know when to celebrate. After Jerusalem fell, Rome became the center, the disagreement grew bitter, and at Nicaea the calendar was cut loose from Jewish reckoning in language that has fed antisemitism ever since. Rudolph does not ask us to condemn the church. He asks us to remember honestly, reject the deicide charge, protect our Jewish neighbors, and, where we share in Israel's feasts, celebrate *with* Jewish people.[^15]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one thing this history changes about how you will approach Easter (or Passover) next spring?

</div>
