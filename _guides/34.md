### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We reviewed the Torah as one story of partnership and of two kingdoms, Empire and Shalom, and left Israel at the Jordan as a kingdom of priests who barely knew their God.

**This week:** Israel enters the land under Joshua. Marty calls this the hardest book in the Bible for him, and the episode models how to wrestle with it honestly without forcing a quick answer.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The book of Joshua contains accounts of the conquest of Canaan that are among the most morally difficult passages in Scripture. Marty does not offer one tidy solution. Instead he lays out several perspectives that help him wrestle: the ancient "conquest narrative" style of writing, a close look at what God does and does not say in the text, the Hebrew word *herem*, the horrific practices of the Canaanites and the long patience of God, and the way Joshua differs from other ancient conquerors.[^1] He also notes that the stories the book develops most fully are rescue stories, Rahab and the Gibeonites.[^2]

The episode also draws two practical lessons from Joshua: the three-way encouragement of *chazak va'amatz* ("be strong and courageous") in chapter 1, and standing stones that prompt people to tell the story of God's faithfulness.[^3] Our group comes from several traditions, and people of good faith read these chapters differently. The aim tonight is not to agree on one resolution but to listen well and to name what each perspective helps with and what it leaves unresolved.

### Strong, Courageous, and Remembering <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#strong-courageous-and-remembering}

*Before the battles begin, Joshua's opening chapters are about encouragement and memory.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Joshua 1:1–18 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** If you did the preparation, share what you found. The notes describe *chazak va'amatz* ("be strong and courageous") as a refrain that moves three ways: God to Joshua, Joshua to the people, and the people back to Joshua.[^4] Look at verses 6, 7, 9 and 18. Who says it, to whom, and what is different about the last time it appears?

*Read Joshua 4:1–7 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes explain that standing stones (*masava*) carried no inscription, unlike a stele. They were meant to look out of place and prompt observers to ask "What happened here?"[^5] Marty keeps his high school football belt as a reminder of the time God redirected his life from law school toward Bible college and ministry.[^6] If you brought an object, or thought of one, what is your "standing stone," and what story does it make you tell?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes suggest that the greatest takeaway from Joshua "may not be about military conquest at all, but about the power of mutual encouragement and unified support in facing the challenges ('mountains') we each encounter."[^7] In which direction does encouragement flow least in your life right now: from God to you, from you to others, or from others back to the people who lead you? What would it take to strengthen that direction this week?

</div>

### Wrestling with the Hardest Story <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#wrestling-with-the-hardest-story}

*The episode refuses easy answers and offers several lenses instead. Take them one at a time and notice what each does.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Joshua 6:1–5 and Joshua 6:15–19 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes point out that only Jericho has a direct divine command for total destruction, tied to the principle of firstfruits, and that many other actions are reported as Joshua saying "the Lord says" without the narrative confirming that God spoke.[^8] The book "has no problem clearly indicating when God speaks directly."[^9] Compare the two readings: what does God say in verses 2–5, and what does Joshua add in verses 17–19? What do you notice about who speaks and what they say?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The episode offers several perspectives side by side, and Marty says he does not agree entirely with any single resource he recommends.[^10] Here they are, as the notes record them:

- **Genre.** Ancient kings recorded victories on steles with dramatic embellishment, and Joshua may be using that recognized "conquest narrative" style. The notes add that "there is no archaeological evidence supporting a literal conquest" and that this "doesn't make it less 'true' in the sense of inspired Scripture."[^11]
- **Consecration.** The Hebrew word often translated "destroy" is frequently *herem* ("consecrate"). Clean things were given to the sanctuary, unclean things destroyed, and Israel may have read "consecrate" as "destroy" because they saw pagans as unclean, an assumption the New Testament later challenges.[^12]
- **Accommodation.** God met people inside their worldview, where war language was universal, and "gradually moved humanity forward ethically and morally."[^13]
- **Context and patience.** The Canaanites practiced child sacrifice and exploitative fertility cults, and God waited roughly 450 years after Genesis 15:16 before judgment.[^14]

Which perspective helps you most, and which raises the most questions for you? You do not need to choose one, and you do not need to persuade anyone else.

*Read Genesis 15:16 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes name the tension sharply: "We ask 'How can God allow such evil?' and when God finally acts, we ask 'How can God authorize such violence?'"[^15] They also say we face the same tension today when we cry out for God to act against injustice and then question his methods.[^16] Where do you feel the pull of each of those two questions, and what does it mean to you that the episode leaves them partly open?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes observe that Joshua behaves differently from other ancient conquerors: no taunting, removing bodies by nightfall as Torah required, and keeping a covenant even with people who deceived him.[^17] How does that observation sit alongside the harder passages for you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes describe the episode as modeling "sitting with discomfort and allowing questions to remain open," and say that "Faith can coexist with honest uncertainty about how to interpret challenging passages."[^18] When you meet a text you cannot resolve, what is your instinct (to explain it, avoid it, or stay with it), and what would honest wrestling look like in a group like ours, where we come from different traditions?

</div>

### Redemption at the Center <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#redemption-at-the-center}

*The stories Joshua tells slowly are rescue stories.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Joshua 11:16–20 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes report that some scholars read Joshua as a large chiasm, a structure that mirrors around a central point, and that if so, the center would be Joshua 11:19.[^19] That verse sits in a paragraph about destruction, yet it names the one group that made peace and was saved through covenant. The notes add that the conquest accounts are often brief while the rescue stories of Rahab and the Gibeonites are developed in detail.[^20] What do you notice about verse 19 in its paragraph, and how would it change your reading of Joshua if the book's true center were covenant faithfulness rather than conquest?

*If time allows, skim Joshua 9:3–21 together.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say "No other ancient conqueror would spare a vassal who had deceived them."[^21] Why do you think Joshua kept his word to the Gibeonites when he had been tricked?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q11:** If the stories that get the most attention in Joshua are stories of rescue, what story of rescue in your own life deserves more of your attention, and who needs to hear it?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Joshua opens with God, a leader and a people all telling each other to be strong and courageous, and with twelve stones set up so that children will ask what happened. Then it takes us into stories that trouble many careful readers, including Marty. The episode does not hand us one answer. It offers lenses (genre, consecration, accommodation, the long patience of God, the restraint of Joshua) and admits that the tension remains. It also notices that the book lingers on rescue: a prostitute in the wall of Jericho and a nation that tricked its way into a covenant Joshua refused to break.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one thing you want to keep wrestling with from tonight, or one thing that gave you courage?

</div>
