### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Nicodemus met Jesus at night and left saying he was "standing on holy ground," while Matthew walked away from his tax booth at the words "Follow me" and Jesus told a scandalized Simon to "get used to different."[^1]

**This week:** In the season one finale, Jesus reveals himself to a Samaritan woman at Jacob's well, and Nicodemus comes to the edge of following him.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The finale opens with Jacob trying to explain his God to a Canaanite: unlike other gods, this one keeps his promises, even if it takes generations, and "We didn't choose Him, He chose us." The heart of the episode is John 4. The show gives the Samaritan woman a painful backstory to humanize her, and Marty adds a less popular reading in which she may have been a Samaritan priestess. The hosts also notice that Jesus speaks openly about who he is in Samaria, while asking for silence among his own people in Galilee, and they connect this to what the notes call the "Messianic secret."[^2]

Several other threads close out the season. Nicodemus leaves a bag of gold for Jesus but cannot bring himself to join, and Jesus says quietly, "You came so close." Matthew tells the Roman soldier Gaius, "I'm surrendering too." Eden stays home while Simon follows Jesus, and Jesus tells her, "I see you." The hosts also reflect honestly on how people of different theological camps, themselves included, can miss what is happening because they assume too quickly.[^3]

### He Chose Us <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#he-chose-us}

*The season ends where Israel's story began: with a God who chooses a people and keeps his word.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The opening scene is set in Canaan, where Jacob tries to explain his God to a Canaanite. The difference, as the notes put it, is that the pagan gods make promises they do not keep, while Yahweh keeps his promises "even if it takes generations." Jacob's statement, "We didn't choose Him, He chose us," would have sounded revolutionary in that world; Marty draws on Thomas Cahill's *Gifts of the Jews* to stress how "wacky this whole worldview would have been."[^4] Why might "He chose us" have sounded so strange to Jacob's neighbor? How does it sound to you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes name a tension in the episode between "human understanding of God's timeline ('sometimes it takes generations') and God's immediate provision."[^5] Is there a promise or hope in your life that seems to be on the "generations" timeline? How do you keep trusting while you wait?

</div>

### The Woman at the Well <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#the-woman-at-the-well}

*Jesus has his longest recorded conversation about who he is with a Samaritan woman at a well.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read John 4:7–30 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Listen for who asks the questions, how the woman responds to Jesus at each turn, and what Jesus reveals about himself. What do you notice? Where does the conversation surprise you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes observe that Jesus handles his identity differently by setting. In Galilee he tells the healed leper not to tell anyone, because "the religious world is not ready to have this conversation." In Samaria, when the woman says she will tell everyone, he answers, "I was hoping you would." The notes suggest that Samaria does not carry the same "religious baggage," where messianic claims would immediately trigger political and religious confrontation.[^6] Why might the setting change how openly Jesus speaks? The notes apply this today: "In highly 'churched' contexts where people have been wounded by religion, the message may need different framing."[^7] Where have you seen that to be true?

*Read John 4:39–42 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The show takes the traditional view of the woman but gives her a backstory: a husband who abused her, and a man who "smelled like oranges" whom she sabotaged. The notes call this "the humanizing power of backstory" and stress "curiosity over condemnation." Marty also presents a scholarly alternative: the woman may have been a Samaritan priestess, with the "five husbands" representing the five books of the Torah, which could explain her theological knowledge and why the whole town listened to her.[^8] The hosts acknowledge that the show itself follows the traditional reading. What does each reading bring into focus about her and about Jesus? Some of this backstory touches painful experiences, so keep the discussion gentle.

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Marty turns a hard question on himself. He wonders whether progressives might write off Jesus's conversation with the woman as "demeaning" without listening deeply enough to have the transformative encounter, and he admits to being "always out to catch the patriarchy." The same dynamic, the notes say, runs both ways: conservatives quickly labeling progressives, progressives quickly assuming oppressive intent.[^9] When have your own assumptions, from whatever direction, kept you from really hearing a person or a passage? What helped you listen again?

</div>

### Close, but Not Yet <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#close-but-not-yet}

*Three people respond to Jesus in three very different ways.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** After receiving honor from the religious leadership, Nicodemus secretly leaves a purple bag of gold coins for Jesus and the disciples but cannot bring himself to join them publicly. Jesus says quietly, "You came so close," and Nicodemus weeps bitterly. The notes observe that "knowing the right choice doesn't make it easy," and that Jesus's response "isn't anger but sadness."[^10] What do you notice about how Jesus responds? How does it compare with the way communities often treat people who hesitate?

*Read John 19:38–42 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Among its suggestions for further study, the notes point to tracking Nicodemus through John's Gospel, chapters 3, 7 and 19, "to see his eventual public stand."[^11] What do you notice about what Nicodemus does in this passage? What does it mean to you that his story does not end at "so close"?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** Two other responses close the season. Matthew tells Gaius, "I'm surrendering too," comparing his surrender to God's Kingdom with Gaius's people surrendering to Rome, and the notes describe this as recognizing something "even bigger and better."[^12] And Eden, who stays home while Simon follows Jesus, hears Jesus say, "I see you." The notes read this as an attempt "to honor different expressions of calling rather than prescribe one model," and as a reminder not to honor only "visible, public ministry while taking support roles for granted."[^13] Whose unseen faithfulness would you like to say "I see you" to? And what would surrender look like for you in this season?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The first season of The Chosen ends with a God who chooses people and keeps his promises across generations. At a Samaritan well, Jesus reveals himself to a woman others had written off, and she brings her whole town to meet him. Nicodemus comes so close and cannot yet take the step, and Jesus meets his hesitation with sadness rather than anger. Matthew surrenders, and Eden is seen. The show's approach, the notes say, is to treat biblical characters "more as humans than heroes," which makes their faith "more remarkable rather than less."[^14]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one step toward Jesus you have come close to taking, and what would help you take it?

</div>
