### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** In Matthew 19, Jesus took the strict side of the divorce debate for a loving reason, protecting women who could be discarded, and kept making room for children and challenging the wealthy, all resting on our willingness to trust the story.

**This week:** The parable of the workers in the vineyard asks whether we can rejoice when God is generous to people we think have not earned it, and the rest of Matthew 20 shows disciples still learning what greatness means.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode argues that the parable of the workers is deliberately provocative. The landowner pays the latest workers first, in full view of those who worked all day, and the episode says this was not poor planning but a choice meant to expose and transform our sense of fairness. The phrase "the heat of the day" connects the parable to the relationship between Jews and Gentiles, and Isaiah 61 redefines the firstborn's "double portion" as the joy of seeing everyone blessed.

The second half follows Jesus toward Jerusalem. James and John's mother asks for seats of honor, and the episode offers a more sympathetic reading of the brothers' "we can" than many of us have heard. Jesus teaches that greatness is servanthood, and two blind men see who Jesus is before they can see anything else.

### The Workers in the Vineyard <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-workers-in-the-vineyard}

*The landowner's order of payment is the point of the story.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 20:1–16 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** As you hear the parable, pay attention to the order of events. The episode notes that God "could have avoided the conflict by paying in reverse order," and that the deliberate choice "reveals that God wants to expose and transform human selfishness."[^1] What do you notice about who gets paid first and who is made to watch? What would have been lost if the landowner had paid the all-day workers first and sent them home?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> One of the four plain lessons the notes draw from the parable is that "The Kingdom of Heaven is not fair but incredibly benevolent."[^2] What is the difference between "fair" and "benevolent" as you have experienced them? Which one do you instinctively want God to be?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The episode says "bearing the burden in the heat of the day" was rabbinic language for the Jewish people carrying the weight of Torah observance on behalf of the world. The notes are careful to add that Jews did not generally see Torah as a burden but as joyful; the phrase acknowledged the extra responsibility they carried. Jesus' use of it signals that the late-arriving workers represent Gentiles coming to worship the God of Israel.[^3] How does reading the parable this way change who you picture standing in each group of workers?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes suggest heaven may only be heavenly for those transformed to celebrate God's generosity: "If we arrive in heaven only to see 'those people' (whoever that is for us) receiving the same or better reward, our response reveals whether we have been transformed."[^4] The notes apply this to political, racial, denominational, and other divisions, and to deathbed conversions.[^5] Who are "those people" for you? What would it take for your heart to leap with joy at their blessing?

</div>

### The Double Portion <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#the-double-portion}

*The firstborn's blessing turns out to be a calling to bless.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 61:1–7 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes point to Isaiah 61:5, where foreigners work Israel's fields and vineyards, and say that what "initially sounds like a curse is actually a blessing": when the Year of the Lord's favor arrives, everyone is included.[^6] Listen for the double portion in verse 7. The episode says the firstborn (*bechor*) received a double portion and greater responsibility, and that Isaiah 61 redefines the double portion as the joy of seeing the blind see, prisoners released, foreigners welcomed, and everyone having enough.[^7] What do you notice in the passage about who is blessed and how? Why might this definition of blessing disappoint some who hear it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode also hears a hint of Genesis 18, where Abraham sat "in the heat of the day" recovering from circumcision and showed radical hospitality to three unexpected visitors. The notes connect this to the Abrahamic calling to bless all nations.[^8] Read Genesis 18:1–8. How does Abraham's welcome of strangers shed light on the landowner's welcome of late workers?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes say the parable "fundamentally contradicts prosperity theology," because the double portion is "not personal wealth but the joy of seeing all blessed."[^9] Where in your own life do you most want blessing to mean more for you? What would it look like this week to find your joy in someone else's blessing?

</div>

### A Cup, a Request, and Two Blind Men <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#a-cup-a-request-and-two-blind-men}

*On the road to Jerusalem, disciples learn greatness and outsiders see clearly.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 20:20–28 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The episode argues that James and John's answer, "we can," is exactly right for *talmidim* (disciples), who believe they can be like their rabbi, much as Peter did when he stepped out of the boat. Jesus affirms that they will drink his cup. The notes say "The issue is not their confidence but their mother's request about seating arrangements."[^10] How does this reading compare with what you have been taught about this scene? Then Jesus contrasts rulers who lord it over others with a greatness measured by servanthood.[^11] Where do you see "jockeying for position" in groups you belong to, including this one?

*Read Matthew 20:29–34 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes suggest that if Jesus was viewed by some as a *mamzer* (a person of illegitimate birth), the blind men's cry of "Son of David" affirms his royal legitimacy despite the stigma. They "possess spiritual sight even before physical healing."[^12] What do you notice about what the blind men see that others on the road do not?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** Jesus asks the blind men, "What do you want me to do for you?" The notes connect this to Marty's work with homeless people: "Genuine relationship requires listening rather than imposing our assumptions about others' needs."[^13] Who in your life might need you to ask that question rather than assume the answer?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The landowner pays the last first, on purpose, so that the all-day workers have to decide whether they can rejoice. The episode hears in "the heat of the day" the story of Jews and Gentiles, and in Isaiah 61 a double portion that is the joy of seeing everyone blessed. On the road to Jerusalem, James and John are right to believe they can drink their rabbi's cup, but greatness turns out to mean service. And two blind men see who Jesus is before they see anything else. The question the parable leaves us with is not whether God is fair, but whether we have become people who celebrate his generosity.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: whose blessing will you practice celebrating this week?

</div>
