### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** In Psalm 13 we prayed "How long?" with a psalmist who felt mislaid by God, and we saw anguish and trust held side by side until the psalm turned toward God's steadfast love.[^1]

**This week:** Josh leads Brent and Reed through Psalm 23, asking us to hear the most familiar psalm in the Bible "with fresh ears."[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Psalm 23 is read at funerals, memorized by children and printed in literature anthologies, and that familiarity can create a "lullaby effect" that keeps us from hearing what the text says. Josh uses Robert Alter's literary translation and points out how the psalm shifts halfway through, from speaking about God to speaking to God. He then shows that the psalm is a chiasm, a mirror-image structure, with verse 1 matching verse 6, verse 2 matching verse 5, and verse 3 matching verse 4.[^3]

Following that structure, Josh asks what happens if the sheep never leaves the picture. On that reading, the house of the Lord is the sanctuary, the table is an altar, the oil anoints a sacrifice, and the overflowing cup holds blood. The psalm becomes a picture of trusting God so completely that a person can give their life for their enemies without feeling any break in their relationship with God. Josh is clear that this reading is about self-giving love and trust and does not encourage physical self-harm.[^4] Expect the discussion to be both beautiful and unsettling.

**Opening Question:** Brent's grandfather, who was not religious, asked a minister to read Psalm 23 to him near death, and Reed memorized it with his children.[^5] Where have you heard or said Psalm 23 most often in your life? What does it usually do for you when you hear it?

### Hearing a Familiar Psalm Freshly <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#hearing-a-familiar-psalm-freshly}

*Familiarity can hide meaning, so the episode slows down and changes translations.*
{: .guide-note}

*Sit in silence for one minute and breathe slowly. Then read Psalm 23 aloud in the least familiar translation anyone has brought.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The episode suggests two ways to hear the psalm fresh: read an unfamiliar translation, and pause for a short breathing meditation first. Alter's translation changes several familiar phrases. "He restoreth my soul" becomes "my life he brings back," "I will fear no evil" becomes "I fear no harm," and "goodness and mercy shall follow me" becomes "let but goodness and kindness pursue me."[^6] What did you hear this time that you had not noticed before? Which of Alter's changes alters the psalm most for you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The psalm has two halves. Verses 1–3 speak about God in the third person; verses 4–6 speak to God in the second person. The imagery also seems to move from a sheep's life to a person's life, with a table, oil, a cup and enemies, and the second half feels dreamlike and placeless. Yet the "rod and staff" in verse 4 are still shepherd's tools, and Brent and Reed both pictured the table sitting in the meadow.[^7] Look at your Bible. Where exactly do you see the shift happen? What makes you think the two halves belong together?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe the "lullaby effect": a text heard many times starts to work on rhythm and feeling rather than meaning.[^8] Besides Psalm 23, which passage have you heard so often that you may have stopped hearing it? What could you do this week to hear it again?

</div>

### A Sheep All the Way Through <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#a-sheep-all-the-way-through}

*Following the psalm's mirrored structure leads to a hidden and demanding reading.*
{: .guide-note}

*Have three pairs of readers read the matching verses aloud: verses 1 and 6, then verses 2 and 5, then verses 3 and 4.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** In the chiasm, verses 1 and 6 are the only two places the divine name YHWH appears, and both are communal: a shepherd implies a flock, and a house (*bayit*) implies a family. Verses 2 and 5 pair food with food and water with drink, both deliberately exaggerated. Verses 3 and 4 are the only three-line units, and in both the speaker comes close to death and God sustains them.[^9] What connections do you notice between each pair as you hear them together? What changes when you read the psalm as a mirror rather than a straight line?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Josh asks what happens if the sheep never goes away. Read from the outside in: a sheep in God's house is there to be sacrificed; the table is the altar; oil was used with offerings; blood was collected in basins and here overflows; and goodness and kindness "pursue" the sheep, a verb that usually describes hunting someone down. The enemies sit "opposite" the psalmist, from *neged*, the same root as in Genesis 2:18, so they may be across the table rather than far away.[^10] How does this reading sit with you? What does it uncover in the psalm, and what, if anything, makes you resist it?

*Read Romans 5:10 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** On this reading the sacrifice is offered so that enemies can be reconciled, their opposition overcome by a meal rather than by violence. Reed notes that the enemies are not defeated by the sword; the strategy is to "sit down and eat," which points to the Eucharist and to Jesus eating with tax collectors, sinners and his betrayer. Josh argues the psalm skips disorientation because the disorientation happens in the reader.[^11] Who sits "opposite" you right now, at work, in your family, or in your community? What would sitting down to eat with them, literally or figuratively, look like? Share only what you choose.

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Reed imagines David writing this late in life, after decades of enemies. The notes set Psalm 23 beside Psalm 137, which ends by blessing those who dash enemy children against rocks, and Psalm 139, which asks God to slay the wicked. Wanting vengeance is natural, and the Psalms make room for it, but growing in the Spirit moves us toward overcoming enemies by a shared meal and finally by giving ourselves for them.[^12] How has your own view of the people who opposed you changed over the years? What changed it?

</div>

### Many Long Days <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#many-long-days}

*If the sheep is sacrificed, how can it dwell in God's house "for many long days"?*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Reed asks exactly that question, and Josh gives two answers. The burnt offering rises to God as a "pleasing aroma," which can also be read as "a spirit of rest." And the peace offering (*shelamim*) had to be eaten quickly at the sanctuary, so families shared it, and Josh imagines a well-off worshiper sharing a meal with someone who brought a sin offering they could not eat. Former strangers, or enemies, become companions, and the animal's "days" continue in the feasting its death made possible.[^13] What does this suggest to you about how a life given away keeps going?

*Read 2 Corinthians 4:10 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** In verse 3 God brings back the speaker's life "for his name's sake," and Josh says the Hebrew blurs whose life is in view. As God's love becomes part of our identity, self-giving love is no sudden hardship but the same pattern continuing: God saved my life because he loves me, and now I get to give my life to redeem someone else. Even at the altar, the sheep still says "you are with me."[^14] What does Paul's phrase add to this picture? Where are you being asked to give something of yourself right now?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Reed says that pursuing restorative rather than retributive justice for enemies takes something from you. The psalm says "my life he brings back," and the cup keeps overflowing, like a stream.[^15] When self-giving love wears you out, what has God used to bring your life back? What could you do this week to make room for that restoration?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Psalm 23 is a beautiful picture of God's care, and it may also be harder than we thought. Followed through its mirrored structure, the sheep goes into God's house, the table becomes an altar, and the cup overflows with the offering's blood, all "opposite" enemies who are reconciled not by the sword but by a shared meal. The psalmist feels no break in trust, because giving one's life is a continuation of the meadows and still waters. This turns a funeral psalm into an invitation to love our enemies, trusting that God keeps bringing our life back and filling our cup.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, name the line of Psalm 23 you will carry this week and what it now means to you.

</div>
