### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** At the center of the garden story is shame, and God's question "Who told you that you were naked?" Bearing God's image includes knowing when to say "enough."

**This week:** Cain and Abel. Marty and Brent read the story through Cain's name, ask why God would seem to prefer one son's gift, and hear in God's words to Cain a God whose view of people has not changed, even after failure.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Cain's name means "acquired," a word used for something you could not get on your own. As a farmer, Cain depends on rain, sun, and soil that he cannot control. That dependence can lead to grateful trust or to anxious fear. The hosts are honest about a problem in the story: what kind of father would prefer one child's gift over another's? They conclude that the story "has to be about something else."

Their answer is fear. When Cain sees his brother receive favor, fear turns God's love into a limited supply and his brother into a competitor. God responds with a question and an invitation: sin is crouching at the door, "but you must master it." The notes draw a striking conclusion: "God's position on humanity has not changed but humanity's position on themselves has."

### A Name and a Problem <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#a-name-and-a-problem}

*Cain's name and his work both speak of dependence.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 4:1–7 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes explain that in Hebrew culture, "A name isn't just a name... your name was your essence and your destiny and it was something that you would live out," in either a positive or a negative sense.[^1] Cain means "acquired," a word used not for something you do on your own but for something "likely given to you."[^2] Look at what Eve says when Cain is born and at the work Cain takes up. What details in these verses connect to the idea of receiving rather than achieving?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode illustrates "acquisition" with Thomas Edison: he did not make glass, metal, or electricity from nothing, but combined what already existed. All human creativity "builds on God's foundational provision and the work of others."[^3] What is something you are proud of having accomplished? What did you "acquire" along the way that you could not have produced yourself?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The hosts name the problems honestly. How does Cain know what to bring? "What kind of father would prefer a gift from one child over the other especially when they were not asked to bring gifts?" Marty pictures two children bringing drawings home, one artistic and one mediocre; a good father puts both on the refrigerator. The notes conclude, "This story has to be about something else."[^4] How do you usually handle a passage where God seems to act unfairly? What do you make of the hosts' choice to name the problem out loud rather than explain it away?

</div>

### Fear and the Spiritual Bank Account <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#fear-and-the-spiritual-bank-account}

*Fear turns a brother into a competitor and God's love into a limited supply.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** If fear enters Cain's story, he may worry that what he brings "may not be enough." Seeing his younger brother win favor, he begins to see Abel "as a threat." The notes say, "Fear ends up being the antithesis of trust," and the question underneath becomes: "Am I loved enough? Am I accepted?"[^5] Where have you seen someone else's blessing feel like your loss, in a family, a workplace, or a church?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes put it plainly: "God's love isn't quantitative," and there is no "spiritual bank account" where one person's blessing reduces another's.[^6] Why is that so hard to believe in practice, even for people who affirm it in theory? What would change in your relationships if you believed it fully?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes list modern forms of scarcity thinking: colleagues as threats, sibling rivalry, competition in church for recognition or leadership, and comparing our lives to "others' highlight reels."[^7] Which of these do you find yourself most prone to, and what usually sets it off?

</div>

### You Are Not a Beast <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#you-are-not-a-beast}

*God's words to Cain reveal how God sees people when they fail.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** God asks Cain, "Why are you angry and downcast?" The notes argue that this question "only makes sense if Cain still has the capacity to do right and be accepted," and they conclude: "God's position on humanity has not changed but humanity's position on themselves has."[^8] Looking back over the story so far, the notes observe that the text tells us humanity now has shame, downcast faces, and anger, but "has not told us that the nature of humanity has changed at all."[^9] Our traditions teach different things about sin and human nature, and this reading will sound familiar to some of us and surprising to others. What do you think each view is trying to guard? What does this reading of Genesis 4 invite you to notice in the text itself?

*Read Genesis 4:7 aloud again, slowly.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes summarize God's word to Cain this way: "Cain, you are not a beast. Sin is crouching at your door and you are intended to master it."[^10] They describe sin here as an action, "something you do," crouching outside like a beast that "can be mastered."[^11] What kind of picture is this? What does it say about what Cain is capable of, and about what God expects of him?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The story "ends in tragedy," yet it "defines God's position on people when they make mistakes."[^12] How can a story end badly and still reveal something hopeful about God?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes stress that transformation happens through daily right choices: tomorrow offers a new opportunity to "do what is right" regardless of yesterday's failures.[^13] What is one "beast" crouching at your door right now (a fear, a resentment, a comparison), and what would mastering it look like tomorrow?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Cain's name says that everything he has was received. Fear twisted that dependence into scarcity, until his brother looked like a threat and God's love looked like a limited supply. God responded with a question and a word of confidence: sin is crouching at the door, and you can master it. The story ends in tragedy, but it shows us a God whose view of people has not changed, and who still believes we can do what is right.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, where do you want to trust that there is enough of God's love to go around this week?

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 4 — His Bow in the Clouds](/guides/4)** (34:00): The flood story in Genesis 6–9, read alongside ancient flood stories like the Epic of Gilgamesh. The hosts trace a mirror structure centered on "But God remembered Noah," show how the story echoes creation, and explain why the rainbow is really a "bow" pointed away from the earth. It reveals a God who knows when to stop destroying, just as he knew when to stop creating.
