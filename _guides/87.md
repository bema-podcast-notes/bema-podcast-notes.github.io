### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We considered Luke as a Jewish convert who wrote an "ordered" Gospel, possibly arranged to accompany the synagogue's weekly readings from the Torah and the Prophets.

**This week:** We meet the fourth voice. John, the pastor to Asia, writes for a community where Jews and Gentiles have been grafted into one family.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The notes describe John as the "Pastor to Asia," ministering in the great Greek cities of western Turkey such as Ephesus, where perhaps a fifth of the population was Jewish, mostly Hellenistic and assimilated to varying degrees.[^1] John did not write to fill gaps left by the other Gospels; he wrote for a blended audience of Hellenistic Jews and Gentiles. Marty once called this audience "blended" and now prefers "grafted," the image of Gentiles grafted into an existing family tree.[^2]

The episode's showcase is John's opening. Greek readers would hear *logos* ("word"), a philosopher's term for the force that holds the universe together; Jewish readers would hear Torah, which was said to exist before creation. Both would agree with verse 1, and both would be startled when verse 2 calls the Word "He."[^3] The seven "I am" statements work the same way, answering pagan gods and claiming what Torah represented at the same time.[^4] The notes also point to sub-narratives such as new creation imagery in the garden resurrection scene, and to the principle of "Text to Context": the Gospel writers took the teaching of their Rabbi and set it in the context of their audiences.[^5]

### A Pastor to Asia <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#a-pastor-to-asia}

*John wrote for a particular people in a particular place, and he took his time getting to know them.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read 1 John 1:1–4 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** In the episode, Brent reads this passage as part of asking who John was.[^6] What does John stress about how he knows Jesus? Notice the senses he names and the purpose he gives for writing. What do those details tell you about the person writing the Gospel?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes explain that the Gospel writers did not wait decades because their memories were fading. They first earned their "Ph.D. in Jesus" and then spent years earning a "Ph.D." in their cultural context, so they could bring "Text to Context."[^7] Where are you being asked to translate your faith into a setting you are still learning (a workplace, a generation, a neighborhood, a family)? What would it look like to take the time to understand it well?

</div>

### In the Beginning Was the Word <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#in-the-beginning-was-the-word}

*The same paragraph says one thing to two audiences.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read John 1:1–5 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The original notes say that Greeks would agree with verse 1, since the *logos* was something that transcended the world and held everything together, but "When they read 'HE was with God in the beginning' that would be a completely brand new idea. Logos was NOT a person." Jews, who spoke of Torah existing before creation, would agree with verse 1 too, but "Torah was NOT a person."[^8] Read verses 1 and 2 again. What happens to each audience when the word "He" arrives? Which group do you think would find it harder to accept, and why?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q4:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> *Read John 20:11–18 aloud.* The notes describe a new creation sub-narrative in John: Jesus is raised in a garden, and a single woman, Mary, comes looking for him and speaks with someone she takes to be the gardener, which the notes connect to Eden.[^9] What details in this scene stand out to you? What does the echo of Genesis add to your understanding of the resurrection?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** *Read Romans 11:17–18 aloud.* Marty used to call John's audience a "blended" family of Jews and Greeks; he now uses "grafted," "implying Greeks have been grafted into an existing family tree."[^10] What changes when you move from "blended" to "grafted"? If you are grafted into an existing tree, how should that shape the way you relate to the Jewish roots of your faith and to the people grafted in alongside you?

</div>

### Seven I Am Statements <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#seven-i-am-statements}

*Each "I am" answers a pagan god and claims what Torah meant to the Jewish people.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes pair each statement with a Greek deity and a Jewish association with Torah:[^11]

- "I am the bread of life" (John 6): Demeter, goddess of grain / Torah as the bread of life
- "I am the light of the world" (John 8): Apollo / "a lamp unto my feet and a light unto my path" (Psalm 119:105)
- "I am the gate for the sheep" (John 10): Janus, god of doorways and transitions / Torah as the doorway to eternal life
- "I am the good shepherd" (John 10): Pan, overseer of the flocks / Torah as the voice of God that leads
- "I am the resurrection and the life" (John 11): Asclepius, the healer said to have been raised / Torah as the resurrection and eternal life
- "I am the way, the truth, and the life" (John 14): Athena / a Jewish reference to Torah as "the way, the truth, and the life"
- "I am the true vine" (John 15): Dionysus, god of wine / a Torah connection Marty assumes but has not yet found

Which pairing surprised you most? How does hearing a statement against its Greek and Jewish backgrounds change the way it sounds to you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** *Read John 14:6 aloud.* The original notes record Marty's view on this verse: "Jesus may or may not be the only way to God but that is not what Jesus is saying, according to Marty." The study notes describe Jesus as claiming to be the way to see God, the truth that both Athena and Torah pointed toward.[^12] How has John 14:6 been used in the churches you have known, and for what purposes? What do you make of the episode's reading? Where do you find yourself agreeing, and where do you hesitate?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes sum up John's message to Gentiles: "Everything you're looking for in Apollo, Demeter, Pan, Dionysus, and all these gods—you can find in Jesus."[^13] The study notes imagine John writing today and confronting consumerism, scientism, relativism and self-help culture.[^14] If John wrote to your neighborhood, which "I am" would he lead with? What false source of that same thing are you most tempted to lean on?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> John spent years among the Greeks and Hellenistic Jews of Asia before he wrote, and his Gospel shows it. With one opening paragraph he speaks to philosophers and to people who love Torah, and he surprises both with the news that the Word is a person. His "I am" statements tell Gentiles that what they sought in their gods is found in Jesus, and tell Jews that Jesus embodies what Torah always pointed toward. Four Gospels, four audiences: a Jew to Jews, a Jew to Romans, a convert to the synagogue, and a pastor to a grafted family. Each one brings the Text to its Context.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: which "I am" statement do you most need to hear this week, and why?

</div>
