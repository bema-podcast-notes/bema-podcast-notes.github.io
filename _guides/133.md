### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** At the empty tomb, the question the whole Bible had been asking was answered: the order of life gets the last word. On a mountain in Galilee, Jesus sent his followers out, doubters included, with the promise "I am with you always."[^1]

**This week:** Our final week of Session 3. Marty and Brent step back to view the whole biblical story from Genesis through the four Gospels, and we look back together over sixty-one weeks before sending one another out.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The capstone tells the whole biblical story as one narrative of God's partnership with humanity, framed as a confrontation between two kingdoms: Empire (fear, coercion, and worldly power) and Shalom (invitation, peace, and order from chaos).[^3] It begins with the foundational truth of Genesis 1 "that the story is good, which is the root of the Gospel itself," and moves through the Torah, the land and the kings, wisdom, and the prophets.[^4]

It then turns to the ground we covered this session: the "silent years" between the testaments, the five Jewish responses to Hellenism, and the distinct authors, audiences, and purposes of Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John.[^5] Finally, Marty contrasts rabbinic discipleship, following a rabbi "24 hours a day, 7 days a week, 365 days a year," with valuable church practices that may still leave "something is missing."[^6] Because this is our last meeting of Session 3, we will spend more time at the end looking back together and sending one another out.

### One Story, Genesis to John <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#one-story-genesis-to-john}

*The capstone reads every part of the Bible as one story with one central tension.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 1:31 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes say Genesis 1–11 establishes that creation is "good, sufficient, and trustworthy," a bedrock truth that "forms the root of the Gospel itself." They apply this directly: trusting the goodness and sufficiency of creation resists "the empire messaging that we need more, better, newer things to be happy," and means "starting from abundance rather than scarcity."[^7] What do you notice in this one verse? Looking back over the Gospels, where did you see Jesus acting as though the story is good and there is enough?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes describe Empire as fear, coercion, and force, "symbolized by an upraised fist," and Shalom as "invitation, leading by voice, peace, and bringing order to chaos." They offer some contrasts: "Empire asks: How can we force compliance? Shalom asks: How can we invite participation?" and "Empire consolidates power. Shalom distributes blessing."[^8] Across this session we watched Jesus enter Jerusalem from the east while Pilate entered from the west, "a literal collision of two kingdoms."[^9] Where in the Gospels did that collision become clearest for you, and where do you feel its pull in your own parenting, work, church, or politics?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say the family of Genesis "survives a lot of shipwrecks" but keeps returning to trust in God's story, showing that while God's people fail, "their faith doesn't have to fail." The capstone's closing note is that "humans may fail, but faith never does when people keep returning to trust in God's story."[^10] We saw the same pattern in Peter. Where has returning, rather than never failing, been the shape of your own faith?

</div>

### Walking Back Through Session 3 <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#walking-back-through-session-3}

*Before we close, we retrace the road we have walked since Week 1.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Here is Session 3 in nine movements:

- **The Silent Years:** after the exile, a return to the Text, with the synagogue, education starting at age five, and Scripture memorization at the center of Jewish life.[^11]
- **Five responses to Hellenism:** Sadducees, Herodians, Essenes, Zealots, and Pharisees, each with strengths and weaknesses we were invited to recognize in ourselves, and Galatians 4:4's "perfect timing" as the moment these responses were in full dialogue.[^12]
- **How the Gospels came to be:** "gospel" (*euangelion*) as an imperial term the writers took over to announce the Kingdom of God, and four Gospels written by specific authors to specific audiences.[^13]
- **The four Gospels:** Matthew the *mumzer* (outsider) Gospel, where "the Kingdom of Heaven eliminates the category of outsiders"; Mark the Roman Gospel, with the crucifixion as a coronation; Luke the ordered Gospel, possibly a companion to synagogue readings; and John the grafted Gospel, bringing Jews and Gentiles together.[^14]
- **Matthew's beginnings:** the small region around the Sea of Galilee where about 80% of Jesus' recorded ministry took place, two kingdoms clashing at his birth, John's *tavilah t'shuvah* (immersion of repentance), and Jesus bringing order out of chaos in the wilderness.[^15]
- **The Sermon on the Mount:** upside-down blessings, fulfilling Torah by interpreting and living it rightly, the "good eye" (*ayin tovah*), and orthopraxy (right practice) rather than just orthodoxy.[^16]
- **The Galilean ministry and the parables:** healing for lepers, centurions, and outsiders; crossing to "the other side"; a Kingdom that grows "small, gradual, but unstoppable"; and parables made "intentionally harder" so we would discover rather than simply receive.[^17]
- **The road to Jerusalem:** Caesarea Philippi, where the Kingdom goes on the offense against the "gates of hell"; the mountain of Transfiguration; and a triumphal entry and a week of confrontation with the chief priests.[^18]
- **The final week and the resurrection:** the passion read whole, a plot by a corrupt few rather than a people, two disciples who failed and two different endings, and an empty tomb that gives the order of life the last word.[^19]

Which movement most reshaped the way you read the Gospels, and which one still feels unfinished for you?

*Read Galatians 4:4 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The capstone says the silent years "aren't truly silent - they're formative, shaping the world Jesus entered."[^20] Early in this session we were asked which of the five groups we resonate with: the Sadducees' accommodation, the Herodians' compromise, the Essenes' separation, the Zealots' militancy, or the Pharisees' devotion to Torah.[^21] Sixty weeks later, which group do you recognize most in yourself, and has that answer changed since we began?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty names three common church practices, assimilation into community, weekly spiritual formation, and evangelism, as "Not True Discipleship but Still Valuable." The point, the notes say, "is not to stop these practices but to recognize that something is missing - the intensive, full-life commitment to following that Jesus actually called His disciples to pursue."[^22] Our churches have each built their own ways of forming people. Without judging any of them, what has formed you most deeply, and what do you think might be missing?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">35 min</span> {#closing}

> We began in the Silent Years, watching a people return to the Text after exile and answer Hellenism in five different ways. We learned how the Gospels came to be, and met four writers telling one story to four audiences. We followed Matthew from a genealogy full of outsiders to the Jordan and the wilderness, sat on the mountain for the Sermon, walked the shores of Galilee and across to the other side, and wrestled with parables that refused to explain themselves. We climbed to Caesarea Philippi and the mountain of glory, then followed Jesus to Jerusalem for one last week: the plot of a corrupt few, two disciples who failed, a cross, and an empty tomb. Through it all ran one question: Empire or Shalom? The resurrection gives the answer, and the risen Jesus sends us out to live it.[^23]
{: .guide-summary}

*Sit together in silence for 90 seconds before the next question.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Over these sixty-one weeks, how has this study changed the way you read the Bible, the way you understand it, and the way you relate to it? Take one of the three, or all of them.

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes suggest each Gospel speaks to different people: Matthew's *mumzer* emphasis for "someone concerned with justice," Mark for "someone from a power/honor cultural background," Luke for "someone seeking systematic understanding," and John for "someone navigating multiple cultural identities."[^24] Think of one person in your life. Which Gospel would you want to read with them, and why?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** Going around the room, finish this sentence in one sentence each: "As we leave the Gospels, I want to keep ____." Let everyone speak before anyone responds.

*To close, one person reads aloud: Go as people who trust that the story is good and that there is enough. Choose Shalom over Empire: invite rather than coerce, and distribute blessing rather than consolidate power. Remember the outsider whom the Kingdom will not leave outside. When you fail, return, as Peter did, and believe there is a tomorrow. Doubts and all, go and make disciples, for the risen Jesus keeps all authority and has promised, "I am with you always."*[^25]

</div>
