### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Session 6 opened with a look back at how BEMA learned to hold conviction with grace, and with a toddler reciting Genesis 1, learning goodness before sinfulness.[^1]

**This week:** The first guest of Session 6, campus minister Reed Dent, asks what we mean when we call the Bible "true," and why genre matters for the answer.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Reed Dent works with Campus Christian Fellowship at Truman State University. His central claim is "All facts are truthful, but not all truth is factual."[^2] He contrasts a newspaper report on 9/11 with a poem about it, a clinical manual with *Where the Wild Things Are*, and a dictionary definition of narcissism with the myth of Narcissus, to show that we already read genres differently everywhere except the Bible.[^3]

He draws on Frederick Buechner's distinction between fact and truth, seen with "the eyes of the heart," and applies it to Thomas meeting the risen Jesus.[^4] He also addresses the worry that this makes the Bible elitist, using Gregory the Great's picture of Scripture as a river shallow enough for a lamb and deep enough for an elephant, and he argues that "plain reading" is itself one view on a spectrum.[^5]

### A Library, Not a Book <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#a-library-not-a-book}

*Reed starts with everyday reading habits we already have and asks why we switch them off when we open the Bible.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Reed contrasts the Wall Street Journal's 9/11 report ("if these details are wrong, the article is false") with Scott Cairns' poem "September 11," which "cannot be judged by factual accuracy but resonates with truth about human experience."[^3] Take the example you shared in the opening. What kind of truth does it carry, and how would you go about testing whether it is true?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Reed offers two definitions of narcissism: an abstract dictionary definition and the myth of Narcissus, who falls in love with his own reflection. He asks his students, "Which of these portrayals of narcissism matters more to you?"[^6] How would you answer, and what does your answer say about how stories work on us?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** In Reed's thought experiment, someone 3,000 years from now finds two Blu-rays, *The Lord of the Rings* and Ken Burns' war documentary, and has to work out which is history and which is fiction. His conclusion: "it would be pretty presumptive of you to just say, 'well, I know exactly how these should be read.'"[^7] The notes list the Bible's genres as narrative, poetry, prophecy, wisdom, parable and apocalyptic.[^8] Think of a passage that has confused or troubled you. How might asking "what kind of writing is this?" before "did this happen?" change the way you come to it?

</div>

### The Eyes of the Heart <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-eyes-of-the-heart}

*Buechner's distinction between fact and truth gives us a way to read the moments when people meet God.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read John 20:24–29 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Buechner says a mountain's height is a fact, and its majesty is a truth seen with "the eyes of the heart." Applied to this scene, Thomas had known the facts of Jesus all along but now "saw the truth of Jesus and not just the fact of him."[^4] Look closely at the passage. What does Thomas ask for, what is he offered, and what does he actually say? What do you notice about the distance between those three?

*Read Exodus 24:9–11 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Reed asks of the mountain scene, "Did there really appear a gem-laden pathway up into the sky? Well, I don't know. I won't say one way or the other, but I can resonate with experiences with God where that seems to be the only language that is adequate to convey the meaning of it and not just the happening of it."[^9] What details in this short passage stand out to you? What would be lost if it were rewritten as a plain report?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Reed's main worry is that when we read everything like a clinical manual or a news report, God "becomes the thing that is there simply to be diagnosed and analyzed and charted," and "we're missing all the parts of the scripture that are trying to tell us what an experience with God is like."[^10] When has your faith been more like knowing facts about God than knowing God? What helps you read with the eyes of the heart?

</div>

### Shallow Enough for a Lamb <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#shallow-enough-for-a-lamb}

*Reading genre well does not have to make the Bible a book for experts only.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Reed quotes Gregory the Great: Scripture is "shallow enough for a lamb to go wading and jumping around in, but then down there, it's deep enough for an elephant to go swimming in." The notes add that the trouble starts when people in the shallow end claim "the whole river is shallow," or when people in the deep end claim there is no shallow entry.[^5] Which of those two mistakes do you find easier to make, and why?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Reed says, "that view, that's like, 'well, I'm just reading the plain meaning of it' - that is itself a view on the spectrum," and the notes connect the "plain reading" approach with modernism and the Enlightenment.[^11] Several traditions in our room have valued reading the Bible plainly and directly. What has that commitment protected or given your tradition? What changes, if anything, when you hold it as one view on the spectrum?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** Reed's son's response to the Good Samaritan inspired the family to keep "homeless packs" in their van, and after Reed's sermon some adults came up to explain why "you can't help everybody because they'll take advantage of you."[^12] Where are you tempted to reason your way out of a plain call to compassion? What would your version of a homeless pack look like this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Reed Dent asks us to read the Bible as a library. A news report, a poem and a story can all be true, but each is true in its own way, and asking the wrong question of a text can make us miss what it is offering. Buechner calls that deeper truth something seen with the eyes of the heart, the way Thomas finally saw Jesus. The river of Scripture still has a shallow end for the lamb, and there are depths we only reach with help. Our task is to keep wading in, and to let the parts that describe meeting God actually change us.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one passage you want to reread this month by asking first what kind of writing it is?

</div>
