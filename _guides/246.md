### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We closed the spiritual abuse series by praying a liturgy of lament and repentance that tells survivors their suffering is not their fault and calls the church to believe them and act.[^1]

**This week:** Eugenia Ortega opens a window into her family's life with their son Dante, who has severe autism, and asks the church to see a population it often misses.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Eugenia Ortega's son Dante, now 19, was diagnosed with autism at age two. In his case it means almost no sleep even with strong medication, deep frustration from being unable to communicate, self-injury, and the need for care around the clock.[^3] Her book, *A Look Into Our Window*, was written to help others see what families like hers live with, to let other sufferers know they are not alone, and to encourage churches to remember this population when they plan.[^4] She speaks honestly about crying out "Why?" and "Where are you, God?" and about finding God faithful even when circumstances do not change.[^5]

Marty brings in Paul's picture of the body in 1 Corinthians 12, where the members that seem weaker are indispensable, and an earlier BEMA image of a community that puts the weak at the center instead of the margins.[^6] The conversation ends by naming two postures for hearing stories like Eugenia's: curiosity and empathy.[^7] Some in the room may be caregivers, or may live with disability themselves; share only what you choose to.

### An Unseen Population <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#an-unseen-population}

*Eugenia's first gift is simply letting us look in, so we begin with what she shows us.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Eugenia describes people with severe special needs as an "unseen population." The notes list reasons: it is very hard to take them out in public, families become isolated by constant care, churches are not structured for them, and support has long waiting lists (her family's callback for behavioral therapy came two years later, after they had moved across the country).[^8] Which of these reasons surprised you, and which had you simply never thought about?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Eugenia notes, "If you know one person with autism, you know one person with autism."[^3] What mistake is that sentence meant to prevent? Where else do we tend to assume we understand someone because we know one person "like them"?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes describe a survival mode of "hanging on to dear life," where caregivers cannot step away, take a break, or unplug.[^9] Eugenia also says the exhaustion makes it "hard to go to church in person."[^10] If "bearing one another's burdens" were taken seriously for a family like hers, what would it practically require from a church, beyond good intentions?[^11] What would it require from you?

</div>

### Weaker Members, Indispensable <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#weaker-members-indispensable}

*Marty turns to Paul to ask where the vulnerable belong in a community shaped by the gospel.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read 1 Corinthians 12:12–26 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Listen especially to verses 22–26. What words does Paul use for certain parts of the body, and what does he say God has done about them? What do you notice about how Paul describes suffering and honor at the end of the passage?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes draw out the point: people with disabilities or chronic struggles "are not peripheral to the community but essential to its wholeness," and their suffering is not just "their experience" but becomes "our experience" when we understand our unity in Christ.[^6] What would actually change in a congregation if it believed that, not as a nice thought but as a description of its own health?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Marty recalls earlier BEMA teaching about the tribe of Dan: in a community reflecting shalom, the weak are at the center with the wagons circled around them; in empire-based communities, the weak struggle at the margins trying to keep up. The notes call this a way to diagnose whether churches have been shaped by "cultural norms that prioritize strength, efficiency, and normative experiences."[^12] Treat this as a diagnosis rather than a verdict: in churches like the ones represented here, where do the vulnerable usually end up, and what in our structures (schedules, programs, buildings, expectations) puts them there?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Eugenia also receives criticism and unsolicited advice, especially from people without special needs children; the notes call this offering solutions before seeking to understand.[^13] Why is advice so tempting when we are faced with someone else's suffering? What does it protect us from?

</div>

### Lament, Curiosity and Empathy <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#lament-curiosity-and-empathy}

*Eugenia's faith is not a story of struggle that ended; it is faith from the middle of it.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Psalm 55:1–8 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** When Eugenia reads in the Psalms, "My heart is in anguish within me," she finds deep connection; the Psalms give language to her experience.[^14] What do you notice about how the psalmist speaks to God in these verses? What permission does a prayer like this give?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes say Eugenia's testimony "is powerful precisely because it comes from a place of ongoing struggle rather than past suffering now resolved."[^5] Why might a testimony with no tidy ending be more trustworthy, or more helpful, than one that ends in resolution? What does that suggest about the stories churches usually invite people to tell?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> After Marty left Eugenia's book on a plane, the gate agent who retrieved it told him she has a sibling with severe autism and called their situation "such a difficult, lonely place to live."[^15] What does that small encounter suggest about how many people are carrying something like this quietly?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q11:** Marty describes curiosity as "listening to discover" (not to respond or even just to learn) and empathy as getting "bound up in their story."[^7] Who is one person or family in your life whose experience you have never really asked about? What would a curious, empathetic first step toward them look like this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> This week Eugenia let us look through her window: sleepless nights, a son trapped inside his own distress, long waiting lists, and a faith that keeps crying out to God and keeps finding him faithful. Paul tells us the parts of the body that seem weaker are indispensable, and that when one member suffers, all suffer together. The tribe of Dan image asks whether we circle the wagons around the weak or leave them struggling at the margins. Curiosity and empathy are where seeing begins. The wholeness of the body depends on every member, especially the ones we are most likely to miss.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, name one person or group you want to see more clearly, and one way you will try.

</div>
