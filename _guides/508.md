### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Reed Dent described discipleship as a forge: shared, ordinary life slowly brings a relationship "up to temperature," and only then can a word of wisdom or challenge land.[^1]

**This week:** Marty and Brent begin the fourth pillar, wrestling, which Marty calls the "universal pillar" because it can happen anywhere, at any time, in any setting.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This episode argues that questions are not a threat to faith. Marty starts from the claim that Western Christianity, built heavily on philosophy, has drifted toward an obsession with certainty, so that our confidence often rests in a theological system rather than in the living God. He is careful to say this is not a rejection of creeds or orthodoxy. His central picture is Dunkeld Cathedral in Scotland, where iron that once reinforced the stone is now pushing the walls apart, so the building has to be carefully taken down and rebuilt to be saved. Brent adds Notre-Dame, where restoration meant returning to older materials and methods.

The hosts then read two passages. Moses at the burning bush shows the posture wrestling needs: curiosity, attention and wonder. Peter's vision in Acts 10 shows the process, as scripture, tradition and experience collide and Peter follows God rather than his belief system. Marty closes with a method from constructive theology (critique, retrieval, construction) and with the claim that the Holy Spirit and prayer run through all four pillars.

### Why We Wrestle: Two Cathedrals <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#why-we-wrestle-two-cathedrals}

*Marty and Brent use two restored cathedrals to explain what wrestling is for.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Dunkeld Cathedral is about 800 years old. Its stone was reinforced with iron, and over the centuries that iron has aged and expanded until it is "pushing the building apart." To save the cathedral, restorers must take it apart stone by stone, remove what once helped but now harms, add new supports and rebuild.[^3] What details of this picture stand out to you? What does it suggest about how something good in our faith can, over time, start to cause harm?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Brent's counterpoint is Notre-Dame, restored after the 2019 fire with wood from the original sources and traditional methods. In his telling, wrestling asks two questions at once: *What should we return to?* and *What should we rebuild differently?* Because it holds both, wrestling "always involves tension."[^4] Where have you seen a person or a church need the first question more than the second, or the other way around?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Marty says creeds, orthodoxy and systematic theology have real value; the trouble begins when certainty becomes the goal. The notes summarize his core problem this way: "We say our faith is in God, but under pressure it often turns out to rest in a system rather than a person." He also says of theology, "It's not a test that we're going to pass. It's a life that we're going to live."[^5] Every tradition in this room (Church of Christ, Baptist, Christian Church, Methodist) has its own strengths and its own "iron." Without judging any tradition, including your own, how could you tell in your own life whether your confidence is resting in a system or in a person?

</div>

### Moses Turns Aside <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#moses-turns-aside}

*The burning bush becomes a portrait of the posture wrestling requires.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Exodus 3:1–6 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Listen for what Moses sees, what he says to himself and what he does before God speaks. What does the passage tell us about the bush, and which detail seems to be the one that matters? The notes point out that a burning bush in the wilderness was not remarkable in itself; what caught Moses's attention was that the bush was not consumed.[^6]

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The rabbis read this scene as revealing Moses's character. He needed curiosity (wanting to know more), attention (watching long enough to notice something unusual) and wonder (being open to something beyond his understanding). Brent adds that a shepherd leaving his flock shows how compelling the sight must have been. The hosts compare it to a well-made gas fireplace: you can only tell it from a real fire by watching long enough to see whether the wood burns.[^7] Which of the three (curiosity, attention, wonder) comes most naturally to you, and which is hardest? What tends to keep you from turning aside?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty contrasts puzzles, which pair a problem with a solution, with mystery, where "walking through one door shows you four more." He does not call puzzles bad, but he asks, "Where do I settle for puzzles when the greater reward is found in mystery?" He also introduces hypocognition: assumptions so deeply built in that we no longer have words for them.[^8] Where in your own faith are you tempted to want a puzzle solved when a mystery might be the greater gift?

</div>

### Peter on the Rooftop <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#peter-on-the-rooftop}

*Peter's vision shows scripture, tradition and experience colliding, and what faithful wrestling does with the collision.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Acts 10:9–29 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** As you listen, sort what you hear into three piles: what Peter knows from scripture, what his tradition taught him and what he experiences on the roof and at Cornelius's house. Marty stresses that avoiding table fellowship with Gentiles was a "biblically based, logical tradition," not arbitrary exclusion. The notes sum up Peter's realization: "The Bible called pork unclean; it never called the Gentile unclean."[^9] What do you notice about how Peter moves from refusal to his conclusion that he "should not call *anyone* impure or unclean"?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The hosts compare models for holding scripture, tradition and experience. Marty's graduate program treats them as an equal triangle. Richard Rohr's tricycle has one large front wheel that steers and two smaller back wheels; Rohr puts experience in front, while Marty prefers scripture as the front wheel. Marty also offers his own framing: the world we live in, the word we live by and the work we're called to.[^10] If you drew your own tricycle, which wheel actually steers most of the time, and which do you wish steered?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** Marty names a three-step method from constructive theology: critique (think critically about something), retrieval (recover what is right and good) and construction (build something new from what was recovered). Peter critiques his tradition without throwing out Judaism, retrieves what scripture actually says and constructs the worldview of the gospel. At the Jerusalem Council the church does this together: "It seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us."[^11] The notes suggest a practice: take one belief you hold firmly and ask where it came from (scripture, tradition or experience).[^12] Choose one belief, or one "Cornelius moment" with someone outside your tradition who clearly loves God. What might critique, retrieval and construction look like for you, and who would you want to pray it through with?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Dunkeld Cathedral is still standing because someone was willing to put up scaffolding and look honestly at what was holding it together. Wrestling, as Marty describes it, is that scaffolding: it critiques, retrieves what is good and builds again. Brent reminds us that sometimes the rebuilding means returning to something old. Moses shows us the posture, curious and attentive enough to notice a bush that does not burn up. Peter shows us the process, following the God behind his system when the two seemed to pull apart. Held in prayer and in community, questions become one of the ways faith keeps growing.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one question you are willing to keep open this week instead of rushing to an answer?

</div>
