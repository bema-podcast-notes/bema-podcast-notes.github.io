### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Jonah's backwards story showed God's mercy reaching even Nineveh, the capital of Israel's conquerors, because God sees potential in all people[^1].

**This week:** Nahum, the other Assyrian-period voice about Nineveh, tells oppressed Israel that God's patience is vast but not endless, and that he will act against unrepentant cruelty[^2].

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode presents Nahum as the counterbalance to Jonah, "two sides of the coin" in how God deals with evil[^3]. Jonah explains God's patience, but, as the notes put it, "the problem with the book of Jonah is that while the book explains God's apparent inaction and unbelievable patience, it still doesn't deal with the problem of evil that continues to exist."[^4] Nahum was written to Israel after the Assyrian conquest, at a time when Assyria was known for extreme cruelty to conquered peoples. Its message to victims is that God sees their suffering and will act[^5].

The episode introduces a second Hebrew word for justice. *Mishpat* (restorative justice) is the dominant note of Scripture, but *diyn* (judgment with finality) sometimes has to happen so that restoration can begin. The two appear in roughly a 1000-to-3 ratio, which the episode insists is not a balance[^6]. It closes by inviting us to hold Jonah in one hand and Nahum in the other and to trust God with the tension between them[^7]. Nahum uses intense, hyperbolic language, and some of the history behind it is painful; take care of one another as you read.

**Opening Question:** Think of a time you were waiting for something to be made right, whether in your family, at work or in the news. What was the waiting like, and what helped you through it?

### The Other Side of the Coin <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#the-other-side-of-the-coin}

*Nahum answers the question Jonah leaves open.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Nahum 1:2–8 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Last week's preparation asked you to mark phrases about God's patience and phrases about God acting. The notes summarize God's patience as "slow to anger" and God's justice with the line "The Lord will not leave the guilty unpunished"[^8]. As we listen, what do you notice about how the passage holds these two together? Which descriptions of God stand out to you, and how do they sit beside last week's portrait in Jonah?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe the tension this way: God's patience gives hope to people who might repent, but it creates anguish for victims of ongoing injustice. Nahum's answer is that God is patient, forgiving, mournful, pleading and grieving, yet eventually acts[^9]. Why might a book like Jonah, on its own, feel incomplete to someone who has suffered at the hands of others?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes give a practical picture for prayer: pray for the transformation of those who do evil (Jonah) and pray for justice and deliverance for victims (Nahum), and trust God to navigate the tension between those prayers[^10]. Which of those two prayers comes more naturally to you, and what would it look like to pray the other one this week?

</div>

### Diyn and the 1000-to-3 <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#diyn-and-the-1000-to-3}

*Two words for justice, and why one is used far more than the other.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes define *mishpat* as putting things back in their proper place, restoration rather than retribution, and *diyn* as a decision with an "air of finality." *Diyn* is the moment when, in the episode's words, "the discussion is over, and we need to get on with restoring the world," and sometimes it is required in order to achieve *mishpat*[^6]. How can a judgment that ends something serve the goal of restoring something? Can you think of an example, biblical or ordinary?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes are emphatic that patience and judgment are not equal forces: "It's 1000-to-3 in favor of patience and mercy. But the 3 matters - it's real and necessary."[^11] What would go wrong in our picture of God if we treated it as 500-to-500, or if we dropped the 3 altogether?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes apply the ratio to personal relationships: our default when wronged should be patience, forgiveness and hope for restoration (the 1000), but persistent, unrepentant abuse may require boundaries and consequences (the 3)[^12]. This can touch tender places, so share only what you choose. How do you discern when patience is love and when a boundary is love? What has helped you, or someone you know, tell the difference?

</div>

### Good News for the Oppressed <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#good-news-for-the-oppressed}

*Nahum was written to comfort victims, not to frighten readers.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Nahum 2:11–13 aloud, then Nahum 3:1 and 3:19.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes explain the lion's-den image as a lion that kills not for food but to stockpile prey, a picture of empire taking from conquered peoples out of greed. God's repeated "I am against you" would have been deeply comforting to those suffering under Assyria, and the book closes with "endless cruelty"[^13]. What do you notice about the reasons God gives for opposing Nineveh? Whose side is God on in these verses?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes warn against reading Nahum with the question "What does this mean for ME?" as if it were a personal threat; read in context, it says to victims, "God sees injustice and will eventually act"[^14]. Who in our world today most needs to hear Nahum's message, and how might the church become a voice of that hope?

*Read Deuteronomy 32:35 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode ends with "Vengeance is mine, declares the Lord," the reminder that "Humans would not do well in deciding when to show patience and when to bring judgment," and the image that both Jonah and Nahum "go in our pockets"[^7]. Where in your own life do you need to hand a situation over to God's timing instead of deciding it yourself?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Last week Jonah showed us a God so patient that he spares even the capital of Israel's conquerors. This week Nahum tells the conquered that their suffering is not invisible, and that God will not ignore unrepentant cruelty forever. The two are not equal weights on a scale; mercy outweighs judgment a thousand to three. But the three is real, and it exists so that the world can finally be put back together. We carry both books in our pockets and trust God with the timing.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, which book do you most need to carry in your pocket this week, Jonah or Nahum, and why?

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 54 — Zephaniah — T'shuvah](/guides/54)** (30:18): Zephaniah speaks to Judah during the reign of King Josiah as a new Babylonian threat rises, and he is the seventh prophet in the series. His central image is *t'shuvah*, repentance understood as returning to one's original design rather than merely feeling sorry, and he shows idolatry and injustice to be inseparable[^15]. He also catalogs judgment on the surrounding nations while holding out hope for a humble remnant.

**What to bring next week:** Read Zephaniah 3:9–17. Come ready to share one image of restoration that stands out to you.
