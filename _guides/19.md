### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Egypt became our first picture of empire, built on fear and coercion, set against God's *shalom*, built on trust and invitation. The question became not only how God gets his people out of Egypt but how he gets Egypt out of his people.[^1]

**This week:** Following Rabbi David Fohrman's book *The Exodus You Almost Passed Over*, the episode argues that the Exodus is about God pursuing hearts, including Pharaoh's, and inviting Israel to become his firstborn.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Marty notes that rabbis like to start with questions, and Fohrman asks several. Why is the festival named after just one plague? Why would an all-powerful God need ten plagues? Why does Moses ask for only three days? Who hardens Pharaoh's heart, Pharaoh or God? And why does Pharaoh seem worried about the wrong things? The answers run through God's names (El Shaddai, which the rabbis read as "The One Who Said To His World Enough," and YHWH, built from "I was, I am, I will be"), the difference between polytheism and monotheism, and two different Hebrew words for "hardening."[^3]

The episode ends with the *bechor* (firstborn). Israel is not God's firstborn by birth; God is looking for a people who want to be his firstborn, carrying the Father's values to their siblings. Marty connects the Passover's bloody doorway and the Red Sea, which the rabbis called a birth canal, to Jesus' conversation with Nicodemus about being born again.[^4] Two parts of this touch long-standing differences among our traditions: how God's sovereignty and human choice fit together, and how to read "water and Spirit" in John 3. The guide invites each of us to say what we were taught and notice what this reading adds.

### Precision, Not Power <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#precision-not-power}

*Pharaoh's odd reactions to the plagues reveal what he actually believed about the gods.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Exodus 8:8–10 and Exodus 9:1–7 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Fohrman's fifth question is why Pharaoh seems concerned about the wrong things. Asked when he wants the frogs gone, Pharaoh says "tomorrow." In the livestock plague he seems less interested in his own animals than in whether the Israelites' animals are also affected. The notes put it this way: Pharaoh seems unfazed by the power of the plagues and concerned instead with precision.[^5] What do you notice in these two passages about what Pharaoh asks, checks and wants to know?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** Fohrman illustrates with a Monopoly game: the pieces could never describe Parker, the company that made the board, except in the language of the board. Pagan polytheism makes gods in the image of things on the board: a rain god, a fire god, an ice god. All of them are powerful, but they are chaotic and none of them are precise. So hail with fire inside it, as in Exodus 9:23–24, should be impossible, because the fire god and the ice god do not work together.[^6] Why would precision, more than raw power, be the thing that threatens Pharaoh's whole view of the world?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say the patriarchs knew God as El Shaddai, which the rabbis read as an acronym for *Mi she'Amar Dai L'olamo*, "The One Who Said To His World Enough." Their conclusion is that "G-d's fundamental posture is not about power."[^7] Read Exodus 6:2–3. Where in your own life would you rather God show power than restraint, and what might his restraint be teaching you?

</div>

### Two Kinds of Hard Heart <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#two-kinds-of-hard-heart}

*God pursues Pharaoh's heart, and Pharaoh's heart moves through more than one kind of hardness.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Exodus 5:1–3 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes point to two speeches in this passage. Moses first speaks of going to worship in the wilderness, and Pharaoh answers, "Who is Adonai that I should listen to his voice? I don't know Adonai." Marty says it would make sense for Moses to retreat or raise the stakes. Instead Moses rephrases the request in terms Pharaoh's worldview understands: our God might strike us.[^8] What do you notice about how Moses changes his message, and what does that suggest about how he is trying to reach Pharaoh?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes describe two Hebrew words for hardening. *Kavad* means "to be heavy": Pharaoh just doesn't get it. *Hazak* means "to strengthen": Pharaoh sees it and decides, with resolve, to push through. Sometimes Pharaoh does each of these, and sometimes God does.[^9] Picture someone you know (or yourself) in each state. What is the difference between a heavy heart and a strengthened one, and why might each call for a different response?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Fohrman's fourth question raises the old debate between Arminianism and Calvinism. The notes answer that God takes so long because he is trying very hard to bring Pharaoh to a real choice: ten plagues, so that even Pharaoh will understand. Eventually Pharaoh does understand and says, in effect, "I see it and I just say no," and only then does God strengthen his heart and finish the story so everyone can see what is happening.[^10] Our traditions hold God's sovereignty and human choice together in different ways. Without needing to settle that debate, what does this reading show you about God's heart toward Pharaoh, and how does it sit alongside what you were taught?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes observe that the God of Exodus pursues every heart to the very end, even an enemy's, and suggest this as a model for how believers might approach people who oppose or misunderstand them.[^11] Who is your "Pharaoh" right now, and what would pursuing that person's heart, rather than writing them off, look like?

</div>

### Born Again as Firstborn <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#born-again-as-firstborn}

*The answer to "Why Passover?" turns out to be an invitation.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Exodus 4:22–23 and Exodus 13:11–16 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes explain that the tefillin, the small boxes bound on the forehead and arm in prayer, contain the Shema and the strange law of the firstborn donkey. That raises Fohrman's first question: why the obsession with the firstborn?[^12] What do you notice in these passages about the firstborn, and about what Israel is supposed to remember and teach its children?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty connects the Passover to Jesus' words to Nicodemus. Rabbi Akiva pointed out that blood on the top, bottom and sides of the door meant Israel left "in haste through a bloody door," as a newborn enters the world, and the rabbis spoke of the Red Sea as a birth canal. Marty argues that "Water and Spirit" in John 3 "is all about the Exodus," an invitation to join God's project.[^13] Read John 3:1–8. Our traditions read "born of water and the Spirit" in different ways, and some connect it closely to baptism. Alongside what your tradition emphasizes, what does the Exodus birth image add to your understanding of being born again?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** According to the notes, Israel is not God's firstborn by birth; God is looking for a people who *want* to be his *bechor*. A firstborn who truly honors the father tries to bring the siblings along to honor him too. God started with Moses, a man who would go and be the message, and now invites all of Israel to do the same, "Not just having a message, but being the message."[^14] Where in your ordinary life could you be the message this week rather than only bringing one?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Fohrman's questions lead to a surprising picture of the Exodus. God, who first showed himself as the one who knows when to say "enough," reveals himself to Moses as the One who was, is and will be. He works with a precision Pharaoh's gods could never manage, and he pursues even Pharaoh's heart until Pharaoh sees clearly and chooses. Then God invites Israel to be born again through a bloody door as his firstborn, a people who will not only carry his message but become it.[^15]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, who is one "sibling" in your life you would like to help come to know the Father?

</div>
