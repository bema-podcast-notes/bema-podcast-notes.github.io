### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Brad Gray and Brad Nelson invited us to pray the Lord's Prayer again, beginning with a Father "in the heavens" who reigns above everything and still comes near, and to see ourselves as part of the prayer's answer.

**This week:** Marty begins a 12-part series on BEMA's four pillars with the first one, the text. He tells how the text became his central spiritual practice and walks through the passages that convince him God's word carries real power.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The four pillars of BEMA are text, community, discipleship, and wrestling. Marty first put them into words after studying in Israel and Turkey with Ray Vander Laan, as a deliberate counterpoint to the four pillars of Hellenism. Each pillar will get three episodes this season. In this first one, Marty tells his own story: mentors who taught him prayer and contemplation, a lesson above the ruins of Qumran that left him in tears, and a conversation with Ray that let him see devotion to the text as a form of prayer.

Marty and Brent then read through his favorite passages about God's words, from Isaiah's word that does not return empty to the king who must write out his own copy of Torah. The episode closes with Romans 11 and Marty's claim that the text is what the Jewish people bring to the shared family of Jews and Gentiles, along with a guardrail (honor the text without worshiping it) and a set of practical ways to make space for it.

### Where Are the Essenes Today? <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#where-are-the-essenes-today}

*Marty's path to the text ran through prayer first and scholarship second.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Standing above Qumran, where the Dead Sea Scrolls were found, Ray Vander Laan taught about the Essenes, a community that gave their whole lives to knowing, copying, and preserving the text. He asked, "Where are the Essenes today?" A friend shouted into the valley, "I want to be an Essene!" and Marty sat there in tears, agreeing.[^1] What do you think Ray was really asking, and why might that question move someone so deeply?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty calls the four pillars a "pocket-sized" summary, like the 30-second version of a trip story you need ready when you get home. His thought after his trips was, "If the Greeks could change the world with four pillars, so can we."[^2] If you had to give a 30-second version of what you believe the Bible is for, what would you say?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Marty's mentors were not scholars. They taught him prayer and contemplation and a personal relationship with the risen Jesus; only later did God bring him "all the nerdy, crazy stuff." When Marty asked Ray whether devotion to the text could count as prayer for him, Ray said yes.[^3] Where does your time with Scripture usually sit, somewhere between study and prayer? What shaped that, and what might it look like to let the two meet?

</div>

### Words That Do Not Return Empty <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#words-that-do-not-return-empty}

*Marty's favorite passages share one conviction: God's word has a living power.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 55:6–13 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes describe this passage as a poem about repentance and renewal with God's word at its center, a word that does not come back empty. Marty believes the text has a supernatural power that a blog post or a song we write does not have.[^4] What images does the poem use for how God's word works? What do you notice about where the word sits in the poem and what surrounds it?

*Read Ezekiel 40:4 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> We might expect "look and listen" or "see and hear," but the verse pairs *looking* with the eyes and *hearing* (*shema*, which means taking something in and responding, not just listening) with the ears. Ray taught that God wants people who use their eyes but whose deepest trust rests on what they hear, His word. The notes call this being "people of the ears."[^5] What would it look like to trust what you hear from God's word over what you can see?

*Read Deuteronomy 17:18–20 and Joshua 1:7–9 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Out of everything a king might be required to do, God asks him to write out his own copy of Torah and read it daily. Before the conquest, Joshua's basic tool is the book of the law, kept on his lips day and night. Marty warns that our culture pushes leaders toward platform and celebrity, while God calls them to humility, to knowing His text, and to leading out of lives changed by it. He adds that memorizing verses does not make anyone a spiritual giant.[^6] Each of our traditions has its own picture of what qualifies someone to lead. What does your tradition look for in its leaders, and where does a leader's own life in the text fit in that picture?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** You may not hold any formal leadership role, but most of us lead someone: a family, a team, a friend who watches how we live. Where in your ordinary week could the text sit closer to the center, the way it did for the king and for Joshua?

</div>

### The Root Supports You <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#the-root-supports-you}

*Marty argues that the text is the gift Israel brings to one shared family.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Romans 11:17–24 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** In Marty's reading, Gentiles are wild branches grafted into Israel's olive tree. Gentiles should not be arrogant, and Jews should remember that their story was always about including the Gentiles.[^7] What does the passage say to the grafted-in branches? Report one phrase that stands out to you and why.

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty's claim is that what the Jewish people brought to the Jew–Gentile relationship was the text, held in a unique way, and that Gentile Christianity lost much of that when the two split in the first and second centuries. At the same time, he sets a guardrail: the text is not Jesus, and it should be put in its proper place rather than worshiped.[^8] Where have you seen churches (including ones you have belonged to) lean toward neglecting the text, and where toward treating it almost as an object of worship? What helps keep it in its proper place?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** BEMA said in Session 1 that when we make space for God, He fills it, and Marty says time in the text "will never return void." He lists many ways in: memorize a verse a week, read and meditate without turning it into study, study, write it out, listen on a commute, make art, or read aloud to others. He warns that people tend to default to study and skip the rest.[^9] Which of these ways is your default, and which one would stretch you? Name one you could try this week.

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Marty's love of the text grew out of prayer, a lesson above Qumran, and his own Jewish identity. The passages he treasures all say the same thing: God's word does real work, renewing what is dry, opening ears that are stopped up, and keeping leaders humble. BEMA takes its name from the platform where the text was read, because the vision is a people who gather around it. That does not make the text an idol; it means we give it room. Even a small seed planted in that space bears fruit.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one specific way you will make space in the text this week?

</div>
