### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** In Isaiah 7–12, God asked a frightened Ahaz to trust the story and do nothing, and the hosts read the Immanuel sign in its setting before turning to the messianic hope of chapters 9 and 11.[^1]

**This week:** After twelve chapters spoken almost entirely to Israel and Judah, Isaiah's focus widens to the nations around them in the "oracles against the nations."[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Readers often find Isaiah 13–23 depressing, like a long list of everyone who has been naughty. Marty and Brent argue instead that these chapters show how complete God's care is: nothing goes unnoticed and nobody is left out. Superpowers like Babylon and Egypt are addressed, and so is small Philistia. In the middle of the list, God also addresses his own city, Jerusalem.[^2]

Along the way the hosts discuss whose voice is speaking in prophecy, Gregory Boyd's *Cross Vision*, the Jewish "both/and" view of God's action and human choice, and an unplanned look at what the name "Jerusalem" means. Since the oracles name places that are in the news today, some questions may touch strong feelings. The aim is not to pick a side but to ask what it means to stand on the side of *shalom*.[^2]

### Nobody Goes Unnoticed <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#nobody-goes-unnoticed}

*The oracles reach small nations and great ones, and their tone is not always what readers expect.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 14:28–32 and Isaiah 15:1–5 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** In the oracle to Philistia, "the poorest of the poor will find pasture" and Zion is a refuge for the afflicted. In the oracle to Moab, mourning spreads through its towns, and the prophet says, "My heart cries out over Moab." The hosts note that Philistia was roughly in the area of today's Gaza Strip.[^3] What in the tone of these two passages surprises you, given that they are "oracles against the nations"? Point to specific lines.

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty describes two incomplete ways of reading the prophets. A general reading hears only "God is angry; all have sinned" and misses the concrete injustices God names. An insider reading hears only "you are my people and you have sinned against me" and misses God's care for everyone outside the covenant. Isaiah 1–12 and 13–23 together correct both, much as Jonah ends with God caring for every person in Nineveh "and also many animals" (Jonah 4:11).[^4] Which of the two readings have you tended to default to, and where did you pick it up?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Marty says God cares about the church and about Christians, and cares "literally just as much" about people who don't bear his name. He applies this to the nations in the news at the time of recording, all at once and with the same care. "No corner, no flag, no nation, no people group, no church" is exempt from God's concern for justice.[^5] Without debating current events, think of a nation or group you find it hard to imagine God caring about as much as he cares about you. What would change in how you pray, speak or act if you believed he did?

</div>

### Who Is Doing the Destroying? <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#who-is-doing-the-destroying}

*The oracles raise hard questions about God's action, human choice and the prophet's point of view.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q4:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> In Isaiah 13:3 it is unclear whether Isaiah or the LORD is speaking. Prophets often move in and out of God's first-person voice without marking it, and translators have trouble placing quotation marks; a phrase like "declares the LORD Almighty" shows where a quote ends but not always where it began.[^6] If you have more than one translation in the room, compare how they mark speech in Isaiah 13:1–5. What do you notice?

*Read Isaiah 14:19–22 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** In these verses God seems to be destroying Babylon, yet verse 20 says the king is condemned "for you have destroyed your land and killed your people." Marty says the biblical and Jewish world tends to hold such things as both/and rather than either/or: Babylon's guilt lies in its own imperial injustice, and God also rises up and acts. He compares it to two colors of play-dough smashed together that will never come apart.[^7] Find both threads in the passage. Who is said to be doing what?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Brent has been reading Gregory Boyd's *Cross Vision*, which argues that Jesus on the cross is the clearest picture of God and that violent portrayals of God should be read in that light, with God's true character breaking through the prophet's perspective. Marty clarifies that Boyd would not say the prophets made *mistakes* (Brent says that word was his own). Instead Marty speaks of inspired, infallible authors who each have their own vantage point, so readers need other perspectives, and finally Jesus, to see the full picture. Both hosts also worry that Boyd's focus on the cross can underplay the rest of Jesus's life and teaching.[^8] Our traditions hold different convictions about inspiration. How do you personally hold together "inspired" and "written from a human point of view"? What helps you, and what makes you uneasy?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Marty reframes judgment away from tit-for-tat punishment. The world human empires build "is not built for shalom"; it rests on people who are oppressed and exploited, so it can't last, and God also actively dismantles what causes destructive injustice.[^9] Where in the systems you are part of (work, economy, church, community) do you see something that is not built for *shalom*? What is one way you could stop propping it up?

</div>

### Jerusalem in the Middle of the List <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#jerusalem-in-the-middle-of-the-list}

*Brent notices that Isaiah 22 is about Jerusalem, and it becomes one of the main points of the episode.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 22:1–14 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** God's own city is judged alongside the pagan nations; God "doesn't have favorites." The passage includes a strange detail: the leaders fled while the enemy was still far away and were captured without using the bow. Marty reads this as a lament about lack of trust, echoing Isaiah's challenge to Ahaz to stand firm in faith, with God grieving, in effect, "You didn't even give me a chance to show up."[^10] What details in the passage support Marty's reading? What do the people rely on instead of God?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty had been taught that "Jerusalem" means "valley of vision." Checking on the spot, the hosts found lexicons offering meanings like "foundation of peace" or "possession of peace," and none linking the name to "vision." Marty laughs at himself for repeating something he had never checked.[^11] What is something you once repeated confidently about the Bible that turned out to be different when you looked? How did it feel to find out?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** When people ask which side they should be on, the hosts' answer from Isaiah is the side of *shalom*: celebrate peace wherever it happens, grieve where it is missing, and listen to the warning when we are the ones causing harm.[^12] What would standing on the side of *shalom*, rather than on a team, look like for you in one specific relationship or situation this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> On a first read, Isaiah 13–23 looks like a grim list of nations in trouble. Read closely, it shows how far God's care reaches: Babylon, Philistia, Moab, Damascus, Egypt, Edom and Tyre, with Jerusalem dropped into the middle of the list. God does not care about the poor in Philistia any less than the powerful in Babylon, and his own people get no exemption. Kingdoms built on oppression collapse because they cannot last and because God works to dismantle them, with human choice and divine action tangled together. The question is not which nation or team to back, but whether we are on the side of *shalom*.[^13]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: name one place where you want to stand on the side of *shalom* this week.

</div>
