### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Marty described discipleship in Jesus' world as a full-time life of imitation that moves from copying, to interpreting, to friendship, to being sent. He stressed that this calling was always rare, and that its principles (follow someone ahead, reach back to someone behind) are open to everyone.

**This week:** Elle Grover Fricks looks at the words behind "disciple" and argues that making disciples often means becoming a spiritual parent.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Elle starts with the Latin, Hebrew and Greek words for "disciple," all of which mean a learner. She then reads verses from across the Old Testament and the New Testament letters where the language of discipling keeps appearing next to the language of children. From that pattern she argues that discipling often means spiritual parenting: taking real responsibility for someone while protecting their independence. The hosts are honest that spiritual authority has been badly misused, and they ask whether the good version can be restored.

The conversation also gives two working pictures: the teacher (*moreh*) whose teaching "flows like water," sent downstream when the learner needs it, and the "I do, we do, you do" pattern Jesus used with his disciples. It ends with a strong case for naming what people do well. Some questions draw on family and parenting language. You do not need to be a parent to answer, and if family is a painful subject for you, feel free to answer from friendships, mentors or work, or to pass.

### Learners and Children <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#learners-and-children}

*Elle starts with plain word meanings and then traces a pattern that runs through the whole Hebrew Bible.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Elle notes that the Latin *discipulus*, the Hebrew *talmid* (from the root *lamad*, "to learn/teach") and the Greek *mathētēs* (from *manthanō*, "to learn") all mean a learner[^1]. She then reads several Old Testament verses, translating *lamad* as "disciple"[^2].

*Read Deuteronomy 11:19, Deuteronomy 31:12, Psalm 34:11 and Isaiah 54:13 aloud.*

What words or groups of people keep showing up in these verses? Where do they come from in the Bible, and what does that range suggest about how central this pattern is?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Elle shows that the apostles used the same pairing. Paul writes that he became the Corinthians' father through the gospel, and he calls Timothy and Titus his true children; John writes to "my little children"; Peter calls Mark "my son"[^3]. The notes also point out that some of these bonds grew over years of ministry (Timothy) and some formed quickly in a crisis (Onesimus, whom Paul met in prison)[^4].

*Read 1 Corinthians 4:15 and Philemon 10 aloud.*

What difference is Paul drawing between a guide and a father? What do you notice about how quickly the bond with Onesimus seems to have formed?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Elle's main point is that learning from God means coming as a child. Her five-year-old son Lewis shouted "Bioluminescence!" during a lesson on nocturnal animals because he knew something about the topic, and she calls that the energy we should bring to the things of God[^5]. Marty adds Matthew 13:52: a teacher who becomes a learner again keeps bringing new and old treasures out of the storehouse[^6]. Where have you lost that eager-learner posture in your faith, and what has helped you recover it?

</div>

### Responsibility and Independence <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#responsibility-and-independence}

*The hosts describe what makes a spiritual parent different, and they are honest about how that role gets twisted.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Elle compares three roles. A professor focuses on content. A coffee mentor listens and offers wisdom when invited, but has no authority. A spiritual parent has the same limits but carries extra responsibility: "This is my kid. If they're not doing well, it's on me to find out where they are and what's going on." Her illustration is the difference between giving someone directions home and walking them home[^7]. Think of someone who walked you home in this sense, rather than just giving directions. What did they do that a mentor or teacher would not have done?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Healthy discipleship, the hosts say, means taking responsibility while protecting independence. Marty warns that unhealthy leaders reverse this: they take away independence, neglect responsibility, and still call it discipleship. Turn a good approach "a couple of degrees" and it becomes manipulation and control[^8]. Elle notes that individualism ("It's just me and Jesus") resists any human authority, and that some of that resistance comes from real harm. She asks whether the good version can be restored, pointing to the early church's use of *abba* and *amma* (father and mother) for its leaders[^9].

Our traditions have handled spiritual authority in different ways. In the church you grew up in, how did people think about one believer having real responsibility in another's spiritual life? What was that instinct protecting, and what might it have cost? (Speak in general terms if that is easier.)

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Elle's picture is the hydrangea: a child or disciple who will grow into a hydrangea can't be made into a tulip, but the gardener still has to weed, water and protect from frost. The two ways to fail are forcing people into a copy of ourselves, or leaving them to fend for themselves[^10]. The hosts add that discipleship should be consensual, named and agreed on rather than done "secretly"[^11]. With the people you have influence over (at home, at work, at church), which of those two failures are you more drawn to, and what would better care look like?

</div>

### Flowing Like Water <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#flowing-like-water}

*Elle describes teaching that fits the moment, and Marty confesses where he has struggled.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Elle links *moreh* ("teacher") to the root *yarah*, connected to the early rain, and explains it as "to flow like water into me." A disciple-maker has a reservoir, and the skill is knowing what to send downstream, and when. First, figure out who someone is, where they are and what they're struggling with[^12]. The episode also traces "I do, we do, you do" through Jesus' ministry, from the disciples watching him, to learning to pray with him, to the sending of the seventy (or seventy-two) in Luke 10[^13].

*Read Isaiah 30:20–21 aloud.*

What do you notice about where the teacher is and how the word comes? How does that fit Elle's picture of teaching that flows?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Marty confesses that he tends to treat the 80% done right as expected and comment only on the 20% done wrong. He says this "killed the spirit" of disciples early in his ministry, and Brent says he does the same[^14]. Elle observes that if attention only comes after misbehavior, the misbehavior continues, and the hosts point to 1 Thessalonians 5:11 and to saying things like "That's it! That moment was full of Jesus"[^15]. Who in your life needs to hear what they are doing right, and what exactly will you tell them this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Elle starts with the plain meaning of *talmid*, a learner, and shows how Scripture keeps pairing discipling with children, from Deuteronomy to Paul's "my true child." The hosts are honest that spiritual authority has been misused, yet they argue for recovering the good version: take real responsibility for people while protecting who God made them to be. Disciple-makers are gardeners, not sculptors. They let teaching flow when it is needed, move people from "I do" to "we do" to "you do," and above all name the good, because delight shapes people more than criticism[^16].
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, name one good thing you have seen in someone in this room tonight or over these weeks.

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

Reed uses two main images: a butcher who works with the grain of the meat, and a blacksmith's forge where the iron must be brought up to heat before it can be struck. It matters because it lifts the pressure of programs and puts the focus on paying attention to who a person really is.
