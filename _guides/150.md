### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We met the Roman church as a blended family, unsettled by Claudius's expulsion of the Jews and their later return, and heard Paul show that every group in it falls short of its own measuring stick.

**This week:** Having shown that everyone falls short, Paul announces the solution that is just as universal: righteousness that comes from God as a gift, with Abraham as the example for Jew and Gentile alike.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode works through Romans 3:21–5:6. It argues that Romans 3:23 is usually quoted without the verse that completes it, so that a line about universal access to grace gets heard as a line about condemnation.[^1] It describes God, drawing on the Hebrew idea of a judge (*shofet*), as both the one who declares justice and the one who carries it out, "just and the justifier."[^2]

The heart of the episode is Paul's contrast between wages and a gift, and his use of Abraham, who was counted righteous in Genesis 15 before he was circumcised in Genesis 17. That order, the notes say, makes Abraham the father of faith for both Jews and Gentiles, so neither group in Rome has grounds for boasting.[^3] The episode also asks listeners to read Romans by looking forward through the biblical story rather than backward through later theology.[^4]

### All Have Fallen Short, All Are Justified <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#all-have-fallen-short-all-are-justified}

*Paul turns from the problem everyone shares to the solution everyone is offered.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Romans 3:21–26 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes say Romans 3:23 "is commonly quoted out of context," because the sentence goes on to say that "all are justified freely by his grace."[^5] Read verses 23 and 24 together. Who are the "all" in each half? The notes sum up: "Paul's point is not condemnation but universal access to grace."[^6] Where have you heard verse 23 quoted on its own, and what was it being used to do?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The episode draws on the Hebrew word *shofet*, "judge," explaining that a judge "not only pronounces judgment but also carries it out."[^7] So God is both the one who declares justice and the one who brings about the restoration justice requires.[^8] The notes also define justification as being "declared righteous" or "exonerated," adding that "it's a legal declaration, not earned wages."[^9] How does picturing God as a judge who restores, not only one who sentences, shift the way you hear verse 26?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes point out that "gospel" (*euangelion*, "good news") was already a Roman word for "announcements of imperial victory," so Paul's gospel challenges the empire's own gospel of Caesar's rule.[^10] What would a first-century listener in Rome have heard in Paul's claim that righteousness has been revealed from God?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes describe a shared human condition: whatever standard we use, we fall short and live with "the testimony of a guilty conscience," which creates "universal fear and insecurity that cannot be resolved through human effort or rule-following."[^11] What measuring stick do you most often hold yourself to, and what would it mean for you to hear "all are justified freely" as addressed to you?

</div>

### Wages or Gift: The Faith of Abraham <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#wages-or-gift-the-faith-of-abraham}

*Paul uses Abraham to show that righteousness came before any marker that could divide Jew from Gentile.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Romans 4:1–12 aloud, then Genesis 15:5–6 and Genesis 17:9–11.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Look at the order of events. In Genesis, when is Abraham counted righteous, and when is he circumcised? Now look at Romans 4:9–12. What does Paul conclude from that order, and who does he say Abraham is father of? The notes say this chronology "proves that righteousness preceded the covenant sign."[^12]

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Paul contrasts wages with a gift (Romans 4:4–5). The notes say righteousness "functions more like an inheritance or gift - something received based on relationship rather than performance," and that Abraham's righteousness was credited "not because of his perfect behavior (which he didn't have) but because of his trust in God's character and promises."[^13] Why might a Jewish believer returning to Rome and a long-time Gentile leader each have needed to hear that Abraham belongs to both of them?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode says "Faith and trust are synonymous terms referring to believing in God's story and promises," and connects this to "trusting the story" from Genesis 1, a theme from earlier BEMA sessions.[^14] It also says "Reading Romans requires looking forward through biblical narrative context, not backward through Reformation theology," naming Augustine, Luther and Calvin as later lenses.[^15] Each tradition in this room has inherited some way of reading Romans. What has your tradition taught you to listen for in these chapters, and what does reading forward from Genesis help you notice that you might otherwise miss?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes warn against "modern tendencies to turn faith into a new kind of performance-based system."[^16] Faith can become a wage too: believing hard enough, getting doctrine exactly right, feeling the right things. Where in your life are you still working for wages from God, and what would it look like this week to receive instead of earn?

</div>

### Peace and a Hope That Does Not Shame <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#peace-and-a-hope-that-does-not-shame}

*Paul draws out what justification means for how we live now.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Romans 5:1–6 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** What words in this passage describe what we have now, and what words describe what we are waiting for? The notes point toward the Spirit pouring God's love into believers' hearts as an "experiential knowledge of acceptance beyond intellectual understanding."[^17] Where do you see the difference between knowing about God's acceptance and experiencing it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes close with the claim that "God's love and acceptance are foundational truths to be trusted rather than rewards to be earned."[^18] If that were the foundation of a church community, how might it change the way different groups in that church treat one another?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q11:** Paul says that while we were still weak, Christ died for the ungodly (5:6). Who is someone you find it hard to see as included in that "all," and what would one step toward them look like?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Paul has shown that every group in Rome falls short of its own standard, and now he shows that the solution is just as universal: righteousness comes from God as a gift. God is a judge who does not only declare justice but carries it out, just and the justifier. Abraham trusted God before he was circumcised, so he is the father of everyone who trusts, Jew and Gentile together. Faith is not a new wage to earn but trust in God's story and promises, and that trust leaves no one in the family with grounds to boast.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one place where you want to stop earning and start trusting?

</div>
