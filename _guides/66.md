### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Isaiah's fourth voice offered the remnant scandalous grace, called them to the fast God chooses (loosing chains, feeding the hungry, repairing broken walls), and framed it all with Sabbath rest.

**This week:** The remnant goes home. Ezra and Nehemiah tell one continuous story of a hard return and the three very different leaders who carried it.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This episode reads Ezra and Nehemiah as one continuous narrative, traditionally treated as a single volume in Hebrew manuscripts.[^1] After Persia overthrew Babylon around 539 BC, the Jews were allowed to return, but the return came in waves, not as a joyful mass exodus, and the people who went back were not the educated elite.[^2] Rebuilding Jerusalem meant opposition, political pressure from Persia, and people losing heart.[^3]

The heart of the episode is a comparison of three leaders: Zerubbabel the pioneer, Ezra the shepherd, and Nehemiah the prophet. God blessed all three because each worked as God had made him to work.[^4] Ezra and Nehemiah both confronted intermarriage, in very different ways, and Nehemiah's methods were physical and harsh. The notes are clear that his actions, 2,500 years ago, are "not a model for abusive leadership today."[^5] Many churches have recently wrestled with questions about leadership, so hold this part of the conversation with care.

### The Hard Road Home <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#the-hard-road-home}

*The decree to go home was good news, but going home was hard.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Ezra 1:1–4 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes explain that Persian rule differed from Babylonian and Assyrian brute force. Persia established suzerain-vassal relationships, and Cyrus's decree "was part of Persian policy, not just generosity toward Israel."[^6] The return happened in waves, "Very few people were 'filled with joy'" on the journey, and those who went were "below-average, C- students," while the cream of the crop stayed in Babylon.[^7] As you listen to the decree, what do you notice about who is invited to go, who is asked to help, and what the decree says about God?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe Ezra and Nehemiah as full of letters between the Jews and the Persian authorities. Opponents wrote letters portraying the Jews as disloyal, and every building project required correspondence and patience.[^8] The notes put it plainly: "It's difficult to crawl out of bed each morning and encourage God's people to write another letter only to wait for a delayed response from some Persian king."[^9] What kinds of slow, unglamorous work do you think rebuilding a city required? What does it suggest about God that he chose to rebuild with an ordinary, unimpressive remnant?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes name a biblical pattern: mountaintop experiences are followed by testing in the desert. Jesus is baptized and then tested in the wilderness; Israel experiences the Exodus and then is tested in the desert; the remnant receives Cyrus's decree and then faces hardship in rebuilding. "One constant piece is hardship."[^10] The notes apply this to rebuilding after addiction, divorce, a career change, or a health crisis.[^11] What are you trying to rebuild right now, and how does it help, or not help, to know that hardship is part of the pattern rather than a sign you are on the wrong path?

</div>

### Two Leaders, One Problem <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#two-leaders-one-problem}

*Ezra and Nehemiah face the same problem and handle it in completely different ways.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Ezra 10:1–12 and Nehemiah 13:23–27 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** According to the notes, Ezra led with "encouragement, tact, and teamwork," calling town hall meetings, creating lists, and caring about people's feelings and buy-in.[^12] Nehemiah, by contrast, wrote: "I rebuked them and called curses down on them. I beat some of the men and pulled out their hair."[^13] Compare the two passages. What do you notice about how each leader learns of the problem, how he responds, and how the people react?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say the intermarriage issue was "Not primarily about ethnic purity but about preserving cultural/religious identity." Genealogy mattered for re-establishing tribal identity, and Nehemiah notes that half the children "spoke the language of Ashdod...and did not know how to speak the language of Judah."[^14] The notes connect the chain: "Language loss meant cultural loss meant loss of covenant identity."[^15] Thinking back to the renamed young men in Daniel, why might a fragile, returning community have felt this threat so sharply?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes say both leaders addressed the same issue and both were blessed by God.[^16] They also add a caution: Nehemiah lived 2,500 years ago, in what the notes call a "different period of human consciousness," and his example is "not a model for abusive leadership today."[^17] The notes raise the question themselves: "What is the difference between prophetic confrontation and abusive control?"[^18] Many of us have seen leadership go well and go badly in churches, workplaces, or families. Without naming anyone, what have you seen that separates bold confrontation that helped people from control that harmed them? What does that suggest for how you lead?

</div>

### Leading as You Were Made <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#leading-as-you-were-made}

*The episode's main point is not which leader was right, but that each led as God made him.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Ezra 7:10 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes say Ezra 7:10 describes Ezra as devoted to studying, observing, and teaching God's law, a three-step process: "pursue/study, model/observe, teach."[^19] The Talmud says he had such passion for the law "that if Moses had not given the law on Mount Sinai, Ezra would have given the law here."[^20] Zerubbabel, meanwhile, was the pioneer willing to go first into the unknown, and Nehemiah led with "Elijah-like fire and chutzpah."[^21] What do you notice about the order of the three verbs in Ezra 7:10? Which of the three leaders do you most naturally resemble, and why?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes insist that passion is non-negotiable but takes different forms. Nehemiah's is easy to see; Ezra's showed in daily perseverance; Zerubbabel's in his willingness to "mount up the troops and head back home into the unknown."[^22] "Patience with slow processes is a form of passion."[^23] Where in your life is passion showing up as quiet perseverance rather than bold action? How does it change things to call that passion?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes are direct: "You are a leader of something." It may be a business, a classroom, a family, or a younger person who watches you.[^24] "If you're a Nehemiah, we need you to be a Nehemiah," and "If you're an Ezra, we need you to be an Ezra." In a "weird, disruptive time," whatever comes next will come through "people doing the one thing that God put you here to do."[^25] What are you a leader of right now, and what would it look like to lead it this week as the person God created you to be?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The return from exile was not a parade. It came in waves, with ordinary people, slow letters to a foreign king, opposition from neighbors, and people losing heart. Zerubbabel went first into the unknown, Ezra shepherded with patience and teaching, and Nehemiah confronted with fire. They could hardly have been more different, yet God blessed each one, because each worked as God had made him to work. Rebuilding anything always brings hardship, and it always needs passionate people willing to lead as themselves.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, finish this: "I am a leader of ___, and this week I will lead it by ___."

</div>
