### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We watched Esau run to embrace Jacob and heard the hosts call that forgiveness true *mishpat*, justice that puts things back in their proper place. Jacob ended his life wrestling between the names Jacob and Israel.[^1]

**This week:** We turn to Jacob's sons and a question the rabbis argued about for centuries: who actually sold Joseph to Egypt, and what was the brothers' real sin?[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode starts from a detail most readers pass over. When the brothers later stand accused in Egypt, they confess only that they did not listen to Joseph's pleading; they say nothing about selling him.[^3] That gap, along with a Hebrew "they" in Genesis 37:28 that does not name the brothers, opened a long rabbinic conversation among Rashi, Rambam (Maimonides) and Ramban (Nachmanides).[^4]

Ramban moves the question from who sold Joseph to what the brothers bought with the silver: sandals, a hint pointing to Amos 2:6.[^5] The episode closes with a cliffhanger: Joseph never knew his brothers had told Jacob he was dead, so he may have assumed his father knew where he was and never came.[^6]

### A Confession That Leaves Something Out <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#a-confession-that-leaves-something-out}

*The episode models a Jewish way of reading that keeps every textual detail in play instead of smoothing it away.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 42:21 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The brothers say: "Surely we are being punished because of our brother. We saw how distressed he was when he pleaded with us for his life but we would not listen."[^3] What do they confess, and what do they leave out? What might that suggest about what they knew?

*Read Genesis 37:12–36 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes trace three plans: kill Joseph and blame a wild animal; Reuben's change to leave him in the cistern (while secretly planning to rescue him); and Judah's change to sell him "for profit (20 shekels of silver)."[^7] They also point out that the Hebrew of verse 28 simply says "they pulled Joseph up," where the NIV adds "his brothers."[^4] As you listened, which groups did you hear named (brothers, Ishmaelites, Midianites), and where does the story get hard to follow?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Rashi says the brothers sold Joseph to Ishmaelites who resold him without the brothers knowing; Rambam says Midianites found Joseph while the brothers were away negotiating; Ramban asks a different question altogether.[^4] The notes contrast this with "Western apologetics," which "often tries to harmonize difficulties by dismissing details."[^8] Which reading do you find most convincing, and what is it like to let several readings stand side by side?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Our traditions have taught us different habits for handling a passage that seems not to add up. When you hit a detail in Scripture that does not fit, what is your first instinct, and where did you learn it? What might change if you treated that detail as an invitation rather than a problem?

</div>

### Sandals and a Cry from the Pit <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#sandals-and-a-cry-from-the-pit}

*Ramban shifts the focus from the mechanics of the sale to what it revealed about the brothers' hearts.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Amos 2:6 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Ramban's question is "what the brothers bought with the 20 shekels of silver: sandals," which the notes call a *remez* ("hint/allusion") to Amos 2:6.[^5] What do you notice in Amos's words, and what changes about Genesis 37 when you hear the two passages together?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes conclude that the brothers "saw him as a variable to be manipulated rather than a person to be valued."[^9] Judah's plan, "Don't let him die for nothing," was framed as the merciful option.[^7] How can a choice that looks like mercy still treat a person as a variable? Where do you see that pattern in the world today?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The brothers read their trouble in Egypt as *middah keneged middah* ("measure for measure"): they ignored Joseph's cries, and now an Egyptian official ignores theirs.[^10] The notes connect their failure to hear Joseph to "the broader biblical theme of God hearing the cry of the oppressed."[^10] Why do you think the brothers' confession is about not listening rather than about the money?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes ask how we might "sell the poor for sandals" by "prioritizing our comfort over others' dignity."[^11] What is one comfort in your own life that could make it easier not to hear someone's cry? What would it take to hear it this week?

</div>

### The Letter Joseph Never Sent <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#the-letter-joseph-never-sent}

*The episode ends by asking us to see the story from inside the pit, and from Egypt.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** Joseph does not know his brothers showed Jacob the bloodied robe. From his side, his father "would know he was missing," "loved him and would come rescue him," but "never came."[^6] How would that belief shape a young man over years in Egypt? What does it help explain about why he never sent word home?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> As firstborn, Reuben tried to save Joseph, but the notes say his approach failed "because he doesn't confront the other brothers directly."[^12] Have you ever assumed someone knew about a hurt and simply chose not to help, or held back from speaking up yourself because you hoped to fix things quietly? Share only what you are comfortable sharing. What might it look like to speak directly this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The brothers' confession leaves something out, and the rabbis refused to let that detail go. Their conversation over centuries shows a way of reading that wrestles with every word. Ramban's answer cuts deepest: the sin was not mainly a transaction but a failure to see Joseph as a person, to hear his cry from the pit. And Joseph, far away in Egypt, may have spent years believing his father simply never came.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, whose cry would you like to listen for more carefully in the coming week?

</div>
