### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Reed Dent taught us that "All facts are truthful, but not all truth is factual," and invited us to read the Bible as a library of genres with the eyes of the heart.[^1]

**This week:** Dr. J. R. Briggs talks about failure, about the both/and space where heaven and earth meet, and about slowing down with Scripture.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

J. R. Briggs is founder and director of Kairos Partnerships. He started the Epic Fail Pastors' Conference, a gathering where pastors talked honestly about failure instead of celebrating success, and he describes failure as "a beautiful gift wrapped in ugly wrapping paper."[^3] His more recent work, *The Sacred Overlap*, argues that Jesus consistently lived in both/and tensions while Western Christianity tends to default to either/or thinking, and that faithful followers will be "too pagan for our Christian friends and too Christian for our pagan friends."[^4]

The conversation also covers writing as a discipline and the practice Briggs started of copying the Gospels by hand, ten verses a day, which helps readers notice details they miss at reading speed.[^5]

### Opening <span class="guide-time">10 min</span> {#opening}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-opening" markdown="1">

**Opening Question:** Think of something you worked hard on that did not turn out the way you hoped (a project, a plan, a skill). Looking back, what did it give you that success might not have? Share only what you are comfortable sharing.

### A Gift in Ugly Wrapping Paper <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#a-gift-in-ugly-wrapping-paper}

*Briggs begins with failure, because he believes grace is hardest to receive exactly there.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The Epic Fail Pastors' Conference met in a former church building that "had failed and become a bar," and it ended with a communion experience that "became profoundly meaningful."[^3] What makes that setting and that ending fit a gathering about failure? What does the communion table say that a success story cannot?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** Briggs notes that churches "lack safe spaces" to discuss failure, and that professional Christians who are "paid to love Jesus" especially forget that grace is available to them.[^3] Why do you think churches find it easier to celebrate success than to talk about failure? What would a genuinely safe space for failure need?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Briggs models his own advice. He says his first book is out of print and "probably should have been," and as a perfectionist he reminds himself, "None of it is perfect."[^6] He also says, "The muse shows up when your butt is in the seat."[^7] Where does perfectionism keep you from starting, finishing or sharing something? What would showing up anyway look like for you this month?

</div>

### Heaven and Earth Overlapping <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#heaven-and-earth-overlapping}

*The overlap Briggs describes starts with the prayer Jesus taught.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 6:9–13 aloud slowly.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Briggs calls "Your kingdom come, your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven" "fundamentally a prayer of overlap."[^8] Read the prayer again quietly. What words or pairs in it hold heaven and earth, or God and daily life, together? Which line do you think we pray most carelessly?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes list both/and tensions Jesus lived and taught: fully God and fully man, justice and mercy, spirit and truth, the Kingdom near and the Kingdom here, old treasures and new.[^4] Briggs calls Western either/or thinking "anemic." Which of these pairs do you think your tradition has tended to lean to one side of, and what has that emphasis protected? What might be gained by holding both?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe the early church as attractive because it was peculiar rather than normal or bizarre: caring for the sick during plagues, valuing women, children and slaves as image-bearers, and refusing "both emperor worship and insular separatism."[^9] Which of these strikes you most, and why might it have made outsiders curious?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Briggs says faithful followers of Jesus will be "too pagan for our Christian friends and too Christian for our pagan friends," and that being peculiar means sitting between "too normal" and "too weird."[^4] Which way do you tend to drift? What would peculiar look like in one ordinary setting of your week?

</div>

### Slowing Down with the Text <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#slowing-down-with-the-text}

*Copying Scripture by hand is a simple way to read slowly enough to notice.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Luke 19:1–10 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** While copying Luke, Briggs noticed that Zacchaeus wanted to "see" Jesus, "not necessarily talk to him."[^5] If you copied this passage during the week, what did you notice? Whether you did or not, look again at verses 3–5. What changes in the story when Zacchaeus's goal is only to see, and Jesus is the one who speaks?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> During a semester in Jerusalem, Briggs learned alongside a rabbi, a Muslim shopkeeper, and "modern-day Pharisees" who knew more of the New Testament than he did, and this challenged his "bento box" approach to faith, where everything stays neatly separated.[^10] Where do you keep faith in its own compartment, apart from the rest of your life? What is one practice that could help those compartments overlap?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> J. R. Briggs invites us to stop hiding our failures, because grace is fully available exactly there. He reads Jesus as someone who lived in the overlap: justice and mercy, God and man, heaven and earth, and he taught us to pray for that overlap every time we say "on earth as it is in heaven." People who live there will seem a little too pagan to some and a little too Christian to others. Slowing down with the text, even ten verses at a time, is one way to start noticing where that overlap already is.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: where in your week do you most want to see heaven and earth overlap?

</div>
