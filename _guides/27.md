### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** The Hebrew word for desert, *midbar*, grows from the same root as *dabar*, "word," so the desert is the place where God's people learn to listen and become "people of the ears" rather than "people of the eyes." The desert shepherd leads with the voice, never the stick, and the desert provides "just enough."[^1]

**This week:** We meet two desert trees. The small rotem bush gives barely enough shade for one person, and the acacia can look dead for years before it bursts to life when the water comes.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The second "Images of the Desert" episode turns from the shepherd to the plants that shape desert life. The rotem (broom tree) is a small, solitary bush that often shades only a person's upper body, and it shows up where people are desperate, as when Elijah lies under one after Mount Carmel. Marty and Brent trace shade through Scripture (Psalm 121, Isaiah 30 and 32 among others) and land on a double calling: God is our shade, and God also calls each of us to be shade for others. Marty describes how this freed him from trying to be an "oak tree of shade" with all the answers when he sat with grieving people.

The acacia, which Bedouin call the "gift of the desert," can stay dormant for seven to twelve years and then spring to life after a flood. Following the biblical botanist Noga Hareuveni, the episode reads the tree of Psalm 1 as an acacia, which changes what "fruit in season" means. Along the way we meet *hagah*, the Hebrew word translated "meditate," pictured as a lion's low growl over its prey.

### The Rotem: Just Enough Shade <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#the-rotem-just-enough-shade}

*The rotem is small, but in the desert a small shade can be the difference between going on and giving up.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read 1 Kings 19:1–8 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes describe the rotem as a small, solitary bush, often two or three feet tall, giving enough shade for one person's upper body. They connect it with biblical figures in desperate moments, and Elijah lying under the rotem wanting to die is the clearest example.[^3] As you listen to the passage, what do you notice about Elijah's condition, what he asks for, and what he actually receives? How does picturing a bush that barely covers him change the scene for you?

*Read Psalm 121:5–6 and Isaiah 30:1–3 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes list these among many passages that use shade as a picture of refuge, including a warning against seeking shade in Egypt instead of in God.[^4] What difference do you hear between the shade God offers in Psalm 121 and "Egypt's shade" in Isaiah 30? What do people tend to run to for shade when life gets hot?

*Read Isaiah 32:1–2 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes read Isaiah 32 as describing one king who reigns in righteousness and many rulers who rule with justice, with each one becoming a shelter and shade. They connect the many rulers to believers as a kingdom of priests: God provides shade for us, and also calls us to be shade for others.[^5] Two weeks ago we heard Leviticus describe a whole nation of priests. What does it mean to you that the calling to be shade is given to "each one," and not only to the king or to a few gifted people?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Marty describes how he used to dread hospital visits and funerals because he felt he had to be an "oak tree of shade" with every answer. He learned instead to offer "rotem bush shade," which is presence without needing to solve everything.[^6] Think of someone in a desert season right now. What would offering rotem shade, rather than oak-tree shade, look like with that person this week?

</div>

### The Acacia: Fruit in Its Season <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#the-acacia-fruit-in-its-season}

*The acacia looks dead for years, yet it is the tree the desert depends on.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The acacia grows at the ends of wadis, where flash-flood waters spread out. Bedouin call it "the gift of the desert" because it gives shade, strong building wood, medicinal sap and pods for livestock. It can remain dormant for seven to twelve years, looking completely dead, and then spring to life when enough water arrives.[^7] Which of these details surprises you most, and why might a desert people prize a tree that so often looks lifeless?

*Read Psalm 1:1–3 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Following Noga Hareuveni, the notes identify the tree in Psalm 1 (and Jeremiah 17) as an acacia. The "streams of water" are the rushing wadi floods, and "fruit in season" means fruit comes when conditions are right, perhaps once in a decade.[^8] The word translated "meditates" in verse 2 is *hagah*, which the notes describe as an onomatopoeic word for the low growl of a lion over its prey: not passive contemplation but intense, possessive devotion.[^9] Read verses 2 and 3 slowly. Which words point to timing rather than constant productivity, and how does a lion growling over its food compare with the way you have usually pictured meditating on Scripture?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** If the blessed person's fruit may come only once in ten years, while the growling over God's word goes on day and night through the dry years, how does that reshape what it means to be "blessed"? What does this reading free you from, and what does it ask of you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Marty tells of a campus team at Bethel University that worked for years with no visible results until a "flash flood" brought fifty people to a Bible study, and of Mitch in Florida, who waited two and a half years alone before a dozen people joined in two weeks.[^10] Where in your life right now does something feel dormant (a relationship, a ministry, your own faith), and what practice will you keep up through the dry season so that you are ready when the water comes?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#closing}

> The desert gives us two trees to live by. The rotem reminds us that God's provision often comes as "just enough," and that we do not need to be oak trees to be shade for someone else; a small presence at someone's right hand can help them take the next step. The acacia reminds us that a season of apparent death is not the end of the story. The person who growls over God's word through the dry years is the one ready to bear fruit when the flood finally comes.[^11]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: which tree do you need to be this week, the rotem for someone else or the acacia in a dry season of your own?

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 28 — Images of the Desert — Ar'ar and Tamarisk](/guides/28)** (28:16): Two more desert plants. Jeremiah 17 sets the ar'ar bush, beautiful outside but empty and toxic inside, against the acacia, which looks dead but holds life. Then the tamarisk, a tree that takes 80 years to mature, explains why Abraham's planting one at Beersheba is a bold statement of trust, and leaves us with the rabbinic question, "How many tamarisk trees did you plant today?"[^12]

**What to bring next week:**
- Read Jeremiah 17:5–8 and Genesis 21:22–34. In the Genesis passage, mark one detail you would normally skim past.
- If you can, offer "rotem bush shade" to one person this week, and come ready to say (without names) what it was like.
