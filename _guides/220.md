### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Becky Solomon joined Marty to talk about *ezer k'negdo* ("the help that opposes"), being *kadosh* ("set apart") without becoming oppressive, and choosing grace for each season of life over rigid religion.[^1]

**This week:** We begin Marty and Brent's commentary on *The Chosen*, a crowdfunded series about the life of Jesus. Its first episode introduces Mary Magdalene, Nicodemus, Simon Peter and Matthew, and ends with Jesus calling Mary by name with the words of Isaiah 43.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The series opens with a disclaimer that it adds backstories, dialogue and combined timelines, and a messianic rabbi consultant described it as "Christian midrash on the gospels," exploring what might lie between the lines without replacing the text.[^3] The hosts praise its historical detail and its portrayal of Mary's condition, which blurs spiritual oppression, trauma and mental illness rather than offering a simple "movie demon."[^4]

Nicodemus emerges as a sincere teacher who wonders whether Torah might be like a "blurry reflection" and asks, "When did Shabbat become theater?"[^5] Peter is portrayed as desperate under tax debt (a choice Marty dislikes), and Matthew as wealthy and despised.[^6] The episode ends with Jesus entering quietly, placing his hand on Mary's, and speaking the Scripture her father taught her: "Fear not, for I have redeemed you. I have called you by name. You are mine."[^7] Throughout, the show is a lens; our focus stays on the biblical text behind it.

### Reading Between the Lines <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#reading-between-the-lines}

*The hosts treat the series as imaginative storytelling that serves Scripture rather than replacing it.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** For the last several weeks we have studied Jewish midrash on Genesis, readings that fill gaps in the text with story. The notes say midrash explores "what might lie between the lines of Scripture - not replacing the text but illuminating it through plausible narrative expansion," and Marty says the show succeeds because it embraces being "a fictional dramatic presentation."[^3] How is a "Christian midrash" on screen like the rabbinic midrash we have been reading, and how is it different?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes raise the question of how we "enjoy creative biblical storytelling while maintaining Scripture's unique authority."[^8] When a film, sermon illustration or novel fills in a Bible story, what helps you enjoy it while keeping the text itself first? Where have you seen an imagined detail end up treated as if it were Scripture?

</div>

### Nicodemus, Peter and the Red Quarter <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#nicodemus-peter-and-the-red-quarter}

*The episode sets a sincere religious leader and a desperate fisherman inside systems that make faithfulness hard.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read John 3:1–2 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The Gospel introduces Nicodemus in a few words. In the show, he is a "teacher of teachers" who cannot help Mary, and his calls on angels and patriarchs have "no power here," which sets up the point that true deliverance "was understood as something only God could accomplish."[^5][^4] When he tells his wife Zohara that Torah might be like a "blurry reflection," she answers, "Never utter those things in public" and "That might even be blasphemy."[^5] What does the Gospel's brief description tell you about Nicodemus, and what does the show's backstory add?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Nicodemus asks, "When did Shabbat become theater?"[^5] The notes apply the question to "church services designed to impress rather than transform," "religious leaders who can't ask honest questions publicly" and "communities that prioritize certainty over wrestling with doubt."[^9] Every tradition, including the ones in this room, can drift into performance. Without pointing at anyone else's church, where do you see that drift most easily in communities like yours, and what helps resist it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty observes, "It's not easy to live in the Red Quarter and follow cleanliness law. It's not easy to live in the Red Quarter and be Torah observant."[^10] The show's Peter faces tax debt that could cost him his boat, and when he claims lives are at stake to justify fishing on Shabbat, Andrew answers, "Lives are not at stake here."[^6] The notes add that the historical plausibility of fishing on Shabbat "remains debated." How does economic pressure change the way we judge other people's faithfulness?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes warn against communities that "condemn the poor for moral failures while supporting economic structures that perpetuate poverty."[^10] Where might you be holding someone to a standard that your own security makes easier for you to keep? What is one way you could respond with understanding this week?

</div>

### I Have Called You by Name <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#i-have-called-you-by-name}

*Jesus's first appearance is quiet, and it is built on words from Isaiah.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 43:1–7 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Listen for who is speaking, to whom, and what is promised. The show places the words "Fear not, for I have redeemed you. I have called you by name. You are mine" in the mouth of Mary's father when she is a child, and then in Jesus's when he finds her.[^7] What do you notice in Isaiah's words, first spoken to Israel, that makes them fit Mary's story?

*Read Luke 8:1–3 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Luke tells us only a little about Mary Magdalene. The notes caution against "reducing her to merely her deliverance."[^11] The show's Jesus "enters quietly," "gently places his hand on hers," and "speaks her name with tenderness," which Brent contrasts with a booming television entrance.[^7] Why might gentleness and naming be a truer picture of Jesus's power than a dramatic display?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty dislikes reading Jesus's knowledge of Mary through "God goggles," the assumption that Jesus simply knew everything because he was God. He prefers to see Jesus as "a person of deep prayer who listened to God's voice," with prophetic gifting that does not negate his humanity.[^12] What difference does it make to you to picture Jesus knowing Mary that way?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes suggest "that being known is often more healing than being fixed."[^13] Who in your life needs to be known more than fixed right now? What would it look like to learn their story, or to speak a word of Scripture over them, this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> *The Chosen* begins by imagining what might lie between the lines of the Gospels, the way midrash does, while keeping Scripture first. Nicodemus wonders when Shabbat became theater, Peter is pressed by debt into compromise, and Matthew is wealthy and alone. Into Mary's darkness, Jesus comes without fanfare and speaks the words of Isaiah her father taught her. The God of Isaiah 43 still calls people by name and says, "You are mine."
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what would it mean for you to hear God say your name this week?

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 221 — The Chosen S1E2 — “Shabbat”](/guides/221)** (54:16): The second episode, which Marty calls potentially "one of the best episodes of a series that I have ever seen," uses Shabbat as a lens. It opens with a heartfelt Shabbat from 948 BC, reframes the Eshet Chayil of Proverbs 31 as a proclamation rather than a checklist, and ends with Jesus gathering outcasts at Mary's table, while presenting the Pharisees as devout people trapped by their own system rather than simple hypocrites.[^14]

**What to bring next week:** Read Proverbs 31:10–31 aloud once at home, imagining it spoken as a blessing over someone rather than a list of requirements.
