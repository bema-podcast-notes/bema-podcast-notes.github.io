### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Genesis 1 is a poem with sacred time at its center, written for freed slaves who needed to learn that their worth came from being God's good creation, not from their output.

**This week:** The garden story retells creation from a different angle. Marty and Brent suggest its center is nakedness and shame, and that bearing God's image includes knowing when to say "enough."

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Marty and Brent note that Genesis 2 does not pick up where Genesis 1 left off; it is "the same creation" told "from a different perspective." They point out details that are easy to miss: only the tree of life is placed in the middle of the garden, the woman is described as *ezer k'negdo* ("help that opposes"), and the serpent's temptation turns on what God really said.

The notes describe the story as a chiasm whose center is the moment "their eyes were opened and they realized they were naked." That makes shame, rather than disobedience alone, the heart of the story. The episode reads the image of God as including the capacity for self-restraint, and it hears God's question "Who told you that you were naked?" as a challenge to every voice that contradicts how God made us.

**Opening Question:** Think of a time you had "enough" of something good (food, money, praise, screen time) and stopped, or a time you did not stop. What made the difference?

### Two Trees and a Help That Opposes <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#two-trees-and-a-help-that-opposes}

*Small details in the garden story change what it seems to be about.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 2:8–9 and Genesis 3:1–3 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes say the Hebrew places the tree of life in the middle of the garden, and does not require the tree of knowledge to be there. A rabbinic tradition suggests that when Eve tells the serpent the forbidden tree is in the middle, it is "actually not in the middle, however, she has made it the center of HER garden."[^1] Compare the two passages you just heard. What does God say, and what does the woman say? What do you notice about where each of them puts the trees, and about what the woman adds or changes?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** God says it is not good for the man to be alone. The usual translation "helpmate" is, in the notes' words, "a bad translation." The Hebrew *ezer k'negdo* means "help" (*ezer*) that is "against or opposition" (*k'negdo*): "the help that opposes." The rabbis picture two planks leaning against each other; take one away and both fall, because "There is no plank that stands by itself."[^2] What kind of relationship does that image describe? How is "help that opposes" different from both "assistant" and "rival"?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes press the point further: woman is "not a subordinate creation," and "Only when male and female are together do you see humanity in its fullness. Not just in marriage."[^3] Our traditions have taught a range of things about men and women in the home and in the church, and this reading may sit easily with some of us and uneasily with others. What do you think each of our traditions is trying to protect in its teaching? What might this "help that opposes" reading add to that conversation, wherever you land?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q4:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Think of a person in your life whose honest disagreement has helped you more than easy agreement would have. What did their "opposition" make possible for you?

</div>

### Who Told You That You Were Naked? <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#who-told-you-that-you-were-naked}

*At the center of the story are opened eyes and the discovery of shame.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes describe the story as a chiasm with "their eyes were opened and they realized they were naked" at its center. That placement suggests "nakedness/shame is the story's focal point, not disobedience per se."[^4] Many of us learned this passage mainly as the story of the first sin. What shifts when you read it as a story about shame?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The serpent's temptation focuses on the word "say," questioning whether God really meant his command. It plants doubt about God's goodness and suggests he is "withholding something beneficial."[^5] The notes also point to a wordplay between "naked" (*arowm*) and "crafty" (*eruwm*).[^6] What does it look like in ordinary life to begin suspecting that God is holding out on us?

*Read Genesis 3:7–11 and Genesis 3:20–21 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Adam names his wife twice: first *ishah* ("woman"), focused on who she is, then Eve ("life-giver"), focused on what she can do. The notes connect this shift "from valuing essence to valuing function" with the shame that followed the fruit.[^7] Last week we talked about worth measured in bricks. Where do you see the same move from who someone is to what they can do, in your family, workplace, or church?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** God's question "Who told you that you were naked?" challenges voices that contradict how God created us.[^8] The notes add that the story ends "not with condemnation but with God meeting humanity in their shame, providing covering."[^9] What voices tell you that you are lacking or inadequate? What would it mean to hear God's question addressed to you?

</div>

### Knowing When to Say Enough <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#knowing-when-to-say-enough}

*Bearing God's image includes the divine capacity for restraint.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The episode reads the image of God as the capacity for self-control: "Animals always act on desires; humans can choose restraint." This links to God's own restraint in stopping on the seventh day, and to self-control as a fruit of the Spirit.[^10] The notes also mention that *El Shaddai* may mean "the God who knows when to say enough."[^11] How does it change the idea of self-control to think of it as something God does, rather than only something God demands?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes apply the principle of "enough" to "our consumer culture's constant message that we need more," from media to money to relationship boundaries.[^12] In which area of your life is "enough" hardest for you to say right now, and what is one way you could practice saying it this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The garden story retells creation through a different lens. A woman is formed as a help who opposes, and a tree is moved, in her telling, to the center of her garden. At the heart of the story is not only a broken command but the discovery of shame, and a God who comes looking and asks, "Who told you that you were naked?" To bear God's image is to share God's capacity to say "enough," and to trust God's first word about us: that we are good, loved, and have what we need.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one voice you want to stop listening to, or one "enough" you want to practice?

</div>
