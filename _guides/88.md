### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** John, the pastor to Asia, wrote for a grafted family of Jews and Gentiles, speaking to both at once through the *logos* and the seven "I am" statements. With him, all four Gospel voices have been introduced.

**This week:** Before the story of Jesus begins, Marty and Brent stand on Mount Arbel and look over the land where most of it happened.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The notes explain that about 80% of Jesus' recorded ministry took place around the Sea of Galilee, a region small enough to view from a single mountaintop.[^1] Within it lived five very different worldviews: religious Jews in the towns the notes call "the triangle" (Chorazin, Gennesaret, Capernaum and Bethsaida), Herodians in Herod Antipas' capital of Tiberias, Zealots at Gamla, pagans in the Decapolis on the eastern shore, and members of the priestly parties.[^2] A short walk could take someone from one world to another, and the archaeology shows the difference: the triangle had no paved roads or Greco-Roman art, while Tiberias and the Decapolis had all the marks of Hellenistic cities.[^3]

The episode argues that geography is an interpretive tool, not background scenery. It explains why Jesus' move to Capernaum was an act of courage, why crossing to "the other side" of the sea was a crisis of obedience for the disciples, and why Jesus told some people to spread the news while telling others to stay silent.[^4] Because the episode is short, this guide spends extra time reading Mark 4 and 5 with the map in mind.

### The View from Mount Arbel <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#the-view-from-mount-arbel}

*Seen from one mountaintop, the setting of the Gospels is small, crowded and charged.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Bring out the maps people found this week. The notes describe the five worldviews around the sea:[^5]

- The triangle (Chorazin, Gennesaret, Capernaum, Bethsaida): religiously observant Jews, the Pharisees' territory, who rejected Hellenism without withdrawing like the Essenes
- Tiberias: Herod Antipas' capital and the Herodians' compromise with Greco-Roman culture
- Gamla: the Zealot stronghold, east of Bethsaida
- Hippos (Susita) and the Decapolis: Greco-Roman pagan cities on the eastern shore
- The priestly parties: Sadducees and Essenes, centered elsewhere but present in the region

The notes say that in the triangle, "The choice to pave or not to pave was an ideological statement about cultural identity and theological conviction."[^6] Locate as many places as you can. What does seeing these worlds side by side, a short walk apart, change about how you picture Jesus' ministry?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say that when the Pharisees warned Jesus that Herod Antipas wanted to kill him, Jesus did not flee but made his home base in Capernaum, within Herod's reach. They put his stance this way: "I'm not running from you; I'm running toward you. Come find me if you want."[^7] Where are you tempted to move away from a hard place or person when faithfulness might mean moving toward it?

</div>

### To the Other Side <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#to-the-other-side}

*A short boat ride on a small lake becomes one of the boldest moves in the Gospels.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Mark 4:35–41 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes point out that Bethsaida was only a 20–30 minute walk along the shore, so a boat trip made sense only if the destination was truly "the other side," the pagan Decapolis.[^8] They report that Jews from the triangle regarded the Decapolis as so unclean that they called it "the faraway land" or "the land of the cast out ones," and that "No Jewish child from the religious triangle would ever be caught on this side of the sea."[^9] Who starts this trip in the passage, and what happens on the way? Read with that background, what might the disciples have been feeling before the storm ever began?

*Read Mark 5:1–20 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q4:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes locate the pigs' rush into the sea at the beach below Susita (Hippos), the one place on the lake where a steep bank runs directly into deep water.[^10] They add that the man living among tombs, the herd of pigs, and Jesus' willingness to go there all emphasize the Gospel's boundary-breaking nature.[^11] What details in this passage mark it as foreign, unclean territory for a Jewish listener? What does Jesus do there, and how do the local people respond?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes say that for Jewish fishermen this command "represented a profound crisis of obedience," and that "Jesus was not just teaching about breaking down barriers; he was forcing his disciples to physically cross those barriers."[^12] What boundary might Jesus be asking you, or your church, to physically cross rather than only talk about? What makes that crossing hard?

</div>

### Tell Everyone, Tell No One <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#tell-everyone-tell-no-one}

*Jesus' instructions after a miracle depend on where he is and who is listening.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Mark 5:18–20 and Mark 5:35–43 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes describe a consistent geographical pattern: in pagan territory Jesus told people to spread the news, while in the religious triangle he commanded silence. They are careful about why: "This was not because they were Jewish but because they were religious and trained, convinced they already understood Scripture correctly."[^13] Compare what Jesus tells the man in the Decapolis with what he tells the household of Jairus, a synagogue leader, after returning across the sea. What differences do you notice, and how might the notes' explanation fit them?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say Jesus knew the religious experts were not ready for what he brought; they would "get all wound up about stuff" he was not there to engage.[^14] In what part of your faith are you most like the trained listener who thinks the matter is already settled? Where are you more like the man from the Decapolis, ready to go home and tell what God has done?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> From the top of Mount Arbel almost the whole stage of Jesus' ministry comes into view: the plain synagogue towns of the triangle, Herod's gleaming Tiberias, the Zealot fortress at Gamla, and the pagan cities across the water. Jesus did not stay in one world. He set up his home under Herod's nose, took his disciples into the land they had been taught to fear, and spoke to each audience in the way it could hear. The notes even suggest the four Gospels can be pictured on this map: Matthew speaking to the triangle, Mark across the sea to the Decapolis, Luke to the triangle or Gamla, and John to Hellenistic Tiberias. The geography is not scenery. It is part of the message.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: which shore of the sea do you find yourself standing on most days, and where might Jesus be inviting you to cross?

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 89 — Written in the Stars](/guides/89)** (44:09): The story of Jesus begins with Matthew 1:18–2:12. The episode shows that the Christmas story is not a sanitized fairy tale but a narrative full of shame and social tension for Mary and Joseph, explores Joseph's decision to divorce Mary quietly, and introduces the Magi as Babylonian or Persian astrologers who knew Balaam's prophecy in Numbers 24:17.[^15] It also describes Mithraism as a fast-growing religion in the Roman world and asks how God works outside the boundaries we create.

**What to bring next week:** Read Matthew 1:18–2:12 and Numbers 24:17. Write down one detail of the Christmas story that you have always pictured a certain way, so you can test it against the text next week.
