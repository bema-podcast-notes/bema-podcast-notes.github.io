### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** David and Goliath turned out to be more than an underdog story: Saul sat paralyzed for forty days while a shepherd boy, driven by *kiddush hashem* (sanctifying God's name), was willing to "throw his stone."

**This week:** Before we go further into the kings, we step back to ask how this part of the Bible is put together. Two different sources tell the story of Israel's monarchy, and knowing which one you are reading changes how you read it.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Many readers get lost somewhere in 1 and 2 Samuel, 1 and 2 Kings, and 1 and 2 Chronicles. This episode offers a map. Samuel/Kings is described as "agenda-driven headlines from Israel," written close to the events, while Chronicles is a "documentary perspective from Judah," written centuries later with hindsight. Both are accurate; they simply serve different purposes.

The episode also argues that ancient Eastern history was "prophetic," told to compel readers to action rather than to record every detail with modern precision. It closes by noticing how the order of the Hebrew Bible (the *TaNaKh*) differs from the Christian Old Testament, and why it matters that one collection ends with hope and the other with a curse. Some of this may challenge how you were taught to think about biblical history, and that is a good thing to talk about honestly.

### Why These Books Feel Confusing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#why-these-books-feel-confusing}

*Before we fix the confusion, we name it.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes list four reasons readers struggle here: a large volume of short stories about many kings, confusion between the northern and southern kingdoms, apparent repetition (reading Samuel and Kings and then suddenly meeting Saul and David again in Chronicles), and the simple fact that many readers have never had the relationship between these books explained.[^1] The episode admits many people experience "glazed eyes" at this point.[^2] Which of these have you experienced, and how have you usually handled it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode first reviews the whole story as a conflict between two kingdoms. Empire "represents self-preservation, the order of death, oppressive systems," while Shalom "represents self-sacrifice, the order of life, redemptive partnership with God."[^3] Looking back over the weeks we have studied, from Joshua at the crossroads to the redemption cycle of Judges to Ruth, where has that conflict been clearest for you? Where do you notice the same pull between self-preservation and self-sacrifice in your own week?

</div>

### Headlines and Documentary <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#headlines-and-documentary}

*Two tellings of the same history, each shaped by when and where it was written.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The episode uses an analogy. Samuel/Kings is like the newspaper headlines written during the Vietnam War, while Chronicles is like a Netflix documentary about the war made in the last decade. "Both are accurate, but the passage of time dramatically affects what is emphasized and how the story is told."[^4] What can a headline see that a documentary cannot? What can a documentary see that a headline cannot?

*Read 1 Samuel 17:41–50 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes say ancient Eastern history "Tells accurate details in a way that stirs and moves readers" and is "Less concerned with precision than with compelling readers to action." The episode uses David and Goliath as its example: the question "isn't primarily about historical accuracy but about what message the story is meant to convey to its audience."[^5] As you listened, which details seemed to be there to stir you rather than simply to inform you? What action might the storyteller have wanted to provoke in its first readers?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes insist that both sources are accurate, and that "accuracy doesn't require modern precision - the message matters most."[^6] Our traditions have taught us to think about the Bible's truthfulness in different ways, and this framework may feel freeing to some and unsettling to others. Without trying to settle the question for everyone, what in this way of describing biblical history rings true to you, and what makes you want to ask more questions?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> One application in the notes is to ask of any biblical history "not just 'what happened?' but 'what is God saying through how this is told?'"[^7] Think about how you tell the story of your own faith. If someone wrote it as "prophetic history," what would they want it to stir in the people who hear it?

</div>

### Where the Story Ends <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#where-the-story-ends}

*The shape of a collection, and especially its last word, teaches us how to read it.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** *TaNaKh* is an acronym for the three parts of the Hebrew Bible: Torah (Law), *Nevi'im* (Prophets), and *Ketuvim* (Writings). Samuel and Kings sit in the Prophets, "indicating they are meant to be read as prophetic history," while Chronicles sits in the Writings and concludes the whole Hebrew Bible.[^8] What changes for you if you think of Samuel and Kings as books of the prophets rather than simply as history books?

*Read 2 Chronicles 36:22–23 aloud, then read Malachi 4:4–6 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** These are the last words of the Hebrew Bible and the last words of the Christian Old Testament. The notes say the Hebrew Bible "closes with hope and God's promise to restore His people," while the Christian Old Testament ends with Malachi, "which closes with a curse."[^9] Listen to the final sentence of each. What do you notice about the mood, the direction, and the invitation each one leaves with the reader?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes go further. They say the Christian reorganization was "Supposedly arranged by 'genre'" but "coincided with rampant anti-Semitism," and that ending with Malachi "sets up a Christian worldview where Judaism is failed and cursed, awaiting Jesus as the solution."[^10] This is a strong claim, and the group does not need to agree on it. How might the last page of a collection shape the way its readers think about the people whose story it tells?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** Chronicles was written with long hindsight, and it chose to end on a promise of restoration.[^11] Think about a hard chapter in your own life that you now see with some distance. If you told that chapter today, would it end with a curse or with hope, and why?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The books of the kings are not a confusing pile of repeats. They are two witnesses to one history: the urgent headlines of Samuel/Kings from Israel, and the reflective documentary of Chronicles from Judah, written centuries later. Both are accurate, and both are prophetic, told not just to inform us but to move us. And the Hebrew Bible, in its own order, ends not with a curse but with hope and a promise of return. As we read the kings in the weeks ahead, we can ask not only "what happened?" but "what is God saying through how this is told?"
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what question will you bring with you the next time you read a story about one of Israel's kings?

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 41 — The Story Behind the Story](/guides/41)** (34:15): The two sources are laid side by side for the reigns of Saul, David, and Solomon. Chronicles leaves out David's sin with Bathsheba entirely and points instead to David's request to build a temple and to Solomon's accumulation of horses, wives, silver, and gold. The episode argues that the "story behind the story" is a lust for Empire that leads to moral failure, not the other way around.
