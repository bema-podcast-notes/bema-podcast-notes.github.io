### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Noah planted a vineyard, and his story after the flood echoed Eden: fruit, nakedness, a covering, and a curse. Following the midrash, Noah answered Ham's violation by cursing Canaan, a curse that landed on the wrong person and showed how vengeance spreads beyond its target.

**This week:** The Tower of Babel. The builders want to make a name for themselves and are afraid of being scattered, and God's scattering turns out to be less a slap on the hand than a new project that will teach humanity to work together.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This episode reads Genesis 10:32–11:9 closely as Hebrew literature. The story is a chiasm (a mirrored structure) in which a set of Hebrew consonants repeats in one order and then reverses halfway through, with the fear of being "scattered all over the earth" at the center.[^1] The hosts also trace a pattern of people moving east, away from where God intended them to be, and they connect Babel to Nimrod and to the story of Cain.[^2]

Two ideas carry most of the discussion. First, the technology of brick-making is not the problem; the problem is what the builders decide to do with it.[^3] Second, the confusion of languages is presented as strategy rather than punishment: if humanity wants to accomplish great things, it will have to learn other people's languages, and that requires humility and care for others.[^4] Expect to talk about technology, pride, and the hard work of understanding people who are not like us.

### Moving East <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#moving-east}

*The way the story is built, and the direction its people travel, both point to what it is about.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 10:32–11:9 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes say this story is a chiasm. The Hebrew consonants n-b-l-h repeat through the first half and then appear in reverse order in the second half, and there is language of scattering at the beginning (10:32), at the end (11:8–9), and in the middle (11:4).[^5] We cannot see the consonants in English, but we can see the scattering. As you listened, where did you hear words about scattering, spreading out, or the whole earth? What do you notice about who is afraid of scattering and who actually does the scattering?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The hosts point out that Westerners usually miss a pattern: Adam and Eve go east when they leave the garden, Cain and his family head east, and after the flood people head east again. The notes put it this way: "People continue to move farther and farther away from where G-d has intended for them to be."[^6] In this framework, east stands for the past and regression, and west for the future and God's design.[^7] Look again at 11:2. How does reading the direction of travel as a direction of the heart change the way the story begins?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode traces evil growing more organized across Genesis: an individual choice in the garden, violence within a family with Cain and Abel, a whole society before the flood, and at Babel an organized civilization.[^8] The notes connect Babel to Nimrod, the first *gibor* (mighty warrior or hunter), whose kingdom included Babel in Shinar and whose name means "rebel."[^9] Where have you seen a wrong that started as one person's choice become part of how a group, a workplace, or a system simply operates? What made it hard to see once it was organized?

</div>

### Bricks and a Name <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#bricks-and-a-name}

*The builders' tools are not the problem. What they want the tools to do for them is.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 11:3–4 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The episode uses a Hebrew narrative principle: when characters speak, act, and then the text says "and they said" again, a pause has passed in the story. On this reading, the people invent brick-making and God says nothing; God speaks only when the technology is put toward a prideful purpose.[^10] (The note-taker is honest about having questions about how far this principle goes.[^11]) Look at the two "they said" statements in verses 3 and 4. What is the first one about, what is the second one about, and what changed between them?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes draw a line from Babel back to Cain. Cain was cursed to wander but built a city and named it after his son; Babel's builders feared being scattered, wanted to "make a name for [themselves]," and were scattered anyway.[^12] *Read Genesis 4:16–17 aloud.* What do these two stories share in what people are afraid of and in how they try to protect themselves?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes apply the brick to modern tools like the internet, social media, and artificial intelligence: morally neutral in themselves, with the ethical question arising in how they are used.[^13] They also suggest that churches, businesses, governments, and nonprofits can drift into "making a name" for themselves instead of serving others.[^14] Think of a tool or an institution you care about, perhaps even your own church. What would it look like for it to serve people, and what would it look like for it to start building a name?

</div>

### Scattered for Our Good <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#scattered-for-our-good}

*God's response to Babel can be read as a setup for success rather than a punishment.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 11:5–9 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes record a teaching attributed to Fohrman: "In the confusion of Babel, G-d has not so much slapped our hands as he has given us a new redemptive project that will cause us to be the people that grows into the kind of humanity that bears his image, a humanity that knows when to say enough; a people that trusts the story, a people that just might find a place of rest."[^15] As you read verses 5–9 again, what in the text supports reading the scattering as a project instead of a penalty? What, if anything, makes that reading hard for you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The same teaching says, "You cannot learn the language of another culture or a people without learning something about their perspective," and that learning a diversity of perspectives "requires a sense of learning how to control one's desires in order to reach a common goal together."[^16] The notes sum up God's message this way: "if you want to succeed, you're going to have to learn how to work together."[^17] Our group comes from more than one church tradition, each with its own vocabulary and instincts. What is one "word" from your tradition that others might misunderstand, and what has helped you understand a tradition that is not your own?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes apply Babel to political polarization and tribalism: when groups refuse to learn each other's "languages," they stay scattered and cannot build anything good together.[^18] They describe learning a language as an act of humility: admitting what you do not know and depending on others.[^19] Where do you find it hardest to learn someone else's language right now, and what would the first humble step look like?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes describe the kind of people God is forming as "A selfless group of people who knows how to put other ahead of self."[^20] Name one person or group whose "language" you could begin learning this month. What is one concrete thing you could do to listen before you speak?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The Tower of Babel begins with people moving east, inventing something new, and deciding to use it to make a name for themselves before they can be scattered. God does not object to the bricks. God objects to a humanity settling into a project of pride, and the scattering that follows works less like a punishment than an invitation. To accomplish anything great now, people will have to learn one another's languages, and that takes humility, patience, and care for someone besides ourselves. The story closes the door on one way of being human and quietly holds another one open.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one "tower" you are tempted to build, or one "language" you want to learn?

</div>
