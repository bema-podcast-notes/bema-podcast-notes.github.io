### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We watched the tests of the soul (*nephesh*) and of "very" (*meod*) on the road to Sinai, and saw a God who takes the blow at the rock for his people and a community measured by where it places the vulnerable.

**This week:** Israel finally arrives at Mount Sinai, and Marty reads the whole scene through the steps of an ancient Eastern wedding.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Marty walks through the customs of an ancient Eastern wedding: the betrothal cup, the groom leaving to prepare a room in his father's house, his arrival at an unknown hour, the bride's consecration, the sound of the *shofar*, the couple standing under the *chuppah*, the presentation of the *ketubah*, the gifts, and a honeymoon year. He then lays those steps over Israel's story, from Abram leaving his father's house to the cloud on the mountain, and suggests that Sinai is less a courtroom than a wedding.

Two moments carry the most weight. The first is the Ten Commandments heard as wedding vows, a *ketubah* from a husband to his bride. The second is the golden calf, which Marty describes as unfaithfulness in the middle of the ceremony, followed by a God who chooses to start over. Marty is honest that no single ancient source packages all of these customs together, though he says each part can be verified separately.

### A Wedding at the Mountain <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#a-wedding-at-the-mountain}

*The arrival at Sinai follows the steps of an ancient Eastern wedding.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Marty lists the steps of an ancient Eastern wedding and then maps Israel's story onto them: betrothal (Abram told to leave his father's house), the groom leaving to prepare the house (the time in Egypt), the arrival of the bridegroom (Passover), the bride consecrated, the *shofar* (a ram's-horn trumpet) sounded, the couple gathered under the *chuppah* (a canopy symbolizing God's presence), the presentation of the *ketubah* (the wedding contract), consummation, gifts, and a honeymoon year.[^1]

*Read Exodus 19:1–19 aloud.*

As you listen, mark every detail that fits one of those wedding steps. What do you notice? Which parallel seems strongest to you, and which seems like more of a stretch?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes record that in Exodus 19:5–6, "my treasured possession" is wedding talk.[^2] They also observe that Israel camps "against" the mountain, using the word *k'negdo* (against, opposite), the same term used for Eve as a suitable partner.[^3] What changes for you when the language of Sinai turns out to be the language of partnership and belonging rather than of a ruler and subjects?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** In the betrothal, the groom offers a cup of wine and says, "This is the cup of a new covenant that I make with you today. I will not drink of this cup again until I drink it with you in my father's house." Then he waits for the bride to drink. The notes add, "There is no record of a bride refusing the cup."[^4] Marty connects this to Jesus' words at the Last Supper, to "I go to prepare a place for you" in John 14, and to no one but the Father knowing the hour.[^5] How does hearing those sayings as a groom's words change the way you hear them? Where in your own life do you feel invited, rather than ordered, into relationship with God?

</div>

### Vows Instead of Rules <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#vows-instead-of-rules}

*The Ten Commandments are presented as a ketubah, the vows a husband gives his bride.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes rephrase each commandment as a line in a wedding *ketubah*.[^6] A few examples:

- "I am the Lord your God" becomes "I am your husband."
- No other gods or idols becomes "Have no other lovers. Don't even have pictures of other lovers."
- Honor your father and mother becomes "Trust that my provision for you is enough."
- Do not commit adultery becomes "Protect your sexuality."
- Do not covet becomes "Be satisfied in this life with what we have together."

*Read Exodus 20:1–17 aloud.*

Listen to the commandments with those lines in mind. Which one sounds most different to you when you hear it as something a loving husband would say? What, if anything, gets lost in the translation?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The Sabbath commandment becomes, "Keep a date night and set it aside for me, your husband."[^7] The notes point out that the commandment includes the whole household: children, servants, animals, and foreigners.[^8] If the Sabbath is a weekly date night for the whole family of God, what would it look like for you to keep it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes describe the Torah as wedding gifts meant to enable the relationship, not burden it.[^9] Our traditions have taught us to hear "the Law" in different ways: some of us learned to honor it as a pattern to follow closely, others learned to see it mainly as something Jesus set us free from. What did your church teach you to feel when you heard the word "law"? How would your week look different if you received one of these commands as a gift?

</div>

### Unfaithful at the Altar <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#unfaithful-at-the-altar}

*While the vows are being given on the mountain, the bride turns to a golden calf below.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** In Exodus 32, Israel is unfaithful to the bridegroom during the presentation of the *ketubah*. The notes give Moses' reaction as, "You've DESTROYED this covenant... this cannot be what this relationship is built on."[^10] Moses breaking the tablets is described not as a temper tantrum but as a declaration that the covenant is broken.[^11]

*Read Exodus 32:15–20 aloud.*

Notice what Moses does with the tablets and with the calf. Marty links the drink Moses gives the people to the "law of jealousy" in Numbers 5, a test for a wife accused of unfaithfulness.[^12] What does the wedding frame help you see in this scene that a courtroom frame might miss?

*Read Jeremiah 2:2 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes read Jeremiah 2:2 as God seeing Israel's time in the desert as a honeymoon year.[^13] Given everything that happened in those years, including the calf, what does it say about God that this is how he remembers them?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** After the calf, the notes say God "immediately moves past the transgression and starts over by expressing unbelievable love, compassion, and forgiveness for his people," and makes new tablets.[^14] This is a hard and tender place to sit. When you have been unfaithful to God, what have you expected him to do next? What would it mean for you this week to believe he is already making new tablets?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Sinai is a wedding. God has carried his bride out of Egypt, consecrated her, gathered her under the cloud, and spoken vows that define a life together. In the middle of the ceremony she turns to a golden calf, and the covenant lies shattered at the foot of the mountain. Yet the groom does not walk away; he writes the vows again. The same groom later lifts a cup and promises not to drink it again until he drinks it with us in his Father's house.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: which line of the *ketubah* do you most need to hear this week?

</div>
