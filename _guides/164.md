### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Paul sent the timid Timothy, an outsider he called "my true son in the faith," to Ephesus and the tough Titus to Crete, an example of the Kingdom's "left-handed power."[^1]

**This week:** In a short personal letter, Paul asks a slave owner named Philemon to receive Onesimus back as a brother, and he does it with humor, public pressure and confidence in Philemon's character rather than a command.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode sets Philemon against the realities of the Roman world: how hard citizenship was to obtain, how brutal slavery was, and the limited options a slave had. The notes describe Onesimus not as a simple runaway but as someone who used the patronage system, traveling to find Paul and seek the protection of a Roman citizen. They also suggest that his name, which means "useful," may point to a past in the slave trade that grew out of child exposure (*sublatus*).[^2]

Against that background, the episode points out how much humor and gentle pressure fill the letter, and it names the letter's deeper logic: "faith works" (doing right because it is good) rather than "law works" (doing right from fear of consequences).[^3] Slavery is painful ground, and it is natural to ask what this letter does and does not say about it. The questions below try to stay close to what the notes claim while leaving room for honest discomfort.

### Opening <span class="guide-time">10 min</span> {#opening}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-opening" markdown="1">

**Opening Question:** Think of a time someone asked you to do something hard, not by ordering you but by appealing to the kind of person they believed you were. How did that land with you?

</div>

### The World Behind the Letter <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#the-world-behind-the-letter}

*Before the letter's humor makes sense, we need to feel the weight of what was at stake for Onesimus.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes say Roman slavery "was brutal, and runaway slaves faced execution," and that slaves had three options: flight, legal appeal to the courts, or patronage, seeking "protection from a Roman citizen willing to advocate for them." Onesimus chose patronage, traveling to find Paul, so the notes call this "not an escape attempt but a strategic legal maneuver."[^4] The notes also mention that church tradition suggests Paul's own parents were "manumitted slaves," which would explain his citizenship.[^5] What does it add to the letter to imagine Onesimus making that journey on purpose, and to imagine Paul as the child of freed slaves?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe *sublatus*, the Roman practice of abandoning unwanted babies, whom slave traders would collect, raise "in cage-like conditions," and sell. Because "Onesimus" means "useful," the notes suggest he may have been given "a name designed to make him attractive to buyers."[^6] *Read Philemon 10–16 aloud.* With that possibility in mind, what do you hear in the way Paul describes Onesimus, and in what he hopes Philemon will now call him?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Onesimus may have been named for his usefulness. Where do you see people (including yourself) valued mainly for what they produce or provide? What would it look like to treat one such person this week as a brother or sister rather than as useful?

</div>

### Paul's Humor and Public Pressure <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#pauls-humor-and-public-pressure}

*The notes find this short letter full of deliberate humor and sarcasm.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Philemon 1–9 and 17–25 aloud. If you can, have one person read it in the voice of a friend who is gently teasing.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes list tongue-in-cheek elements: "excessive flattery" about Philemon's character, "false humility" as Paul calls himself "an old man and prisoner" while making demands, wordplay on "useless" and "useful," calling Onesimus "my very heart," and addressing the letter to the whole church so that Philemon "must read it publicly and respond before witnesses."[^7] Which of these can you find in the text? Where did you hear Paul smiling as you read?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes say Paul's humor shows "his confidence in Philemon's character," and that his approach shows "confidence that people will do the right thing when reminded of their true identity in Christ."[^8] Look again at verses 8–9. Why do you think Paul chose persuasion and humor instead? What does that choice risk, and what does it make possible?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes see the letter's public nature as an example of how "community involvement can provide healthy pressure for doing what's right."[^9] When have you seen a community's involvement help someone do the right thing, and when can that kind of pressure go wrong?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes say Paul's approach models addressing difficult situations "with humor, confidence in the other person's character, and clear expectations while allowing for voluntary compliance."[^10] Think of a conflict or hard conversation in front of you. Which of those four would most change how you approach it?

</div>

### Faith Works and Law Works <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#faith-works-and-law-works}

*The episode names the motivation behind Paul's whole approach.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes explain the difference with speed limits. "Law works" means obeying "to avoid punishment," like following the limit to avoid a ticket. "Faith works" means obeying "because you believe it's the right thing that leads to flourishing," like following the limit "because they promote safety and order."[^11] *Read Philemon 14 and 21 aloud.* What words in these verses show which kind of obedience Paul is hoping for?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes call Paul's request for Philemon to receive Onesimus as a brother "a radical shift in social relationships that would have shocked Roman sensibilities." They say kingdom values transform oppressive systems "through changed relationships rather than merely political activism," while "not dismissing the need for systemic change."[^12] Some of us may wish this letter said more about slavery itself, and that discomfort is worth naming. How do you hold together changed relationships and changed systems when you think about injustice, both in Paul's world and in ours?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** Where in your life are you obeying God mainly from fear of consequences? What would it look like this week to do that same thing as "faith works," because you trust it leads to flourishing?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Onesimus came to Paul not simply as a runaway but as someone seeking a citizen's protection, perhaps carrying a name given to make him sell. Paul sent him back with a letter full of affection, teasing and public witness, asking Philemon to receive him no longer as a slave but as a brother. Paul could have commanded. Instead he trusted Philemon to do what was right because it was right. That is what the episode calls faith works: obedience that grows from knowing who we are and what leads to flourishing, not from fear.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: who is one person you will treat as a brother or sister this week rather than as "useful"?

</div>
