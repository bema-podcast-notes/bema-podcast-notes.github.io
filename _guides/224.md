### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Simon's empty nets became a miraculous catch and a call to gather "all kinds," with Eden as an *ezer kenegdo* who would not let him drift into self-reliance.[^1]

**This week:** At a wedding in Cana, a poor family is about to run out of wine, Mary turns to her son, and Jesus performs his first public sign.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode opens with Jesus at twelve in Jerusalem, then moves to the wedding at Cana in AD 26. The hosts explain why running out of wine would have been a crisis: first-century weddings were multi-day community celebrations, hospitality was a sacred duty, and the groom's family was stretching itself to match a wealthier bride's family. Mary asks Jesus for help, and the hosts reflect on the relationship between mother and son, which the show portrays with real influence but "without excessive veneration."[^2]

They also look at Jesus's first followers, a "rogue outfit" with no traditional Torah students among them; at Thomas, who needs everything explained; at Thaddeus, a stone cutter whose words about striking stone frame the miracle as a point of no return; and at Nicodemus, who visits John the Baptist in prison on his own time.[^3]

### A Wedding on the Edge of Shame <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#a-wedding-on-the-edge-of-shame}

*The miracle makes sense only when we feel what the family stood to lose.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read John 2:1–11 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes describe the cultural weight behind this scene: weddings were multi-day community events, Dinah (the groom's mother) welcomes Jesus's extra guests without hesitating despite limited resources, the groom's family has gone into debt to match the bride's family, and running out of wine "would bring lasting dishonor to the host family."[^4] As you listen to the passage, what details tell you what is at stake? What does John leave unsaid that the episode fills in?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The bride's father, Abner, is critical of the groom's family at first but later admits, "I was wrong," which the notes see as "the power of grace and humility to overcome class tensions." The chuppah (wedding canopy) starts out crooked and becomes beautiful once it is covered with flowers.[^5] Where have you seen humility or a single honest admission change the feel of a gathering or a family?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes say Jesus "cares about the shame that would fall on Asher's family, demonstrating that the Kingdom of God addresses real human concerns, not just spiritual abstractions." They add that the wine is not merely adequate but the best the banquet master has tasted: "God's provision exceeds mere necessity."[^6] Where do you tend to separate "spiritual" concerns from ordinary ones like money, embarrassment or a family's reputation? What would change if you brought the second kind to God as readily as the first?

</div>

### Mother and Son <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#mother-and-son}

*Mary's request sets everything in motion, though she cannot see where it leads.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** When Mary asks Jesus to help, she says "Please," looking almost directly at the camera. Later, when the wine runs out, she rushes to assure the hosts that "the wine is coming" before she has any evidence the miracle has happened.[^7] What do you notice about the way Mary asks and the way she acts afterward? What does it suggest about what she knows of her son?

*Read Luke 2:41–52 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode pairs this wedding with the scene of Jesus at twelve in Jerusalem, where Joseph does not quite know how to discipline him. Marty suggests that the exchange at Cana ("My time has not yet come" and Mary's reply) is "a sophisticated Torah dialogue," with both of them drawing on Genesis stories of Jacob, Leah, Rachel and Joseph.[^8] What do you notice about Mary and Jesus in the Luke passage? How does picturing Mary as someone who can trade Scripture with her son change your sense of her?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes point to a tragic irony: Mary's request sets in motion "the public ministry that will ultimately lead to the crucifixion," and at the end of the celebration she mouths "thank you" to Jesus.[^9] Have you ever asked God for something good that turned out to cost more, for you or for someone else, than you could have known? What did you learn from it? Share only what you want to.

</div>

### Water, Wine, and a Ragamuffin Band <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#water-wine-and-a-ragamuffin-band}

*The first sign happens among unlikely followers, and there is no going back from it.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Thaddeus, a stone cutter, explains his trade: "With stone, once you begin working with it, you can't stop. Once you've started, the process is in motion and there's no going back. You can't put the stone back together." The episode lays these words over images of the water becoming wine, marking the miracle as a "point of no return."[^10] Why do you think the show frames Jesus's first sign this way? What does it suggest about the decision Jesus makes at this wedding?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Thomas, a caterer at the wedding, keeps asking questions about the purification jars while Ramah simply starts working. Jesus tells him, "This is how it is sometimes. You can't always explain everything," and Ramah later adds, "Maybe for once in your life, don't think."[^11] Which of the two do you resemble more? What might each of them have to teach the other?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The hosts call Jesus's first followers a "rogue outfit": fishermen, a stone cutter, a former demoniac, and none of them traditional Torah students. Thaddeus met Jesus building ramps so people who could not walk well could reach the restroom facilities, and when Simon asks why Jesus didn't just heal them, the notes answer: "Not everyone will be healed, but everyone should have access and dignity."[^12] The notes say this challenges church systems "that emphasize formal training, proper credentials, and institutional pathways to ministry."[^13] As a diagnostic question about your own tradition, what does it do well in forming people for ministry, and where might its pathways leave a Thaddeus or a Mary Magdalene outside? What is one small "ramp" you could build for someone this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> At Cana, Jesus's first sign is not performed for a crowd of religious leaders but for a struggling family about to be shamed in front of their community. His mother asks, he answers, and the best wine of the night appears in jars meant for washing. The people around him are a ragamuffin band, more comfortable with nets and stone than with Torah schools, and one of them can see that something has started that cannot be undone. The notes close with an invitation to embrace a Kingdom that "makes room for doubters, questioners, and unlikely followers."[^14]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one ordinary, practical need around you that you could treat as Kingdom work this week?

</div>
