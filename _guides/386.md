### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Psalm 91 taught us that God's refuge does not carry us around suffering but through it, and that those who shelter under God's wings are called to become shelter for others.

**This week:** Two psalms attributed to David, read side by side, show the same man as both the hunted and the hunter, and a God who sees the whole of him at once.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Reed Dent guides Marty and Brent through Psalm 139, a psalm of comfort about being fully known by God that turns suddenly to hatred of the "bloodthirsty" wicked, and Psalm 51, David's confession after Nathan confronts him over Bathsheba and the death of Uriah. Reed noticed that the Hebrew word behind "bloodthirsty" in Psalm 139:19 is the same word as "bloodshed" in Psalm 51:14. The man who prayed against men of blood has become one.

From that link the hosts trace mirror-image phrases across the two psalms, warn against turning single verses into fixed doctrines, and question our habit of sorting people into heroes and villains. They lean on Solzhenitsyn's line that good and evil run through every human heart, while insisting this is not an excuse for harm. Over both psalms stands a God who sees all of a person and still restores those who come home. Some questions today touch hurt and anger, so share only what you choose to.

### Fully Known, Then a Jarring Turn <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#fully-known-then-a-jarring-turn}

*Psalm 139 moves from womb-like security to violent language and back again.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Psalm 139 aloud, all twenty-four verses.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes describe three movements: verses 1–18 feel "like I'm in the womb," safe and fully known; verses 19–22 turn to hatred (Robert Alter renders verse 22 "I hate them with complete hatred"); and verses 23–24 return to openness[^1]. Where exactly did you feel the turn as we read it? What words or images change, and what stays the same across all three movements?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The hosts contrast two ways of reading verses 19–22. A scholar asks whom David meant, which keeps the text at arm's length. A person praying it has to ask whom they have wished gone or defeated. Marty and Brent admitted milder versions of this, such as "I wish I would never hear from that person again."[^2] Which way of reading comes more naturally to you, and what does each one protect you from?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Marty says honesty about wanting an enemy defeated is part of the Psalms, but "That's not where the story ends," because the enemy also bears the image of God. The notes add that finding hatred hard to feel is a good sign, because it means we resist "othering," putting people in a category of humanity we tell ourselves we don't belong to[^3]. Where do you see othering happen most easily in your own world, at work, online, in politics or at church? What helps you resist it?

</div>

### You Are the Man <span class="guide-time">35 min</span> {#you-are-the-man}

*A shared Hebrew word shows that David's role has flipped, not just his mood.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read 2 Samuel 12:1–7 aloud, then read Psalm 51 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The Hebrew word *damim* ("blood" or "bloodshed") appears in Psalm 139:19, literally "men of blood," and in Psalm 51:14, "deliver me from the guilt of bloodshed." In Psalm 139 David is threatened by men of blood; in Psalm 51 he has become one. Reed imagines Uriah, abandoned on the battlefield, praying Psalm 139:19–22, and the man he would have meant is David[^4]. What do you notice when you hold those two verses together? What does Reed's thought experiment do to the way you hear Psalm 139?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The hosts found mirror-image phrases in almost every stanza, such as "My frame was not hidden from you" (139:15) beside "Hide your face from my sins" (51:9), and "Search me, God, and know my heart" (139:23) beside "Create in me a pure heart" (51:10)[^5]. With a partner, find one more pair the group has not named yet and report what the contrast shows. Marty suggests David may have pulled out an old favorite and written an "anti-psalm" in reply. Why might someone in their lowest moment write against the grain of a psalm they once loved?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The hosts warn against lifting single verses out and turning them into fixed doctrines, and they name Psalm 51:5 ("sinful from the time my mother conceived me") as an example. Read next to Psalm 139:13, each line comes from a different moment in David's life, and both are true because both come from real experience[^6]. Our traditions in this room have read Psalm 51:5 in different ways. Without settling that, how does reading it as a cry from a particular moment change what it says to you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Asked whether David is a victim or a perpetrator, a hero or a villain, the notes say the honest answer is "yes." Solzhenitsyn put it this way: "The line separating good and evil passes not through states, nor between classes, nor between political parties either, but right through every human heart." Marty adds that in a given moment one person can be plainly the offender, as David was, but over a whole life everyone has been both[^7]. Reed says that if you live long enough you will probably have a "Uriah," someone who could rightly say, "Look what he did to me."[^8] Share only if you wish. How does seeing that "I am my enemy, and my enemy is me" change the way you hold anger toward someone, without excusing real harm?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#closing}

> Psalm 139 and Psalm 51 come from opposite places in one man's life. In the first, David rests in being fully known and prays against the men of blood who hunt him. In the second, after Nathan's "You are the man," he confesses that he has become one. Together they take away the comfortable sorting that makes "complete hatred" easy, without pretending that harm does not matter. Over both stands a God who sees the whole of each person at once, from the womb to our worst choices, and who answers not with hatred but by restoring the joy of salvation to those who come home. "Where sin increased, grace increased all the more."[^9]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** Going around the room in one sentence each, with no comments until everyone has spoken: which of the two psalms sounds more like your season right now, and why?

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 387 — Psalms — More Distant Reflections](/guides/387)** (51:38): Brent and Reed set Psalm 32 beside Psalm 51 and suggest both come from the same event. Psalm 51 is raw and urgent, written in the moment; Psalm 32 sounds like an older David looking back calmly on the same failure. The hosts trace hiding, bones, silence and confession across the psalms, and talk about the cost of hiding, the work of restoration, and how God redeems brokenness for the good of others[^10].

**What to bring next week:** Try the episode's suggested practice: read Psalm 139 and Psalm 51 side by side on at least three days this week and notice which parts fit your current season. Also read Psalm 32 once before we meet.
