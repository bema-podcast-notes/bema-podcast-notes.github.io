### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Matthew's genealogy showed us a Jewish Gospel for a Jewish audience, announcing a Kingdom with no *mamzers*, no outsiders, and warning those who think they are already in.

**This week:** We hear a second voice. Mark, a Jew writing to Romans, tells the story of Jesus at Roman speed and turns the crucifixion into a coronation.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode argues that Mark (John Mark) wrote a uniquely Roman Gospel. Its breathless pace, story after story linked by "immediately," suits an audience that wanted answers and action rather than a treasure hunt, and its stories play to the four pillars of Hellenism: Jesus the teacher, the healer, the one who amazes the crowds, and the champion who always wins.[^1] The heart of the episode is Mark's crucifixion narrative, which the notes show following the nine steps of a Roman imperial coronation, drawing especially on accounts of Nero's.[^2]

Mark is not trying to prove Jesus is king by giving him a coronation like Caesar's. He is redefining power itself: where Caesar's power comes through conquest and the sword, Jesus' comes through sacrifice and the cross.[^3] The episode closes with Mark's abrupt ending at 16:8, where the women flee the tomb "trembling and bewildered," and asks what it would cost a Roman, and what it costs us, to choose this King.[^4]

### A Gospel at Roman Speed <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#a-gospel-at-roman-speed}

*Mark meets his audience where they are before he challenges them.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The original notes put the contrast this way: "Romans don't want a treasure hunt like the Jews do. Romans want answers now and they need to be entertained."[^5] Mark's Gospel moves quickly and plays to the four pillars of Hellenism: education (Jesus the teacher), healthcare (Jesus the healer), entertainment (the crowds amazed), and athletics or competition (Jesus the winner).[^6] Where do you recognize that "Roman" preference for speed, answers and entertainment in yourself, or in the way churches often teach?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes explain that the group spends Session 3 in Matthew rather than Mark because "Mark plays to our fancy as Americans and Matthew has a better message for us."[^7] What might it say about us if the Gospel we find easiest to read is the one written for Rome? How could that awareness change the way you approach Scripture this month?

</div>

### The Crucifixion as Coronation <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#the-crucifixion-as-coronation}

*Read slowly, Mark's account of Jesus' execution follows the script of an emperor's crowning.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Mark 15:16–33 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes list the nine steps of a Roman coronation:[^8]

1. The Praetorian Guard gathers.
2. Royal robes, a wreath crown and a scepter are placed on the emperor.
3. The new Caesar is led in procession through the streets.
4. A sacrificial animal follows, and Caesar carries the instrument of its death.
5. The procession arrives at Capitoline Hill, "Head Hill," where Caesar is offered wine mixed with myrrh and refuses it.
6. The sacrifice is killed, and Caesar pronounces life or death over prisoners.
7. The emperor ascends the temple steps with the high priest on his right and the army's commander on his left.
8. Caesar is proclaimed "Lord and God."
9. The crowds wait for a sign from the heavens.

Go back through the passage you just read. Where do you see each step? Which parallel seems strongest to you, and which seems most surprising?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes highlight step five. Golgotha means "place of the head," matching Capitoline Hill, and wine mixed with myrrh was among the most expensive wines in the ancient world, "wine for kings, not cheap vinegar." In Mark it is offered before the crucifixion, while other Gospels record cheap wine offered during it. The notes add that both can be historically true, but Mark's emphasis serves his purpose.[^9] What does Mark gain by including the royal wine that Jesus refuses?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes insist that "Mark is not trying to 'prove' Jesus is king by showing he had a coronation like Caesar." Instead, he shows that Jesus' coronation "redefines power itself," power through sacrifice and servanthood rather than victory and the sword.[^10] If the cross is Jesus' coronation, what does that say about what real power looks like? Where does that collide with what you have been taught, at work, in politics or even in church, about strength and winning?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> *Read Romans 16:13 aloud.* Mark names Simon of Cyrene as "the father of Alexander and Rufus," and Paul greets a Rufus in his letter to the Romans. The notes imagine the moment Mark's Gospel was first read aloud in Rome: "Our father carried the cross of Jesus."[^11] Who in your life carried something for Jesus in a way that shaped you? How would you want your own part in the story to be remembered?

</div>

### Trembling and Bewildered <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#trembling-and-bewildered}

*Mark may end his Gospel with a question rather than an answer.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Mark 16:1–8 aloud, and stop at verse 8.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes explain that the earliest manuscripts end at verse 8, and that the later endings "reflect later Christian discomfort with such an abrupt, unsettling ending."[^12] Sit for a moment with the reading ending where it did. What is it like to have the story stop there? What do you notice about the women's response and about the message they were given?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Marty says, "Any Roman who reads Mark's gospel and accepts it is going to feel just like those women. If they affirm the truth that Jesus is a better king, their Roman life as they know it is over. They have much to fear."[^13] He goes on: "They had to deal with 'Pax Romana.' We have to deal with 'Pax Americana' in our world."[^14] Without turning this into a debate about policy, where does saying "Jesus is Lord" cost you something, or where do you suspect it should? What other loyalties compete for that title in your life?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes observe that many churches measure success by Empire's standards (bigger buildings and budgets, competition for members, numbers over transformation) and suggest that crucifixion-shaped power "might look like decrease rather than increase."[^15] What measures of success does your church, or your own discipleship, actually use? Which of them look more like Caesar's coronation, and which look more like Jesus'?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Mark gave Rome the Gospel it would enjoy: fast, vivid, full of teaching, healing, amazement and victory. Then he led his readers to a cross and showed them a coronation they would never have expected, a King crowned with thorns, flanked by criminals, mocked with titles that were true. And then he stopped, leaving the women trembling at the tomb and handing the next chapter to his readers. The question he asked Rome is still in front of us: are we willing to believe that real power looks like Jesus on the cross?
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: if Mark's ending is an unwritten chapter, what is the next line you want to write?

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 86 — Luke — Ordered](/guides/86)** (22:12): The third Gospel voice. Marty challenges the common view of Luke as a Gentile writing a more chronological account for Gentiles, proposing instead that Luke was a convert to Judaism. He introduces M. D. Goulder's theory that Luke arranged his Gospel to follow the weekly Torah and Prophets readings of the synagogue, an "ordered" rather than "orderly" account.

**What to bring next week:** Read Luke 1:1–4 slowly. Write down what Luke says others have done, where the accounts came from, and what he says he is doing. If your church follows a lectionary or a yearly reading plan, bring an example.
