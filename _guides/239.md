### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We closed the Jewish roots series by hearing that the church was meant to have a Jewish character, not just Jewish roots, and that Gentile believers are branches grafted onto a tree they did not plant.[^1]

**This week:** Reed Dent joins Brent (Marty is away) to pick up the conversation about deconstruction and reconstruction from Episode 231, asking what faith actually is.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Reed and Brent argue that biblical faith is trust in God expressed through action, not simply agreement with doctrinal statements. Scripture says "Abraham believed God," and then Abraham upended his whole life and walked; that walking was his faith.[^3] Reed returns to his image of worldviews and doctrines as scaffolding around the building of faith, and he warns that deconstruction can swap one set of ideas for another without ever growing in trust.[^4]

The episode also describes an arc from innocence through experience to a renewed innocence, where certainty gives way to assurance, and it finds that arc in Scripture itself: Proverbs and Ecclesiastes held together.[^5] The second half is pastoral: why some people respond to questions with anger, why families and churches make questions feel dangerous, and why we should never say "I disagree" until we can say "I understand."[^6] Sharing personal faith stories this week is welcome and always optional.

**Opening Question:** Think of a time you trusted someone enough to act before you had all the answers: a move, a job, a relationship, a first step of some kind. What made it possible to take that step?

### Abraham Believed God <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#abraham-believed-god}

*Reed's starting point is that Abraham's faith was a life lived in response to a promise, not a list of correct beliefs.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 12:1–4 aloud, then Genesis 15:1–6.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Reed notes that the text says "Abraham believed God" and not "believed in God" or "believed X about God," and that Abraham had no catechism and "certainly didn't understand everything about where God was leading."[^3] Looking at these two passages, what does Abraham actually know, what does he not know, and what does he do?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** In the notes, Reed says the upheaving of Abraham's life, "the walking," was his faith, and he warns that when faith becomes mainly intellectual agreement with propositions, "it can become a form of idolatry regardless of whether one is conservative or progressive."[^3] What would it mean to measure faith by walking rather than by agreement? What might we gain, and what might we worry about losing?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Reed calls worldviews, doctrines, creeds, dogma and hermeneutics "scaffolding around the building of faith itself": necessary and not bad, but "not the thing itself." The danger is changing which scaffolding we prefer without touching the assumption "that faith is fundamentally about ideas we agree with."[^4] Every tradition in this room has given its people some scaffolding. What scaffolding has served you well, and how can you tell when scaffolding has started to stand in for the building?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes say Abraham's faith "included mistakes and misunderstandings," yet he "trusted God enough to walk the path even when things went wrong."[^7] Where in your life right now does faith look less like knowing and more like walking?

</div>

### Innocence, Experience, and Renewed Innocence <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#innocence-experience-and-renewed-innocence}

*Reed describes a three-stage arc of faith and finds it in the conversation between Proverbs and Ecclesiastes.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Proverbs 3:5–6 aloud, then Ecclesiastes 7:13 and Ecclesiastes 9:11.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes set Proverbs' "do this and that will happen" beside Ecclesiastes' "the race doesn't always go to the swift" and "who can make straight what God has made crooked?" and conclude that "wisdom is holding both perspectives together."[^5] What does each voice notice about life that the other one does not? What happens to a person who hears only one of them?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Reed's arc moves from innocence (certainty, with a risk of anti-intellectualism), through experience (questioning and doubt, with a risk of cynicism), to renewed innocence, where "certainty gives way to assurance," "doubts become reverence for mystery," and "cynicism becomes conviction and honesty."[^5] Without needing to share more than you want, which description in that arc feels most familiar, either from your own life or from someone you love?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> As a campus minister, Reed met students who had memorized every verse and "were the most influential kids in their youth groups," yet whose faith crumbled when they met questions they had never faced. Quoting Buechner, he describes faith as "more as a verb than a noun, more as a process than a possession."[^8] What would it look like for a church or family to form young people for questions rather than only for answers?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Reed tells of his brother nervously asking him late one night whether a person could read the creation days as something other than literal 24-hour days and still be a Christian, and of the relief when Reed said yes. The notes say communities can make people "afraid to voice doubts even to family members, not knowing if it's 'okay to even ask.'"[^9] What could you do so that the people closest to you know they can bring you their questions?

</div>

### Tell Me What You Mean <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#tell-me-what-you-mean}

*The last part of the episode is about how we talk with each other when we see things differently.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** Reed contrasts Babel, where power consolidates around one approved vocabulary, with Acts, where everyone hears the gospel in their own language, so that "the Gospel has no native language." His principle: "Don't ever say 'I disagree' until you can say 'I understand'," and ask "tell me what you mean by that" in a spirit of charity.[^10] Think of a word or phrase that often divides Christians. How might asking "tell me what you mean by that" change the conversation?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe anger in response to theological questions as "often a defensive emotion protecting something vulnerable," and Brent mentions Jon Steingard, whose income, band and reputation all gave him reasons not to engage his doubts.[^11] What kinds of things might someone be protecting when a question makes them angry, and how does seeing that change your response?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q11:** The episode keeps returning to Galatians 5:6: "The only thing that counts is faith expressing itself through love."[^12] Who is one person you will listen to this week with "tell me what you mean by that," and what would faith expressing itself through love look like in that conversation?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> This week Reed and Brent asked us to see faith the way Abraham lived it: not as agreeing with every line of a catechism, but as trusting God enough to walk, mistakes and all. Scaffolding matters, but it is not the building. Scripture itself holds Proverbs and Ecclesiastes together, and many of us travel from innocence through experience toward a renewed innocence marked by assurance rather than certainty. Along the way, we can make room for each other's questions by saying "tell me what you mean" before we say "I disagree."
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one step of walking (not just believing) that you sense God asking of you right now?

</div>
