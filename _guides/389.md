### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Psalm 19 showed the silent heavens and the shining Torah as one song, and left us with two questions: are you listening, and will you join in the proclamation?

**This week:** A second walk through Psalm 23 slows down at its earthy Hebrew images and finds God's remedy for sin, fear and scarcity in rest, abundance and nearness.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This is the series' second episode on Psalm 23. Brent calls it a "second course" in the feast, in the spirit of the rabbinic idea that every text has "seventy faces," many valid ways to read it. Elle Grover-Fricks offers her own translation, deliberately less polished, to bring out the concrete pictures under the familiar English. The notes also recall that before the cross became the main Christian symbol, the Good Shepherd carrying a sheep on his shoulders was the main picture of Jesus.

The group stops at word after word: "I will not lack" instead of "I shall not want," a verb for lying down that echoes sin crouching at Cain's door, the "calf tracks of justice," a ravine where the "shadow of death" may hide the word for "image," God "standing with" us like family, a table set in full view of our enemies, and goodness and compassion that chase us down. The common thread is that God answers sin, fear and scarcity with rest and closeness, not a lonely struggle. Some questions touch fear and need; share only what you choose.

### Not Lacking, Lying Down <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#not-lacking-lying-down}

*The opening lines are about having enough and learning to rest.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Psalm 23:1–3 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes contrast "I shall not want," which can suggest that desire itself is bad, with "I will not lack," which means I will have enough because the shepherd provides. Josh adds that in modern English "I shall not want" can sound like luxury, while "I will not lack" sounds like a life of *enough*[^2]. Listen to verse 1 with each phrase in turn. What changes in the picture of the shepherd and the sheep?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Elle observes that some church cultures treat every desire as evidence of a deceitful heart, yet Scripture uses the language of desire even for God (1 Timothy 2:4). The notes add a nuance: not every desire is good, but desire is not evil in itself. They also point out that in a communal society, admitting what you need lets others care for you[^3]. What did your church upbringing teach you about desire and need? How easy is it for you to say out loud what you need?

*Read Genesis 4:6–7 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** In verse 2 the notes translate "He makes me crouch down." The verb, *ravats*, is the same one used of sin "crouching at the door" before Cain. The posture is a dog lying on its front paws, relaxed but watching. Elle suggests that resting with God, rather than trying harder on our own, may be how sin is defeated. Josh adds the Jewish idea of the *yetzer hara*, the inclination toward evil, and suggests that when God leads, "rest can become as natural as sin."[^4] Where in your life do you tend to fight alone by trying harder? What might it look like to let the shepherd make you lie down there instead?

</div>

### Through the Ravine <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#through-the-ravine}

*The paths of justice run straight through a dark place, and the shepherd walks close.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Psalm 23:3–4 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The word for "paths," *ma'gal*, means a worn animal track, the kind used to lead young livestock to pasture and back. Josh notes that the root suggests roundness, a circuit that goes out and comes home. Where Western culture hears "going in circles" as stagnation, biblical life is built on cycles: Sabbath, festivals, letting the land rest, Jubilee. In Josh's words: "We go out, the good shepherd feeds us, he brings us back in. That's what justice is like."[^5] Elle's summary of the word *tsedeq* is "Righteousness is just justice embodied."[^6] What steady rhythms in your week or your church feel like a track that brings you home?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes render the pair in verse 4 as "your staff and your supporting branch." The first word, *shevet*, is the same word as "tribe," a staff that marked a leader's authority, like Moses' staff or Aaron's budding staff in Numbers 17. The second, *mish'enet*, comes from the verb "to lean," something you can put your weight on. Elle pushes back on readings where God's rod is mainly for hitting, since the psalm calls these tools comforting[^7]. The word "with" here is also unusual, *immad*, the word Laban uses when asking whether Jacob is "with" him in building a household[^8]. How does picturing God as family who stands with you and a branch you can lean on compare with the picture of God you grew up with?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes point out the future tense: "I *will* walk," not "though I may walk." If the calf tracks run through the gorge, the dark ravine is part of the route to the meadows, not a wrong turn[^9]. Then a wordplay: squeezing the letters of *tsalmavet* ("shadow of death") together suggests *tselem*, "image," the word used for the image of God and for idols. Many of our fears are imagined threats that harm us mostly through the fear itself, and Josh notes that sometimes we only grow afraid once an image of the danger is "painted" for us[^10]. Share only what you are comfortable sharing. What images of danger have been painted for you, and which of your fears might be shadows rather than the thing itself?

</div>

### A Table Among Enemies <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#a-table-among-enemies}

*God sets the feast before the threats are gone.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Psalm 23:5–6 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Elle reads verse 5 as "a table set parallel to those who oppress me." God does not make us solve every problem first, settling every doubt or finishing a checklist of repentance; he invites us to the table while the enemies are still there. Josh notes that what stands between the sheep and the wolves is not a wall but a table, and compares this to Jesus putting the kingdom first rather than playing "sin whack-a-mole."[^11] The notes also share Reed's picture of a banquet as a "forward operating base of the kingdom," with communion as a foretaste[^12]. What do you notice about where the table is set? What would change if your church's meals and communion felt like that kind of outpost?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Brent pictures two armies setting up their tables back to back along the front line, and Elle wonders whether the wolves have places set too, since Jesus came for our oppressors as well[^13]. How does that possibility sit with you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** A footnote Brent reads says this is the only place in the Hebrew Bible where goodness or compassion is the subject of the verb "chase" (*radaf*). Elle's challenge: some people dodge goodness and treat rest as something "for other people."[^14] Are you more often the one chasing, or the one dodging being caught? What would it look like this week to stop running and let goodness catch you?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> This second walk through Psalm 23 turns a well-worn passage into something new by staying with its earthy images. The psalm is about *enough* rather than having no desires. The posture sin takes outside Cain's door becomes the posture of rest beside our shepherd. The paths of justice are worn calf tracks that take us out and bring us home, and they run straight through a dark ravine full of shadows, where the shepherd walks close enough to lean on. God stands with us as family, sets a feast in full view of our enemies without waiting for us to fix everything first, and sends goodness and compassion to chase us down for many long days[^15].
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** Going around the room in one sentence each, with no comments until everyone has spoken: which picture from Psalm 23 do you most need to carry into this week?

</div>
