### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Elle Grover Fricks gave us three arenas for wrestling (the text, our community and tradition, and God himself) and a blessing to grip all three and not let go.[^1]

**This week:** The Four Pillars series closes with Reed Dent's reflection on Jacob at the Jabbok and the difference between wrestling with ideas and wrestling with our own souls.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Reed does not walk through Genesis 32 line by line. Instead he reads it the way Lectio Divina works, noticing phrases that stand out and asking what God brings to mind through them. His main move is to separate "brain wrestling" (working through doctrines, interpretations and ideas about God) from "soul wrestling" (a personal struggle with God's presence, with our own identity and with how the way of Jesus asks us to treat other people). Both matter, but people often try to settle a soul problem with brain work.

Moving through the passage, Reed reflects on Jacob being left alone, on struggling "with God and with humans," on the question "What is your name?" and on what it means to overcome when you walk away limping. The match, the hosts suggest, prepares Jacob to meet his estranged brother Esau with a softened heart. The episode's tagline sums it up: "You came in looking for better answers but ended up leaving a better person."

### Brain Wrestling and Soul Wrestling <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#brain-wrestling-and-soul-wrestling}

*Reed names two levels of wrestling and warns that it is easy to live on only one.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Reed's framework sorts wrestling into two levels, each played out with humans, with God and with ourselves.[^3]
- **Brain wrestling with humans:** re-examining interpretations, doctrines, traditions, sermons and denominational positions.
- **Soul wrestling with humans:** struggling with Jesus's commands about other people: forgive, love your enemies, "Who is my neighbor?"
- **Brain wrestling with God:** asking who God is and why God acts or doesn't act.
- **Soul wrestling with God:** asking *where* God is; longing for presence when God feels absent.
- **Brain wrestling with self:** thinking about your beliefs.
- **Soul wrestling with self:** asking "Who am I?" and staying open to change.

Which of these describe the kind of wrestling you have done most, and which feel less familiar?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty compares Reed's approach to Lectio Divina, which asks what God is saying to *me* through these words today. Historical-critical reading asks what the text meant to its first audience. The notes say BEMA usually works in the first mode, this episode deliberately uses the second, and "both are valid ways to engage scripture."[^4] What does each way of reading offer that the other might miss?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes put one warning bluntly: "You can use God to avoid God." On BEMA's Israel and Turkey trips, Reed noticed people asking detailed contextual questions to avoid harder topics like their marriage or their kids. Marty admits he often tries to satisfy a soul need with brain answers.[^5] If study ever becomes a way of keeping God at arm's length for you, what does that look like? What might you be avoiding?

</div>

### A Night at the Jabbok <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#a-night-at-the-jabbok}

*Reed reads Jacob's story phrase by phrase, listening for what God might be stirring up.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 32:22–30 aloud, slowly, twice. Leave a short silence between the readings.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Without explaining or analyzing yet, which word or phrase caught your attention in this reading? After everyone who wishes to has shared, what do you notice about the phrases the group chose?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** "Jacob was left alone." Reed reads this as a picture of going "to the end of yourself," and notes that Jesus, Elijah and Moses were led into solitary places because some work can only happen there. Marty pushes back: nobody stripped Jacob; he sent everyone away himself. Sometimes we push ourselves into lonely deserts, and God says, "You've done this to yourself. I'll meet you here." Brent wonders whether the wrestling started right away or only after hours alone.[^6] How do these readings of Jacob's solitude add to each other?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The man tells Jacob he has struggled "with God and with humans." Reed found it freeing to realize that much of his own theological struggle was with *people's representations* of God, not with God. When hard questions feel like falling down a well, he offers the image of a cenote: a sinkhole that looks like a dangerous pit, with beautiful pools underneath. The notes add that wrestling doesn't always overturn tradition: "Sometimes the truth is hard because it isn't what you were taught. Sometimes it's hard because it *is* what you were taught."[^7] Where have you found that a struggle you thought was with God was really with what people had told you about God?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Jacob asks for a blessing and gets a question back: "What is your name?" His new name, Israel, means "one who struggles with God." Reed reads this as: maybe what I'm missing isn't knowledge of God but understanding of myself. He also observes that the biblical writers ask *where* God is, not *whether* God exists; Job and the Psalmists ask why God is hiding his face.[^8] Which question sits closer to your life right now: "Where is God?" or "Who am I?" Answer only as much as you want to; you are welcome to simply hold the question.

</div>

### Limping Toward Esau <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#limping-toward-esau}

*The match ends with a limp, a new name and a reconciliation Jacob never expected.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Marty says overcoming isn't coming out washed clean and victorious; it can mean coming out "even more of a mess, limping." At Peniel Jacob says, "I saw God face to face, and yet my life was spared." Brent notices that Jacob begins the night gripping hard and ends it saying, in effect, "God spared me. I didn't have anything to do with it." The hosts warn that "I won't let you go unless you bless me" can be bent into prosperity thinking, and say the wrestling should center on getting *God*.[^9] How does this change what you think of as "blessing" or "winning" in your own struggles?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Jacob is about to meet Esau. Marty says the encounter left him "soft enough, open enough" to receive Esau's forgiveness. Reed also calls for a "tag team": friends who honor your discomfort without bulldozing it with quick answers. The notes suggest taking time alone with God before a hard conversation so your own heart can be softened first.[^10] Without naming anyone, where might you need that kind of softening, or whose questions could you sit with this week without fixing them? What would one step look like?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Jacob goes out alone, grapples all night without knowing his opponent, asks for a blessing and gets a question about himself instead. He leaves with a new name and a limp, and only afterward realizes he has seen God face to face, much like the disciples on the road to Emmaus who recognized Jesus only in hindsight. Reed's point for those of us who love studying the text is that study is good, but it can also keep God at a distance. Soul wrestling means staying in the ring, saying "I don't know" when that is true, and insisting on God's presence rather than on mastery. The goal is to become someone who can cross the river and embrace an estranged brother.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one thing the four pillars (text, community, discipleship, wrestling) have stirred in you that you want to carry forward?

</div>
