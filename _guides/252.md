### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** John Roberts showed us grief as a companion to be faced, the wisdom of the house of mourning, and a Jesus who weeps with those who mourn.[^1]

**This week:** We begin the Gospel of John with its prologue, and with the word John chose to introduce Jesus: *Logos*.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Marty notes that John seems to be many people's favorite book and is often recommended to first-time Bible readers, though he says it is not an area where he feels like an expert. He recommends resources, including Yohanna Katanacho's *Reading the Gospel of John Through Palestinian Eyes* and the work of Dr. Gary Burge.[^3] Then he reads John 1, pointing to John's light motif (which he calls "very Essene-like") and to the way John plays with the word *Logos* ("speech, reason") to speak to, and subvert, both the Greek and the Jewish worldviews at the same time.[^4]

Marty also touches on questions of authorship and a theory, drawn from Alexander Shaia, that the four Gospels were packaged to guide a new believer through a journey of change, conflict, illumination and service.[^5] The notes for this episode are brief outlines, so this guide is shorter and stays close to what they record.

### One Word, Two Worlds <span class="guide-time">35 min</span> {#one-word-two-worlds}

*John opens his Gospel with a word that people in very different worlds would each recognize.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read John 1:1–18 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Read slowly and listen for words and images that repeat. What do you notice? Which words sound like they come from Genesis, and which ones sound like they would land with someone who had never read the Hebrew Scriptures?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes quote two Greek thinkers on *Logos*. A saying attributed to Heraclitus speaks of listening "to the Logos" and agreeing that "all things are one," and Aristotle describes the *Logos* as what lets humans "perceive good and bad and right and wrong."[^6] Marty sums up John's move this way: "Oh I'll tell you all about Logos, but this Logos was a person. Not just an abstract, rational construct but a person named Jesus."[^7] What would it have meant to a Greek listener to hear that the *Logos* was a person who became flesh?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes observe that within 18 verses John moves from *Logos* to "the Jewish context of messiah, Torah, the Law of Moses and observance."[^8] Look again at verses 14–18. Where do you hear that Jewish world, and how does it change what "Word" means?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Marty describes John speaking to and subverting two worldviews "using the same words, in the same sentence, in the same paragraph."[^4] When you explain your faith to people with very different assumptions, what words or ideas do you find yourself reaching for? What would it look like this week to speak about Jesus in a way that meets someone in their own world?

</div>

### Light, Flesh and a New Kind of Family <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#light-flesh-and-a-new-kind-of-family}

*Marty draws out the light motif and John's picture of a family not defined by bloodline.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read John 18:15–16 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Marty notes that when Jesus is arrested, it is John who gets Peter into the courtyard, which likely means John had priestly connections. He links this to John's light motif and the Essene language of being "sons of light," light versus darkness.[^4] What do you notice in these verses about this disciple's access? How might a background like that shape the way a Gospel is written?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** On John 1:13, Marty says Jesus is inviting people into something "not just connected to bloodline," not tied to human descent or a husband's will, and he connects this to the sons of light and to Jesus's later conversation with Nicodemus.[^9] He also notes that *sarx* ("flesh") in "the word became flesh" does not carry the overtones of sinfulness it has in Paul's letters.[^10] What does it mean to you that belonging to God's family is not decided by birth? And what does it suggest that the *Logos* took on ordinary human flesh?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty shares Alexander Shaia's theory that the Gospels were packaged to guide new believers: Matthew is about "change," Mark about "conflict," John about "illumination" (Shaia places John third), and Luke about "service." Marty says the idea has been rattling around in his head for five or six years.[^5] Whether or not the theory persuades you, which of those four stages best describes where you are right now?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** John's prologue says the light shines in the darkness. Marty notes that light is one of John's largest motifs.[^4] Where in your life right now do you most need illumination, a place where you are waiting to "see"? What is one step you could take this week to bring that place into the light?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> This week we stepped into the Gospel of John. John opens with a word every Greek thinker prized and every Jewish listener would hear echoing Genesis: in the beginning was the *Logos*. Then he makes it personal: this Word was not an abstract principle but a person, who took on ordinary human flesh and lived among us. John's light shines into darkness, and his family is not built on bloodline but on receiving the one who came. As Marty put it, John uses the same words, in the same sentence, to speak to two worlds at once, and he invites us into the light.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one word or image from John 1 you want to carry with you this week?

</div>
