### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Hosea's marriage became prophetic theater for Israel's unfaithfulness, and we heard God say "I desire mercy, not sacrifice" before promising to make the Valley of Achor a door of hope[^1].

**This week:** We cross to the southern kingdom of Judah, where Micah pictures God as a judge who actively sets things right, and where, for once, the prophet's warning is heeded[^2].

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Micah prophesied to Judah during the reigns of Jotham, Ahaz and Hezekiah, in a season of prosperity and military strength that made his warnings seem out of place[^3]. His central image is God as *shofet* (judge), but in the Hebrew sense of one who actively makes things right, like the leaders in the book of Judges, rather than one who sits behind a bench pronouncing verdicts[^4]. Micah addresses both Story A (idolatry and immorality) and Story B (injustice and empire-building), with the weight falling on Story B[^5].

Two passages anchor the episode. The Bethlehem prophecy of Micah 5, familiar from Christmas, is read first in its original setting as a reminder of David's humble beginnings and of a God who works through the small[^6]. Then Micah 6:6–8 builds through escalating offerings to its famous answer: act justly, love mercy and walk humbly with God[^7]. The episode also notes that Hezekiah heeded Micah's message, and Jerusalem was delivered without military might[^8].

**Opening Question:** Last week's preparation asked which of Micah's three requirements (justice, mercy, humility) feels most natural to you and which feels hardest. Whether or not you read ahead, which of those three words do you most often see lived out well by someone you admire, and how?

### The God Who Sets Things Right <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#the-god-who-sets-things-right}

*Micah's picture of God as judge is active, not passive.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes explain that *shofet* is an action word. The book of Judges (*Shofetim*) shows figures like Samson, who did not sit in courtrooms with gavels but fought to set things right[^4]. When you hear "God is judge," what picture comes to mind first, and how does the Hebrew picture of a judge as one who makes things straight change it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode points to Beit Shemesh as a site with archaeological evidence of Hezekiah's reforms. When Sennacherib of Assyria besieged Jerusalem, Hezekiah repented, and God delivered the city not by military might but by a plague on the Assyrians, an example the notes call "Shalom vs. Empire"[^8]. What does it suggest to you that at least one prophetic call to repentance was actually heard and made a difference?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes apply Story A and Story B to church life today. A Story A emphasis focuses on personal morality, correct doctrine and individual salvation; a Story B emphasis attends to systemic injustice, power and God's concern for the poor, the immigrant and the marginalized. The notes say both contain truth, but many Western Christians have been trained almost entirely in one[^9]. Thinking of the churches that formed you, which emphasis did you hear most, what good did it protect, and what might the other emphasis add to your own walk this month?

</div>

### Out of You, Bethlehem <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#out-of-you-bethlehem}

*A Christmas text heard first in Micah's own day.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Micah 5:2–5 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes suggest that for Micah's hearers, "Out of you, Bethlehem" immediately evoked King David, the youngest son of Jesse, and that "seven shepherds, even eight commanders" may recall David's seven brothers with David as the eighth[^6]. What words in the passage emphasize smallness or humble beginnings? What do you notice about the setting of threat around it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes draw out the message for Micah's time: when enemies surround you, do not rely on imperial military might, because God's kingdom "operates backwards," with strength from weakness and greatness from smallness[^6]. Why would that message have been hard to hear for a prosperous, militarily strong Judah?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Many of us know this verse best from Christmas. The notes say, "Jesus does fulfill this prophecy, but not because Micah was simply predicting the future," and that the meaning of fulfillment will be explored more fully in Session 3[^10]. Different traditions in our group may hear this differently. What helps you hold the original meaning and the Christmas meaning together, and what questions would you like to bring to Session 3?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Where in your life, family or church are you tempted to answer pressure by building a "bigger army" (more control, more resources, more strength), and what would trusting God's "backwards" way look like there?

</div>

### Justice, Mercy, Humility <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#justice-mercy-humility}

*The prophet answers his own extravagant question.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Micah 6:1–8 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes describe a courtroom scene in which the offers escalate: burnt offerings and year-old calves, thousands of rams, ten thousand rivers of olive oil, and finally the firstborn child[^7]. What do you notice about this progression, and what does the person asking seem to believe about what God wants?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes are careful: this "is not a rejection of temple worship or sacrifices, but a clarification of priorities." They describe the three requirements as right action (*mishpat*, actively making things right), right affection (loving kindness and compassion) and right relationship (humility before God)[^7]. How do the three depend on one another? What happens when one is missing?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Choose the one of the three requirements that feels hardest for you in this season. What is one specific way you could practice it before we meet again?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Micah spoke to a Judah that felt strong and secure, and he pictured God as a judge who does not merely pronounce verdicts but rolls up his sleeves to make things right. He reminded them that God's kingdom has always come through the small, the humble and the unexpected, as it did with a shepherd boy from Bethlehem. And he answered the question of what God wants with three demanding words: justice, mercy and humility. This time the people listened, and it made a difference.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, name the one requirement from Micah 6:8 you will carry this week and where you hope to practice it.

</div>
