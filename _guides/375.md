### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Isaiah 40–48 brought comfort to exiles, named the servant again and again as Jacob/Israel, and suggested that the nations see God in *how* his people suffer.[^1]

**This week:** Marty and Brent finish Isaiah's Servant Discourse, come to the suffering servant of Isaiah 52–53, and ask what faithful suffering produces.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode begins with a debated question: who is the servant in Isaiah 49? Through most of the discourse the servant has been Jacob or Israel, but here the language becomes personal, and some Jewish readers think the servant is Isaiah himself. The hosts follow God's admission that Israel's sin led to exile, his question about why no one answered when he came to rescue them, and his call to "remember the rock from which you were cut" (Abraham and Sarah).[^3]

At the heart of the episode is Isaiah 52:13–53:12. Marty asks listeners to hear it at least once as a word to God's suffering people in exile, before reading Jesus into it. Jewish readers have long heard it as speaking of Israel, and Christians hear Jesus in it; Marty does not want to remove Jesus from the passage, but to read it on its own terms first. The episode ends with what faithful suffering produces: a rebuilt city better than the first (Isaiah 54) and the open invitation of Isaiah 55. Some questions touch on suffering; share only what you choose to.[^4]

### Who Is the Servant? <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#who-is-the-servant}

*The servant's identity is debated, and the episode leaves the question open.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 49:5–6 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes explain that Jewish commentators debate whether the servant is Israel collectively or Isaiah the prophet. Verse 5 raises the question: "If the servant has been Israel, how can the servant also bring Israel back?" Brent confirms that the Hebrew is singular, and Marty still sees the same servant, while granting that Isaiah may be living out the role in a distinctive way. "He says the question is not settled."[^5] What do you notice in these two verses that makes the servant sound like a whole people, and what makes it sound like one person?

*Read Isaiah 50:1–3 and Isaiah 51:1–3 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> In Isaiah 50 God admits that the people's sin brought them into exile, but he also asks, "Was my arm too short to deliver you?" The notes say the tone is not only rebuke: "Yes, your sin got you here, but when I came looking for you, why was no one there?"[^6] How does this passage hold accountability and hope together? Where might someone in a long hardship hear that question today?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Isaiah 51 says, "Look to the rock from which you were cut... Look to Abraham your father and to Sarah." Marty's diagnosis is that God's people "forgot the plot": they forgot what it means to trust the story and to be people of **radical hospitality**, as Abraham was. The notes add that churches and individuals often drift "into habit, consumerism, or self-protection."[^7] What is the "rock" you were cut from, as a person or a church? Where have you seen drift, and what would going back to the founding story look like?

</div>

### The Suffering Servant <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-suffering-servant}

*Marty invites us to hear this passage at least once as exiled Israel would have.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 52:13–53:12 aloud, with several voices taking turns.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Brent's practice is to "go line by line and ask what each phrase meant during the exile," for example, "what would 'he was assigned a grave with the wicked' have meant to the first hearers?" The notes stress that the passage comes after 11 or 12 chapters about exile and suffering and "should not be read in isolation."[^8] Choose one line that stood out as it was read. What might it have meant to a people in exile?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty tries to read Isaiah 53 with his eyes instead of hearing it, "because hearing it read aloud pulls him straight to Jesus." The notes suggest that reading a familiar passage in a new translation, or aloud with a different focus, "can help you see it fresh."[^9] Which familiar passages do you find hardest to hear fresh, and what helps you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Read as Israel's story, the notes say, the message is that exile suffering was not wasted: because "this generation was willing to suffer, often for what their parents did," and to suffer "without redemptive violence," the next generation will be healed. Jewish commentators do not identify the servant as Jesus. Marty, for his part, says that once the passage is understood this way, "Jesus becomes the *perfect fulfillment and example* of faithful suffering"; he wants to read the passage on its own terms first, "so that a quick jump to Jesus doesn't blur its meaning."[^10] How does hearing the passage first as a word to Israel change the way you hear it? How can we honor the way Jewish readers have heard it while also hearing Jesus in it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes give an example: a family in which one generation "chooses forgiveness instead of revenge after an injustice," so that "their children grow up in a healthier world than the parents had."[^11] Share only what you are comfortable sharing: where have you seen someone's faithful suffering bear fruit for others, or where are you hoping your own will?

</div>

### Jewels and Free Bread <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#jewels-and-free-bread}

*What perseverance produces is redemption: a better city and an open invitation.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 54:11–17 aloud. Then read Isaiah 55:1–3 and 55:10–12 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes say "no weapon forged against you will prevail" is used in Western, especially American, Christianity "as a slogan for triumph." In Isaiah it follows a celebration of faithful suffering and loss, so "the promise belongs to people who have *already* gone through suffering faithfully." The rebuilt city of turquoise and jewels is better than "Jerusalem 1.0."[^12] What do you notice about how verse 17 reads when you hear it right after Isaiah 53? How does that change the promise?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty calls Isaiah 55 "RELIEF": come, buy, and eat without money; "Why spend money on what is not bread?"; and God's word, like rain and snow, "will not return to me empty."[^13] Which line of Isaiah 55 feels most like relief to you, and why?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes compare Isaiah 54 to a town rebuilt after a fire or flood that "sometimes leaves the place stronger than before."[^14] Think of a hard season you have come through, or are still in. What, if anything, has been rebuilt, and what are you still waiting to see?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Isaiah 49–55 asks us to read one of the best-known passages in the Bible in an unfamiliar way. Before we see Jesus in Isaiah 53, we can hear it as Israel in exile would have: a people called to suffer faithfully instead of turning to violence or despair. Isaiah says that suffering is not wasted. It produces healing for the next generation, a rebuilt city more beautiful than the first, and an invitation for the thirsty to come and eat without paying. Read this way, Jesus does not lose his place in the passage; he becomes its clearest example.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, name one place where you want to keep expecting God to answer.

</div>

They explore the Hebrew word *racham* (womb, compassion) and what these images mean for parenting and masculinity. Birth and parenting images can be painful for some, and the hosts encourage working through them together.[^15]
