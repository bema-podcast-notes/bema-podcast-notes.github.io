### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Hidden in a genealogy, we met Abram choosing to marry Sarai, a woman everyone knew was barren, giving up his own line to give her protection and dignity. Before God ever called him, Abram was someone who knew when to say enough and put others first.

**This week:** God calls Abram to leave his father's household. He builds altars and pitches tents, makes a serious mistake in Egypt, learns from it, and then lets his nephew Lot go, twice.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This episode covers Genesis 12–14. It begins with how radical God's call was: in the ancient world, leaving your *beit av* (father's household) meant leaving your livelihood, identity, family, and even your father's gods.[^1] Abram responds by building altars to God's name and pitching tents instead of settling, a quiet contrast with the builders at Babel.[^2]

The heart of the episode is honest about failure. During a famine, Abram goes down to Egypt with a plan built on a half-truth about Sarai, and the plan backfires at the center of the story.[^3] The hosts describe the tension every believer feels between trusting God and doing the responsible thing, and they show how Abram carries what he learned into his conflict with Lot.[^4] The big idea is that mistakes do not have to define us; what matters is the next right choice.[^5]

### Leaving the House of the Father <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#leaving-the-house-of-the-father}

*God asks Abram to leave the one thing everyone in his world depended on.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 12:1–9 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes describe the *beit av*, "House of the Father," as everything: "EVERYONE lived under this patriarchal umbrella," and "A father provides everything for his children, even his gods."[^6] There is even a story that on the night Abram left, he broke his father's idols with an ax, all except one, in whose hands he placed the ax.[^7] Look at verse 1 again. What exactly is Abram asked to leave, and what is he promised in return? What do you notice about who the blessing is ultimately for?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes observe that Abram builds altars, making God's name permanent rather than his own, and pitches tents, staying mobile and ready to move whenever God asks.[^8] The notes set this against the Babel mentality of settling down and making one's own name great.[^9] What does the combination of altar and tent say about how Abram holds God's promise?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes point out that the whole ancient world lived this way: "It is the Western world that brings this sense of individuality and moves away from this sense of family and community."[^10] Most of us grew up assuming independence from our parents' household was normal and healthy. What does that assumption make easier for us to understand about Abram's call, and what does it make harder? Where in your own life might God be asking you to hold something like a tent rather than a tower?

</div>

### The Uh-Oh Moment <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-uh-oh-moment}

*Abram's plan in Egypt is clever, partly true, and it backfires.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 12:10–20 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes explain the background. The Nile Delta flooded every year and held up to 100 feet of farmable topsoil, so Egypt was the region's breadbasket and going there in a famine was standard practice.[^11] Abram calling Sarai his "sister" was technically accurate, since Hebrew kinship terms like "sister" and "brother" cover a range of relatives.[^12] The plan was economic: suitors would bring gifts to Abram as head of the household, and no one would kill him to get her. But Pharaoh did not follow normal courtship customs.[^13] As you listen to verses 10–16, what do you notice about the plan, about who has power, and about the moment it goes wrong?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes place the center of this chapter's chiasm at verses 14–15, Abram's "uh-oh moment," when Pharaoh's officials see Sarai and she is taken into the palace.[^14] The lessons the notes draw are that things do not always go according to our plans and that there are always factors we cannot see or control.[^15] Why might the writer build the story so that this moment sits at the center, rather than God's rescue or Pharaoh's rebuke?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes name the struggle plainly: "Balance between trusting G-d and doing the responsible thing." They also say, "This ends up being a bad move," while another summary insists that going to Egypt during a famine was "not necessarily unfaithful."[^16] Our traditions draw this line in different places, and thoughtful believers land on different sides of it. When you face your own famine, how do you tell the difference between responsible planning and trying to control the outcome? What has shaped where you draw that line?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> One summary puts the hope of this story simply: "our next right choice matters more than our last wrong one."[^17] Without sharing more than you want to, think of an "Egypt moment" of your own. What would it look like to let it teach you without letting it define you?

</div>

### Letting Lot Go <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#letting-lot-go}

*Abram applies what he learned in Egypt, and it costs him what he thought he needed.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 13:5–12 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes raise a question: why is Lot with Abram at all, when he technically belongs to Terah's household? Their answer is that Abram, out of faithfulness, took Lot along believing his promised great nation would come through Lot. So when the two part ways, Abram "gives up his great nation AND he puts Lot first."[^18] As the senior member of the family, Abram had the right to choose first.[^19] What do you notice about how Abram handles the conflict, and what does he risk by letting Lot choose?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> In Genesis 14, Lot and his family are captured by kings. Abram rescues them, "But then Avram gives his Lot back a second time," and he refuses to keep the king of Sodom's wealth, even though it costs him materially.[^20] The notes connect this to the Egypt story: Abram has learned he cannot control circumstances, and he applies that lesson to Lot.[^21] How does Abram's behavior in chapters 13–14 compare with his behavior in Egypt, and what does that change tell you about how people grow?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes describe mature faith as letting go "even of things we believe are essential to God's promises."[^22] What is something you are holding tightly because you believe God's good future depends on it? What would it look like to hold it with an open hand this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Abram leaves everything his world said a person needed: his father's house, his father's trade, even his father's gods. He builds altars to God's name and lives in tents. Then, in a famine, he tries to manage the outcome in Egypt and learns at the center of the story that he cannot control everything. What makes him remarkable is not that he never fails, but what he does next: he lets Lot choose first, rescues him, gives him back again, and walks away from Sodom's wealth. Our next right choice can matter more than our last wrong one.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one thing you sense you need to let go of, or one tent you want to keep pitched?

</div>
