### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** The "good eye" (*ayin tovah*) tied together treasure, worry, judging, and prayer. We saw that worry makes us stingy and quick to judge the worth of others, while trusting the story that begins in Genesis 1 makes us generous.

**This week:** The Sermon on the Mount ends. Jesus closes with images of gates, trees, and houses, and each one asks whether we will actually live what we have heard.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode reads the end of the sermon as a call to orthopraxy (right practice) and not only orthodoxy (right belief). The narrow gate, it argues, is less about heaven and hell than about the deliberate effort Kingdom living requires, echoing Moses' call in Deuteronomy to choose life. Teachers are known "by their fruit," not first by their doctrine.

The hosts also introduce a Jewish teaching about how the Kingdom comes, drawn from the Exodus: God's finger moves, people call him Lord, and people respond in obedience. They suggest that Protestant Christianity tends to be strong on the first two and uneasy about the third, and they are careful to call this Kingdom theology, not salvation theology. Finally, building on rock or sand turns out to be a choice between the bedrock above a desert ravine and the inviting flat floor of a wadi. Several of these readings touch how our traditions think about faith and works, so give each other room.

### The Narrow Gate <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#the-narrow-gate}

*The gate is narrow because Kingdom living cannot be stumbled into.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 7:13–14 aloud, then Deuteronomy 30:15–20.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes say Jesus "makes no mention of eternal destinations" here, and that a Jewish listener would have heard Moses' words in Deuteronomy about setting before Israel life and death and calling them to choose life.[^1] Put the two passages side by side. What words or ideas do you notice they share? What does reading Jesus through Deuteronomy do to the way you hear his warning about the road to destruction?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** Marty describes an interchange in Washington's Tri-Cities where drivers must merge from a left-side on-ramp, cross four or five lanes in less than a quarter mile, and exit right. First-time drivers usually miss it; even experienced drivers have to approach it "with resolute intention."[^2] Where in your own discipleship does following Jesus feel like that interchange? What happens when you drift with the traffic?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes read "destruction" here as "the natural consequences of living contrary to how God designed the world to function," not eternal damnation.[^3] Our traditions have taught this passage in different ways, often as a heaven-and-hell text. How were you taught to read it, and what is gained or lost if you read it the way the episode does?

</div>

### Fruit and the Coming of the Kingdom <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#fruit-and-the-coming-of-the-kingdom}

*Jesus tests teachers by their fruit and describes a Kingdom that needs both confession and obedience.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 7:15–23 aloud, then Galatians 5:22–23.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The episode says Western Christianity tends to define false teachers by orthodoxy, asking "Are they interpreting things correctly?" Jesus says "by their fruit you will recognize them," not by their doctrine or words.[^4] Marty's summary in the original notes: "He's not validating good trees, he's validating good fruit."[^5] Look at Paul's list in Galatians. If you used that list to evaluate a teacher or a ministry, what would you look at? How is that different from how teachers are usually evaluated in your tradition?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes describe a rabbinic teaching drawn from the Exodus about how Kingdom comes: (1) the finger of God moves, as in the plagues and the Red Sea; (2) people call him Lord, as Israel first called on God's name at the Red Sea; and (3) people respond in obedience, as at Sinai.[^6] Read verse 21 again. Which of these three elements do you see in Jesus' words, and which one is he emphasizing?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The episode suggests that Protestant Christianity tends to emphasize the first two elements well (salvation is "all of God," and confession of Jesus as Lord) but often struggles with obedience because of fears about "works-based religion." The notes are careful: "The Protestant audience should not hear this as 'you must have obedience to be saved' but rather as 'Kingdom appears when all three elements are present.'"[^7] Our traditions have drawn the lines between grace, faith, and obedience in different places. Without trying to settle those debates, how does your tradition talk about obedience? Where does this distinction between Kingdom and salvation help, and where does it raise questions for you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes also describe non-Christian friends who seem to have "more Jesus oozing out of their bones" than many church members: God has moved in their lives and they live out Kingdom ways, but they do not call Jesus Lord.[^8] Who comes to mind for you? What does the threefold teaching help you say about them, and what does it leave unresolved?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes stress that the three elements do not have to come in order, because God's finger "has already moved and continues to move." The question "is whether we will do our part with elements two and three."[^9] In your own life right now, which of the two (calling him Lord, or doing what he says) comes more naturally, and which needs more of your attention?

</div>

### Rock, Sand, and Authority <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#rock-sand-and-authority}

*Building on the rock was never the obvious choice, which is why the crowds were amazed.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 7:24–29 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes explain that the "sand" is the floor of a wadi, a desert ravine that is dry most of the year and floods violently in rare storms, and the "rock" is the bedrock or cliff face above it. For eleven months the wadi builder looks wise: flat, accessible, sheltered. The cliff builder looks foolish.[^10] Given how backwards Jesus' teaching sounded, the episode says building on it is not the "obvious easy choice."[^11] What made the wadi the sensible, conventional choice? Where in your life does following Jesus' teaching look like the less sensible option?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The crowds were amazed because Jesus taught "as one who had authority." The notes connect this to *s'micha*, a rabbi's authority to reinterpret Torah, usually received through ordination after years of study; people would have asked, "Who's his rabbi?"[^12] The episode hears Jesus saying, "Trust me. This is what the Torah was always meant to do."[^13] Looking back over the whole sermon, which of Jesus' teachings is hardest for you to build on? What would it take to trust him there?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The Sermon on the Mount ends not with a summary but with a choice. The narrow gate echoes Moses' call to choose life, and it takes intention, like an interchange you cannot drift through. Teachers and disciples are known by their fruit. The Kingdom comes when God moves, people call him Lord, and people actually do the will of the Father. And building on Jesus' words looks foolish for eleven months of the year, right up until the flood comes.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each: what is one teaching from the Sermon on the Mount that you intend to put into practice, not just remember?

</div>
