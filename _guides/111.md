### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We learned the four levels of PaRDeS, followed the word "hundredfold" back to Isaac digging wells, and began practicing on the Parable of the Weeds.

**This week:** We apply the method to two tiny parables. A farmer plants mustard and a woman works yeast into dough, and both images point back to the Hebrew Scriptures and outward to the nations.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode applies PaRDeS to the Parable of the Mustard Seed and the Parable of the Leaven, two short stories the notes call "intimately connected."[^1] Both would have startled their first hearers: mustard was "considered a toxic weed," and leaven "typically represented sin in Jewish thought."[^2] The notes draw out four characteristics of the Kingdom from them: small beginnings, a counterintuitive nature, unstoppable growth, and blessing to outsiders.[^3]

The hints come from unusual details. Birds perching in the branches point to Ezekiel 17, where God replants Israel as a tree that shelters "birds of every kind."[^4] Three measures of flour point to Sarah baking bread for three unknown visitors in Genesis 18, a story of radical hospitality set right before Sodom's inhospitality.[^5]

### The Mustard Seed <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#the-mustard-seed}

*A farmer plants the smallest of seeds, and something unexpected grows.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 13:31–32 aloud, then Ezekiel 17:22–24.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Compare the two passages. What words, images or ideas does Jesus's parable share with Ezekiel's vision? The notes identify "birds come and perch in its branches" as the *remez*, the "hint," because it is the detail that seems unnecessary.[^6] What does Ezekiel say God will do with the tree, and who comes to live in it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes say mustard plants "were considered toxic weeds that farmers would never intentionally plant," that they spread "through extensive root systems," and that "Listeners would have laughed at the idea of someone planting mustard seed deliberately."[^7] They also note Jesus's "botanically impossible claim that a mustard bush becomes a tree," which echoes Ezekiel 17:24 where God makes "the low tree grow tall."[^8] Why would Jesus choose a weed to describe the Kingdom of Heaven?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes explain that in Ezekiel animals represent nations, so birds nesting in Israel's tree picture God's people finally fulfilling "their original Abrahamic calling: to be a blessing to all nations."[^9] They conclude that "the Kingdom of Heaven exists to bless outsiders, foreigners, and those excluded from religious society."[^10] (Marty adds what the notes call "a personal theory," that Jesus hints at Ezekiel more than any other prophet.)[^11] How does this reading fit with what we have seen of Matthew's concern for the *mamzer*, which the notes gloss as an "illegitimate child or outsider"?[^12]

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** If the Kingdom is a tree where all kinds of birds can perch, who are the "birds" in your town or workplace who need a place to land, and what branch could you offer them?

</div>

### The Leaven <span class="guide-time">35 min</span> {#the-leaven}

*A woman works yeast into a large batch of flour.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 13:33 aloud, then Genesis 18:1–8.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Notice the amount of flour in Matthew 13:33 and then what Abraham asks Sarah to do in Genesis 18:6. What do you notice? The notes say "three measures" is about 60 pounds of flour, and that Jewish listeners "would immediately recall Sarah preparing bread for three mysterious visitors."[^13] What does the size of that batch suggest about who the bread is for?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes point out that during Passover, Jewish families "meticulously removed all leaven from their homes," and that comparing the Kingdom to leaven "would have been shocking and provocative."[^14] They also say Genesis deliberately sets Abraham's welcome beside the next story, where "The same visitors who received generous hospitality from Abraham were violently rejected by Sodom."[^15] Putting those pieces together, what is Jesus saying the Kingdom is like?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes list four characteristics of the Kingdom from these parables: "small beginnings, counterintuitive nature, unstoppable growth, and blessing to outsiders."[^16] They add that understanding these parables required change, "treating Romans differently, welcoming foreigners, sharing wealth with outsiders," and that many religious people "weren't interested in this transformation."[^17] Which of the four characteristics is hardest for you to believe or practice, and what would the equivalent changes look like for a church community today?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** From Sarah's example the notes draw three practices: "prepare more than necessary, welcome strangers without knowing their identity or worthiness, and trust God to multiply the impact."[^18] What is one small act of hospitality, the kind that looks like a pinch of yeast, that you could practice this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Jesus compared the Kingdom of Heaven to a weed no farmer would plant and to the leaven every Jewish home cleared out at Passover. Both start small, both are counterintuitive, and both are impossible to stop once they begin. The birds in the branches send us to Ezekiel, where God's replanted people shelter every kind of bird. The three measures of flour send us to Sarah, baking for strangers whose names she did not know. The Kingdom grows through small, generous acts of welcome toward outsiders.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: who is one outsider you could welcome in a small way this week?

</div>
