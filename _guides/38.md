### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** In the dark days of the judges, Ruth and Boaz showed covenant love in practice, and a Moabite widow became the great-grandmother of David.

**This week:** Israel asks for a king. The episode sets Israel's first two kings side by side and asks what each one reveals about God's people and about how God chooses.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode opens at the end of Judges, where the story of the Levite's concubine mirrors Sodom and Gomorrah. The hosts see a shift here from the "redemption cycle" to the "anti-story": Israel is no longer struggling to follow God but working against his purposes.[^1] Asking for a king was not wrong in itself, since Deuteronomy 17 anticipated it. The problem was Israel's motive: they wanted to be "like all the other nations" and wanted a king to fight their battles.[^2]

God gives them Saul, tall and impressive, but first met as a donkey herder who cannot find his donkeys, from the tribe of Benjamin. Then God chooses David, the youngest of eight, a shepherd from Judah.[^3] The episode names the difference between them with the Hebrew phrase *kiddush HaShem*, "hallowing the name," and gives two reasons God keeps choosing people from the bottom of the heap.[^4] This is a short episode, so the guide gives extra time to reading the texts together.

### A King Like the Nations <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#a-king-like-the-nations}

*Israel's request was anticipated, but their reasons gave them away.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Deuteronomy 17:14–15 aloud, then 1 Samuel 8:1–20.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** If you did the preparation, share the reasons you found. The notes say "The issue wasn't asking for a king per se," because Deuteronomy anticipated the request. The problem was Israel's specific language in 1 Samuel 8, wanting to be "like all the other nations" and wanting a king to "go out before us and fight our battles."[^5] Compare the two passages. What does Deuteronomy require of a king, and what does Israel actually ask for?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes call the second statement "particularly egregious because throughout their history, they've been told repeatedly that the Lord goes before them to fight their battles." They add that Israel had "lost the plot of being different - of being a kingdom of priests whose very existence causes the nations to ask, 'What's different about your God?'"[^6] Remember the crossroads of the earth from three weeks ago. Why would wanting to look like the nations undercut the very reason God placed Israel where he did?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes point out that Eli's sons were corrupt, that Samuel's sons also turned out wicked, and that Israel used this corruption as leverage: "The corruption of spiritual leadership becomes the excuse for abandoning their distinctive calling."[^7] The notes recall a warning from Bible college: "Take care of your home life well because who knows how far that gets passed on to your disciples and people after you?"[^8] Where have you seen failures in leadership become an excuse for something else?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes describe the calling to "be distinctively holy while engaged with the world - different enough that people notice and ask questions."[^9] In what part of your life are you most tempted to want what "all the other nations" have, and what would it look like there to be different enough that someone might ask about your God?

</div>

### A Donkey Herder <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#a-donkey-herder}

*God gives Israel exactly what they asked for, with a message tucked inside.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read 1 Samuel 9:1–4 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** What is Saul doing when we first meet him, and how is it going? The notes say "Donkeys in Hebrew culture represent stubbornness and stiff-neckedness," and "Israel needs a shepherd (for sheep), not a donkey herder."[^10] What do you think the narrator is saying about Israel by introducing their first king this way?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes add two more details. Saul comes from Benjamin, whose horrific story at the end of Judges was still fresh, and the "stench of that story is still present in the nostrils of Israel."[^11] His impressive appearance recalls Jeremiah's *ar'ar* bush in the desert, "something that looks impressive but has no substance."[^12] What does the Benjamin connection add to the picture of Saul?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The *ar'ar* bush "represents trusting in human strength rather than God."[^13] Where are you most tempted to trust what looks impressive on the outside: in leaders you follow, in your own image, or in the things you build?

</div>

### The Youngest Son <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-youngest-son}

*Samuel goes down the line of impressive brothers, and God chooses the one who was not invited.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read 1 Samuel 16:1–13 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes compare this scene to Cinderella: Samuel goes down the line of impressive older brothers "like the stepsisters trying on the glass slipper," but the one chosen is "working in the fields, smelling like sheep, overlooked by his own father."[^14] As you listen, what details show how overlooked David was, even by his own family?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes trace a pattern of God "choosing from the bottom" (Jacob over Esau, Ephraim over Manasseh, slaves rescued from the strongest empire, David over his brothers, Ruth in the royal line) and give two reasons for it.[^15] First, clarity of source: "When the least succeeds, no one is confused about whose strength is on display." Second, memory and humility: "People from the bottom remember their story better."[^16] Which of these two reasons speaks more to you, and why?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe *kiddush HaShem* ("hallowing the name") as David's key trait: he wants to hallow God's name, be about God's project, and do things God's way, while Saul wants "to hallow his own name, build his own reputation, pursue his own project, and do things his way."[^17] The notes connect this to the Lord's Prayer: "Hallowed be Your name."[^18] What does it look like in ordinary life to hallow God's name rather than your own?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q11:** The notes say David could remember "what it's like to be the last born of eight sons, to smell like sheep, to be overlooked by his own father," and that "Those who forget where they came from often begin 'hallowing their own name' like Saul."[^19] What is the humble place you came from, your own version of smelling like sheep, and what helps you remember it?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> By the end of Judges, Israel had moved from struggling to follow God to living out the anti-story. When they asked for a king, the request itself was anticipated, but their reasons revealed that they wanted to be like everyone else and wanted someone other than God to fight their battles. God gave them a tall, impressive donkey herder from Benjamin. Then he sent Samuel to a family where the youngest son was not even called in from the fields, and chose him. God keeps choosing from the bottom, so that no one mistakes whose strength is at work and so that the ones he chooses remember where they came from.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one way you could hallow God's name, not your own, this week?

</div>
