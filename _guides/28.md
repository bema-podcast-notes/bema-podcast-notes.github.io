### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** The little rotem bush taught us that God often gives "just enough" shade, and that each of us is called to be shade for others without needing to be an oak tree. The acacia, which can look dead for years, showed us what "fruit in season" in Psalm 1 might mean.[^1]

**This week:** Jeremiah sets a beautiful but hollow desert bush against the acacia, and a slow-growing tree explains why Abraham's planting one at Beersheba was an act of deep trust.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This short episode adds two plants to the desert picture. The ar'ar is a bright green, lush-looking bush with large fruit, but the fruit is mostly empty, holding a toxic milky substance and something like spider webs. Following Noga Hareuveni, Marty identifies it as the bush in Jeremiah 17:5–6, where the one who trusts in human strength is set against the one who trusts in the Lord, pictured as a tree by the water (the acacia from last week). The Hebrew wordplay ties *arur* ("cursed"), *ar'ar* (the bush) and *arava* (the desert wasteland) together.

The tamarisk is almost always planted, needs cultivation, gives shade noticeably cooler than other desert shade, and takes about 80 years to grow. People plant it for their grandchildren. That turns a verse most of us read past, Abraham planting a tamarisk at Beersheba in Genesis 21, into a statement of trust in God's long promise, and it gives us a rabbinic question to carry: "How many tamarisk trees did you plant today?"

### The Ar'ar and the Acacia <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#the-arar-and-the-acacia}

*Jeremiah describes two kinds of people by describing two desert plants.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Jeremiah 17:5–8 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Marty follows Noga Hareuveni in identifying the bush of verse 6 as the ar'ar and the tree of verse 8 as the acacia. The notes point out a wordplay in Hebrew: the one who is *arur* ("cursed," the strongest Hebrew word for binding someone to destruction) is like an *ar'ar* in the *arava*, the desert wasteland.[^3] Listen to the two halves of the passage. What does each person trust, where does each one live, and how does each respond when heat and drought come? Which details stand out to you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The ar'ar is bright green with large fruit; Marty says your first reaction is "It's got to be delicious." But the fruit pops when opened and is mostly empty, holding a toxic milky substance and spider-web-like material.[^4] He sums up Jeremiah's contrast this way: "One looks alive on the outside but is actually the opposite inside. The other looks dead on the outside but deep down actually has what it takes to bring life and become the gift of the desert."[^5] Why might Jeremiah pick such an attractive plant to picture the person who trusts in "mere flesh"? What does trusting in your own strength look like while it is still working?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes apply the ar'ar and acacia to communities as well as individuals, contrasting "flashy programs lacking spiritual depth" with "humble faithfulness bearing genuine fruit."[^6] Each of our traditions has its own ways of measuring whether a church is healthy. Without pointing fingers at any one of them, what are the visible signs churches (including ours) tend to count, and what acacia-like signs of life might those measures miss?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes offer a reflection question: "What 'ar'ar bushes' in my life look promising but may be spiritually empty or toxic?"[^7] Where are you most tempted to tend the outside of your life (how things look, how you are seen) more than the inside, and what is one way you could put down deeper roots this month?

</div>

### Planting Tamarisk Trees <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#planting-tamarisk-trees}

*Some trees are planted for people the planter will never meet.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The tamarisk tree is almost always planted because it needs work and cultivation. Its foliage takes in the little moisture in the air and releases it into its shade, which is about 15% cooler than other desert shade. It takes 80 years to grow, so it is usually planted not for the planter or even the planter's children, but for grandchildren.[^8] Who planted something you now enjoy the shade of (a family member, a teacher, a church, a stranger)?

*Read Genesis 21:22–34 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Marty calls the end of this chapter a passage "we might normally read over" but finds it very moving. After making a treaty with Abimelek, Abraham plants a tamarisk tree in Beersheba and calls on the name of the Lord, the Eternal God. Even in his old age, Abraham plants a tree that takes 80 years to grow because he plans to be there for a long time; that is how much he trusts the promise of God.[^9] What do you notice about the events around the planting, and about the name of God Abraham calls on? What is Abraham saying, to himself and to others, by planting this tree?

*Read Hebrews 11:13–16 and 11:39–40 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Marty connects the tamarisk to Hebrews 11, where people of faith saw God's promise and welcomed it from a distance, and to spiritual disciplines done whether or not we feel like doing them that day. One of the desert's lessons, he says, is how to do things that will live far beyond us, things we may never see the benefit of.[^10] Brent adds a question of his own: does Jacob ever return to the tree, and if not, what does that say about Jacob?[^11] What makes it so hard to invest in what we will never see, and what does it mean when a later generation walks away from the shade someone planted for them?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The rabbinic teaching asks, "How many tamarisk trees did you plant today?" The notes restate it: "How many things are you doing daily that will live beyond you? That your grandchildren will reap the benefits of?"[^12] What is one tamarisk tree you are already planting, or one you could plant this week, whether in your family, your church or your daily habits?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#closing}

> The desert keeps teaching us to look beneath the surface. The ar'ar looks alive and is empty inside; the acacia looks dead and holds what it takes to become the gift of the desert. Jeremiah says the difference is trust: in our own strength, or in the Lord. And the tamarisk asks us to trust God's promise far enough ahead that we plant shade for grandchildren we may never meet, the way Abraham did at Beersheba.[^13]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one tamarisk tree you want to plant before we meet again?

</div>
