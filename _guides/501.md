### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Rabbi Dr. David Rudolph showed how the church moved from relying on the Jewish community to set the date of *Pascha* to cutting that tie at Nicaea, and he called us to honest remembrance and to "do Jewish things with Jewish people."[^1]

**This week:** The series returns to the pillar of Text. Elle Grover Fricks offers a provocation: that Scripture may be "bigger, different, or wilder" than modern readers usually allow.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Elle calls this her "weirdest episode ever." Two earlier episodes in the series covered more conventional ways to approach the Bible, so she uses this one to make a single case. Using a framework called spiral dynamics, which sorts worldviews and value systems by color, she affirms that the Bible can be read through many lenses. Then she argues that the Bible itself is "a purple book about purple things calling us to live purple lives," full of blessings and curses, dreams, angels, holy places, sacred seasons and a risen Messiah.[^3]

The conversation also touches knowing and trusting, Brent's experience of guidance through dreams, cessationism, and how charismatic communities test prophetic "words." Our traditions in this room have different histories with the Holy Spirit's gifts. Elle explicitly says she is not condemning any church or tradition, and this guide asks us to describe and listen rather than to settle that debate tonight.[^4]

### A Lens, Not a Ladder <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#a-lens-not-a-ladder}

*Elle uses spiral dynamics to help us notice the lens we already read through.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** In spiral dynamics, the colors include purple (magic and mystery), blue (order, authority and "capital-T Truth"), orange (Enlightenment rationality), green (the wider circle of all communities), yellow (personal freedom) and turquoise (the unity of all things). Elle stresses that it is a lens, not a ladder: people move between stages in either direction, and a move often feels like a personal "apocalypse," meaning a revelation.[^5] She describes how each color reads Scripture: blue as steady Truth while the world shifts, orange as practice that makes me better at everything, green as "It's not all about me," yellow as the compass I choose, turquoise as being part of the body of Christ, and purple as a sacred, living book.[^6] Which of these descriptions sounds most like the way you were taught to read the Bible, and which sounds most like how you read it now?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Elle says every lens can be life-giving, and that if your devotions have felt fruitless for months, trying a different lens may help, because the goal is faithfulness over a lifetime.[^7] If you were to borrow one lens that is not your usual one for the next month, which would you choose, and what might it look like on an ordinary morning?

</div>

### A Purple Book <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#a-purple-book}

*Elle's central claim is that both basic Christian belief and the text itself are full of the supernatural.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Numbers 22:21–35 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The talking donkey and the angel blocking the road are among the examples Elle gives of "purple" material in Scripture, alongside dreams, demons, holy places, sacred seasons and the pillars of fire and cloud.[^8] Listen to the story as a story. What do you notice about how the text itself presents the angel and the donkey? What does the narrator seem to treat as surprising, and what does the narrator treat as ordinary?

*Read Romans 8:9–11 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Elle argues that even the "barest minimum" of Christian belief is already supernatural: one sovereign God who made every star and every neuron, the Trinity, a Messiah who rose and ascended, and "the Spirit of him who raised Jesus from the dead" living in believers. As she puts it, "That's not a rational, post-Enlightenment idea… and that's only the beginning."[^9] She also suggests that explaining miracles away as a volcano or an eclipse can reflect a wish to be "taken seriously by the world," a world that will not accept the resurrection anyway.[^10] Where have you seen (or practiced) this kind of trimming, and what do you think is driving it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Brent says he struggles with Michael Heiser's *The Unseen Realm* because it seems to rest on assumptions he "can't actually know." Elle answers with two Hebrew words: *yada*, to know (relational, experiential knowing), and *batach*, to trust, putting confidence in someone beyond what can be proven. She notes that even the central claims of the faith are things believers choose to trust.[^11] How do knowing and trusting work together in your own faith, and where do you tend to lean on one more than the other?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Elle passes along an image from Reed: Christians often live inside a house while possibilities pass by outside the windows, and everyone, "even the wildest of charismatics," has something they consider beyond belief. The invitation is to "stick your head out the window": take one text that seems too strange, ask God about it, wrestle with it, and see whether your worldview shifts.[^12] What is one passage or story you have quietly set aside as too strange, and what would it look like to wrestle with it rather than skip it?

</div>

### The Spirit and the Sacred Tome <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#the-spirit-and-the-sacred-tome}

*The conversation turns to the Spirit's ongoing voice and to where we give our attention.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** In a post-credits conversation, Elle observes that even strict cessationism (the view that miraculous gifts ended with the apostles) accepts the Spirit's conviction of sin as a normal, ongoing supernatural act. Her question is why conviction would be the *only* thing the Spirit says. Brent agrees: "It would be very strange to have the Spirit of the living God inside of us and… only this one thing."[^13] Our traditions have answered questions about the Spirit's work in different ways. Rather than debating who is right, describe how your tradition taught you to understand the Spirit's work today, and what Elle's question stirs in you.

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Brent describes two dreams on consecutive nights: in the first God said "not this," and he turned down an unexpected opportunity; in the second he was shown where to go. He is convinced God spoke, though dreams are rare for him. He also raises a fair concern about how to keep ongoing revelation from going off the rails. Elle describes the "big box of rules" charismatic communities use: a word must have a "yes and amen" in Scripture, you are free to reject what does not ring true in your spirit, and the Didache (a very early Christian manual) warns against prophets who ask for money.[^14] Which of these guardrails seems most important to you, and what would you add?

*Read Hebrews 4:12 and Isaiah 49:10 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** Elle contrasts Scripture with the algorithm. Brent's feed is full of woodworking and home-improvement videos, and Elle names the hidden message: "Eden can be built by your own hands. You just need a discount code." Brent adds that the feed also implies mastery takes ten seconds, when it really takes years. Scripture, by contrast, offers the river of life and resilience even in suffering.[^15] Thinking back to the opening question, what is one specific change you could make this week so that the sacred text gets more of your attention than the feed?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Elle invites us to see the Bible as strange again. All of us read through a lens shaped by our upbringing and our communities, and she affirms each of those lenses as potentially life-giving. Her challenge is that the Bible itself is unmistakably purple: full of angels, dreams, sacred seasons, holy places and a risen Messiah whose Spirit lives in us. Christians who already trust the most outlandish claim of all have little reason to trim away the rest just to seem reasonable. Hold knowing and trusting together, stick your head out the window at the part of Scripture that feels hardest to believe, and choose the sacred tome over the algorithm.[^16]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, name the "window" you are willing to stick your head out of this week.

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 502 — Community](/guides/502)** (51:22): Marty turns to the second pillar and explains why he places community right after text: it is the pillar he most needs to be challenged on. He defines biblical community as *radical fellowship with people you don't agree with*, built on accountability, being only one piece of God's image, and learning from what others see. The episode moves through Philippians 2, Galatians 1–2, Genesis 1–2, 1 Corinthians 12, John 9 and Numbers 11, and closes with Bonhoeffer's warning about loving our dream of community more than real community.[^17]

**What to bring next week:**
- Read Philippians 2:1–11 and mark one phrase that challenges you.
- Think of one person in your life who differs from you (in age, background, politics or theology) and whose voice you have learned to value. Be ready to say what makes that relationship work, without naming them if you prefer.
