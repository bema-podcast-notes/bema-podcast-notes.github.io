### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We learned that *euangelion*, "gospel," was an imperial announcement of a new king, and that each Gospel writer announced Jesus' Kingdom in his own voice, which is why we will not blend them together.

**This week:** We meet the first of those voices. Matthew, a Jewish author writing to a Jewish audience, opens with a genealogy that tells us exactly what his Gospel is about.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode asks two questions of Matthew's Gospel: who is the author, and who is the audience? Matthew, a former tax collector, writes as a Jew to Jews, and Marty believes his Gospel was written first and originally in Hebrew, a view he acknowledges runs against most opinion.[^1] To understand Matthew's agenda, the episode says, we need look no further than the genealogy in chapter 1. Jewish genealogies were meant to show off a family's pedigree, followed the fathers, and left women out unless they were essential, yet Matthew deliberately includes Tamar, Rahab, Ruth, "Uriah's wife," and Mary.[^2]

The key word is *mamzer* (also spelled *mumzer*), a term from Torah for a child born of an illegitimate or prohibited union, which Marty uses more broadly for the outsider.[^3] Matthew's message is that in Jesus' Kingdom there are no outsiders, and the episode warns that the people most at risk of missing that Kingdom are those who think they already have God figured out, a warning Marty applies first to his own American audience.[^4]

**Opening Question:** If you were writing your family's history to impress someone, who or what might you be tempted to leave out? (You don't need to name names; a general answer is fine.) Why do families tell their stories the way they do?

### A Scandalous Family Tree <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#a-scandalous-family-tree}

*Matthew's opening list of names is not a dry preface; it is his thesis.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 1:1–17 aloud. Take turns, a few verses each.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Besides the long line of fathers, who else is named in this genealogy? List them together, and for each one share what you know of her story. What do you notice about these women as a group?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The original notes put the puzzle plainly: "Genealogies are meant to show off one's pedigree, but Jesus' does exactly the opposite."[^5] The study notes summarize the women: Tamar's story involves deception and Judah's injustice; Rahab is a Canaanite and a prostitute; Ruth is a Moabite, from a people excluded from the assembly; Bathsheba appears only as "Uriah's wife," recalling adultery and murder; Mary is an unmarried pregnant woman.[^6] If you were a Jewish reader expecting a proud pedigree for the Messiah, what message would you hear in this list?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The genealogy is arranged in three sets of fourteen generations, and the notes observe, "No eastern reader would have trouble skipping a generation to make 14, 14, and 14 work."[^7] What does that suggest about what a genealogy was for in Matthew's world, compared with how we might use a family tree today?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Right after the genealogy, Mary is pregnant before her marriage to Joseph, and the notes raise the question everyone in town would ask: is Jesus himself a *mamzer*? They say Matthew starts Jesus' story with the very question his Gospel addresses: "Can God work through those society labels as illegitimate?"[^8] Where have you seen God work through someone, perhaps yourself, who carried a label of "not legitimate" or "not one of us"?

</div>

### The Tax Collector's Agenda <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#the-tax-collectors-agenda}

*Matthew's own story helps explain why his Gospel sounds the way it does.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Matthew writes "Kingdom of Heaven" where the other Gospels write "Kingdom of God." The notes explain that out of reverence for God's name, Jews used substitutes such as "Heaven," *HaShem* ("the Name"), or *Adonai* ("Lord"), and that Matthew uses "Kingdom of God" only once.[^9] The prodigal son's confession, "Father, I have sinned against heaven and against you," means "against God and against you."[^10] What does Matthew's careful language tell you about the audience he respects, even as he challenges them?

*Read Matthew 9:9 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The original notes say Matthew "was a mumzer as a tax collector who effectively sold his soul to Rome," and yet "Jesus invites Matthew to follow him without hesitation regardless."[^11] The study notes add: "No qualification required, no probation period."[^12] How might that single invitation have shaped the way Matthew told the whole story of Jesus? When has an invitation without a probation period changed the way you tell your own story?

</div>

### Insiders and Outsiders <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#insiders-and-outsiders}

*Matthew's pattern is a warning to religious people, and the episode insists that includes us.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes describe a pattern throughout Matthew: "Insider: not in. Outsider: in. Insider: not in. Outsider: in." They hear Jesus saying, in effect, "Those who are in are actually out and those that are out are actually in."[^13] Examples include the Roman centurion whose faith Jesus praises (Matthew 8:10) and tax collectors and prostitutes entering the Kingdom ahead of the religious leaders (Matthew 21:31).[^14] Marty chose Matthew for Session 3 mostly because "the agenda of Matthew is the message that we, mostly Americans, need to hear."[^15] The notes list examples of people churches have treated as outsiders, including divorced people, children of unmarried mothers, people with addiction histories or mental illness, and immigrants and refugees.[^16] Which groups has your own tradition, at some point, treated as outside, and what reasons were given? How do those reasons look to you now in light of Matthew's genealogy?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes offer a practice: "This week, identify one person or group you've unconsciously categorized as 'outside' God's concern or your community's welcome."[^17] Who comes to mind for you, and what is one concrete step you could take toward seeing them differently?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Matthew could have given Jesus a spotless pedigree. Instead he names a Canaanite, a Moabite, women with painful and scandalous stories, and an unmarried mother, and then tells how Jesus called a tax collector with two words: follow me. Matthew knew what it was to be a *mamzer*, and his Gospel announces a Kingdom where that category no longer exists. The warning is aimed at the insiders, at anyone confident they already understand God. As we follow Matthew through Session 3, we will watch for the pattern again and again: the insiders who are not in, and the outsiders who are.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: which name in Jesus' genealogy will you carry with you this week, and why?

</div>
