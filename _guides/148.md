### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Through the allegory of Hagar and Sarah, Paul named God-fearing Gentiles "brothers" and "children of promise," full members of Abraham's family without converting to Judaism.[^1]

**This week:** We finish Galatians and ask what freedom is for, for Jewish and Gentile believers alike.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This episode concludes the study of Galatians. A key point in the notes is that "Paul never questioned whether Jews should continue following Torah"; the debate was whether Gentiles needed to convert. Both groups have been set free in different ways: Jews from believing Torah observance justifies them, Gentiles from the burden of converting. Jewish believers follow Torah to show the world what walking by the Spirit looks like, while Gentile believers live free from Torah to show that "everyone is invited." Both callings serve the same purpose.[^2]

Freedom, the notes stress, is "not license for self-indulgence but a calling to serve others in love." Paul contrasts living by the *sarx* ("flesh," animal appetites) with living by the Spirit, and gives a diagnostic instead of a rulebook: look at the fruit. The episode ties this to BEMA's Empire and Shalom framework, and closes with Paul's call to gentle restoration and his reminder that what counts is "being made into a new creation."[^3]

### Two Audiences, Two Callings <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#two-audiences-two-callings}

*We notice whom Paul is talking to and what each group is called to.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Galatians 5:1–6 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes point to Paul's pronouns: "It is for freedom that Christ has set **us** free" includes both Jews and Gentiles, while "Mark my words, I, Paul, tell **you**" addresses the Gentiles considering circumcision.[^4] Read the verses again slowly and listen for "us," "you," and "I." What shifts do you notice, and how does each change the meaning of what follows?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes describe distinct callings within unity. Jewish believers follow Torah "to show the world what it looks like to walk according to God's Spirit," and Gentile believers live in freedom from Torah "to demonstrate that everyone is invited into God's family."[^5] Why might Gentile freedom from the law be essential to the gospel's message rather than a loophole? What would have been lost if every Gentile had converted?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe Marty's "Pint o' Bacon" scenario: someone who keeps Torah and would not eat bacon might still consider it if a well-meaning Gentile host served it unknowingly, "prioritizing love and hospitality over dietary law."[^6] Where in your own life do love and hospitality call you to hold a conviction or preference more loosely? Where do they call you to keep it?

</div>

### Flesh, Spirit, and the Fruit Test <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#flesh-spirit-and-the-fruit-test}

*We look at what freedom is for and how to tell whether we are using it well.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Galatians 5:13–26 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** In verses 13–15, what does Paul say freedom is for, and what does he warn it can become? Then look at the two lists in verses 19–23. What kinds of things are on each list, and what do you notice about how many of them have to do with how we treat other people?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes say *sarx* means "animal appetites/fleshly desires," and that Paul's contrast "is not about sinful nature versus good nature, but about choosing between self-focused living and Spirit-led living that serves others." They connect this to Empire (self-preservation, scarcity, "getting more for myself") and Shalom (self-sacrifice, abundance, "blessing others").[^7] Our traditions have taught "the flesh" in different ways, sometimes as a sinful nature we are born with. How was it taught to you? What does the notes' reading add, challenge, or leave unsettled for you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes suggest that instead of following a list of commands, Gentile believers can ask of their choices: "Does this produce love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control?"[^8] Take the decision you chose to test this week. What did the question reveal, and what will you do with it?

</div>

### Freedom with Accountability <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#freedom-with-accountability}

*We see how Paul keeps freedom from turning into "anything goes."*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Galatians 6:1–5 and Galatians 6:15 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes say Paul answers worries about lawlessness "by emphasizing gentle restoration of those caught in sin, carrying each other's burdens while taking responsibility for one's own spiritual growth," and that "an inclusive Gospel does not mean abandoning accountability or absolute truth."[^9] What does gentle restoration look like in practice? How is it different from both ignoring sin and policing it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode asks whether contemporary Christianity has created its own "circumcision requirements," cultural or doctrinal markers that decide who can "sit at the table," rather than focusing on whether people are being made into new creations.[^10] As we finish Galatians, what is one marker you have used to judge whether someone belongs? What would it look like to look for new creation instead?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Over five weeks in Galatians we met God-fearing Gentiles under pressure to convert, watched the pillars shake Paul's hand and Peter falter at the table, followed Abraham's trust back before circumcision, and heard Hagar and Sarah speak of slavery and freedom. Paul ends where the whole letter has been heading: Christ has set us free, Jew and Gentile alike, each with a calling that shows the world God's welcome. That freedom is for serving one another in love, and the test is the fruit it bears. What counts is not circumcision or uncircumcision, but a new creation.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one way you will use your freedom to serve someone this week?

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 149 — Romans — Blended Family](/guides/149)** (38:17): We begin Romans, which the episode reads as a pastoral letter to a divided church rather than a theological treatise. After Claudius expelled the Jews from Rome, Gentile believers led the church for years; when Jewish believers returned, tensions over identity and belonging followed, and Paul shows that no group has grounds for superiority.[^11]

**What to bring next week:**
- Read Romans 1:1–3:20 in one sitting if you can, noticing whom Paul seems to be addressing in each section.
- Think of a time your church or family went through a "blended family" season, when newcomers and returning members had to find their places together.
