### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We saw that church and synagogue parted slowly and unevenly, and we faced the 1,500-year "teaching of contempt" that turned theological anti-Judaism into persecution, along with the cautious hope of post-Holocaust dialogue.[^1]

**This week:** The final chapters of *Understanding the Jewish Roots of Christianity* ask the practical question: if Jesus, the apostles and the New Testament were thoroughly Jewish, what difference does that make for us?[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Marty and Brent weigh two views of the Jewish-Christian schism: one that sees the separation as divinely ordained and irreversible, and one (the view BEMA takes) that sees it as a tragic result of human sin that God never intended.[^3] Drawing on Mark Kinzer, they describe three groups that emerged in the second century rather than two, and they consider Kinzer's claim that the church needs Jews who live visibly as Jews in order to keep its Jewish character.[^4]

The conversation then turns practical. The hosts distinguish cultural appropriation from participation (using the Passover Seder as their example), walk through Archbishop Foley Beach's six implications of Jesus's Jewishness, and look at Gerald R. McDermott's four words that need reconsidering: Christ, Jews, Law and Kingdom.[^5] It is a hopeful close to the series, with honest attention to how much history Christians carry into any conversation with Jewish neighbors.

### A Tragic Schism and Three Characters <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#a-tragic-schism-and-three-characters}

*The episode argues that the separation of Jews and Gentiles in the church was a human tragedy rather than God's design.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Romans 11:17–24 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes summarize Paul's image this way: the church is branches on a tree supported by Jewish roots, "not a separate tree growing from different seeds."[^6] As you listen to the passage, what does Paul say supports what, and what attitude does he warn the grafted-in branches against? Name one phrase that stood out.

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The hosts contrast two views of the schism. The first treats the separation as "necessary, providential, and irreversible," and the notes observe that it is popular partly because it "poses no threat to current identities." The second treats it as a tragic break caused by human sin, and quotes John Howard Yoder: "we should not read history as if the outcome that happened had to happen."[^3] What is at stake in choosing between those two views? How does each one shape the way we see the Jewish people today?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Mark Kinzer names three groups in the second century: the wider Jewish community, Jewish members of the ecclesia who followed Jesus while keeping their Jewish identity, and the emerging Gentile church.[^4] What changes in the story when there are three characters instead of two? Why might the middle group matter so much?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Kinzer writes, "To be Jewish, the ecclesia needs Jews, and it needs them to live in a way that accentuates rather than hides their identity as Jews."[^4] The notes say the goal is a church with a Jewish character, not merely Jewish roots.[^7] What would a congregation have to change, in how it worships, teaches and welcomes, for that sentence to become true? What could you do in your own church to move even slightly in that direction?

</div>

### Participation, Not Appropriation <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#participation-not-appropriation}

*The hosts draw a line between taking Jewish traditions for ourselves and participating in them alongside Jewish people.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes describe appropriation as Gentiles "removing the root and source" of a tradition, such as a group of only Gentiles holding "their" Passover meal, while participation means engaging Jewish practices within a community that includes Jewish presence, or from an intentionally educational posture with proper respect.[^8] Why does the presence of Jewish people make such a difference to the meaning of the same meal?

*Read Luke 22:14–20 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes suggest Jesus's covenant meal might be "another renewal rather than complete replacement," pointing out that Israel's history is full of covenant renewals and that Hebrew has no separate word for "renewed" that differs from "new."[^9] Listening to Luke 22, what in the scene connects Jesus's meal to Israel's story? How does "renewed covenant" sound different from "new covenant" to you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Archbishop Foley Beach lists sharing Jesus with Jewish friends as one implication of Jesus's Jewishness, while the notes admit it has "historically" been "done poorly." The episode also asks Christians to remember that most Jews "have relatives who died in the Holocaust at the hands of people who identified as Christians."[^10] How do you hold those two things together? If you were invited into a conversation about faith with a Jewish friend, what posture would that history ask of you?

</div>

### Four Words and a Debt <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#four-words-and-a-debt}

*McDermott asks Christians to look again at four familiar words through a Jewish lens.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** McDermott's four words are Christ (Jesus as the Jewish Messiah, not an abstract one), Jews (in John, *Ioudaios* often refers to Judeans or a Judean worldview rather than all Jews), Law (Torah in light of "the Messiah's Torah"), and Kingdom (not "boring Enlightenment-style sameness" but a "beautiful divine diversity" of Israel and the nations).[^11] Which of the four words would change the most for you if you read it this way, and why?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The first of Beach's implications is that "it would be incongruent for followers of a Jewish Messiah to be antisemitic."[^12] Where might subtle forms of contempt still show up in ordinary Christian speech or teaching, even among people who would never call themselves antisemitic?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** Beach's last implication is to acknowledge the debt Christians owe the Jewish people, who bore "the burden and heat of the day."[^12] What is one concrete way you could honor that debt in the coming month?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> This week closed our time with *Understanding the Jewish Roots of Christianity*. We heard that the split between Jews and Gentiles in the church was a tragedy, not God's design, and that the church was meant to have a Jewish character, not just Jewish roots. We are branches grafted onto a tree we did not plant. That calls for humility in how we borrow Jewish practices, honesty about the history we carry, and gratitude toward a people who bore the burden and heat of the day.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one thing about how you read the Bible or speak about the Jewish people that this series has changed or confirmed for you?

</div>
