### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** The Four Pillars series closed with Jacob at the Jabbok, where Reed Dent described "soul wrestling" with God's presence and our own identity: "You came in looking for better answers but ended up leaving a better person."[^1]

**This week:** Brent and Elle talk with counselor and minister Dieula Previlon about her book on healing from trauma, and about how the church can become a place where hurting people are received.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Dieula Previlon is the founder and executive director of Elevate Her International Ministries, a counselor and an ordained minister. The conversation explores why hurting people often see the church as the last place they can be honest, how to recognize trauma ("too much, too fast, too soon") and why emotional safety must come before anyone shares their story. Dieula describes lament as an act of faith and trust, and the hosts discuss the role of the body in spiritual practice, with Miriam's song after the sea as a biblical example. She also introduces Sankofa, a West African practice of going back to retrieve what is at risk of being lost, as a model for the church to remember its history honestly.

Dieula talks about guarding herself against secondary trauma, training Haitian leaders, and how ordinary pastors and church members can become trauma-informed. A closing segment addresses the revocation of Temporary Protected Status for Haitian communities and what she calls "holy trickery" in Ruth and Esther. In keeping with the episode itself, every question tonight is an invitation. You never need to share your own story, and passing is always welcome.

### A Church That Can Receive Hurting People <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#a-church-that-can-receive-hurting-people}

*Dieula names why the church often struggles with trauma and what safety requires.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Dieula says many congregations quietly signal that healed, put-together people are the norm. She names common responses that make it worse: pat answers like "all things work together for good" used too early, and advice from elders such as "just give it to God" or "I got over it, so should you." The ideal she points to is the church as a *hospital*, a place that receives people "with all their messy stories."[^3] What other phrases or habits have you seen close down honest grief, and why do you think they come so quickly to us?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** Dieula describes trauma as "too much, too fast, too soon," experiences that overwhelm a person's ability to cope. She warns against pressing people to tell their story in shallow small groups, where a wound can be opened with no one checking in the next day. Healing, she says, requires safety, time and a loving presence that says, "I see you, I hear you, and I'm not going anywhere." Elle adds that "mandatory share-and-cry circles" assume a level of trust that hasn't been earned.[^4] What would safety look like in a group like ours? Name one practice that would help.

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> When her twin sons told her they had experienced generational trauma, Dieula's first instinct was defensive: "I'm a therapist. Have you read my book?" Instead she held the tension, stayed quiet, listened, asked questions and created space. Her core questions for anyone who wants to help are whether we are willing to slow down, truly listen, respond reflectively and "not be a jerk" to people who are hurting.[^5] When someone tells you that you or your community hurt them, what helps you stay curious rather than defensive? What makes it hard?

</div>

### Lament as an Act of Faith <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#lament-as-an-act-of-faith}

*Dieula and Elle describe lament not as a lack of faith but as one of its deepest expressions.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Psalm 13 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** What movements do you notice in this psalm? Listen for what the psalmist asks God, what the psalmist requests and where the tone shifts. The notes describe a biblical pattern of lament: complaint, request and (eventually) trust, and they caution, "Don't force a resolution."[^6] Where do you hear each part, and how long does the psalm stay in the hard place before it moves?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Dieula defines lament as "the ability to sit in theological tension" without dismissing the trauma, defending God or jumping to easy answers. After her brother's death, a friend told her it was okay to be angry "but don't take it too far." Dieula was "just getting started." She asks, "Why are some questions off limits? What kind of relationship is this?" Elle frames lament as trust in God's goodness *and* in a relationship strong enough to survive our anger.[^7] Our traditions differ in how much lament shows up in worship and prayer. Without ranking them, what has shaped your own sense of what you are allowed to say to God?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Elle would never tell her grieving five-year-old to sort out his feelings alone and come back once he could say thank you; she would scoop him up right away. Dieula sums this up as "connect first, lesson later," a pattern she sees in how God treats her.[^8] Where have you experienced comfort coming before the lesson, from a person or from God?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** After several losses in a row, some of Dieula's relatives wanted to "be done with God," while others over-spiritualized the loss. Dieula modeled a third way, praying, "I'm not done with you yet, but you're not getting off the hook."[^9] Our group is walking through a season of mourning, and each of us carries it differently, or carries other things entirely. You don't need to share anything personal here. What would honest prayer, in your own words, look like for you this week? If it helps, consider writing a few lines of your own lament at home.

</div>

### Bodies and Memory <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#bodies-and-memory}

*The episode invites us to bring our bodies and our history into the work of healing.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Exodus 15:20–21 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** After the Sea of Reeds, Miriam and the Israelites *sing and dance*. Elle notes that singing stimulates the vagus nerve, which helps regulate the nervous system after trauma, and that many church communities treat people as "brains in bottles." Dieula's practice is noticing: what happens in your stomach, chest, shoulders or knees when you hear a song or read a Psalm?[^10] If you tried last week's preparation with Psalm 13, what did you notice? How does your own tradition involve (or not involve) the body in worship?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> *Sankofa*, from the Akan people of Ghana, means "go back and retrieve what is at risk of being lost." From Dieula's book: "Remembering helps us confront the truth of evil. Remembering cautions our souls to never get comfortable with injustice toward anyone anywhere." She applies this to the church's complicity in slavery, segregation and apartheid, and Elle adds that remembering also recovers treasures, such as abolitionists whose activism came out of their faith.[^11] Every congregation has a history with both failures and faithful witnesses. What might a Sankofa look like for your own church: what would need honest confession, and what would be worth retrieving?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Dieula reminds us that hurting people are not looking for quick answers. They are looking for safety, time and someone who will say, "I see you, I hear you, and I'm not going anywhere." Lament turns out to be one of the deepest forms of trust, because it assumes God is good enough, and the relationship strong enough, to hold our anger and questions. Like Miriam dancing after the sea, we are invited to bring our bodies and our memories into healing. And her message is encouraging: you don't need to be a therapist to do this work, only willing to slow down, listen and be present.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room (passing is welcome): which practice from tonight would you like to try this week, such as writing a lament, taking a slow walk, noticing your body while reading a psalm, or asking someone, "Can you tell me more about what that was like?"

</div>
