### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Brent, Elle and Josh slowed down on Isaiah 6, where Isaiah confesses he lives among "a people of unclean lips" and an already-burning coal is enough to cleanse him.[^1]

**This week:** Marty and Brent move into Isaiah 7–12, where King Ahaz faces an invading alliance and God asks him to trust the story and do nothing.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Aram and the northern kingdom of Israel join forces against Jerusalem, and Ahaz panics. Through Isaiah, God offers a simple but very hard invitation: stand firm in faith and let God act. The hosts read the famous "virgin will conceive" passage (Isaiah 7:14) in its original setting and argue that the sign is mainly a timeline for coming judgment. They set it apart from the openly messianic visions of Isaiah 9 and 11, which they see as partly fulfilled in Hezekiah and more fully in Jesus.[^2]

The episode also deals with fear and conspiracy thinking, the Bible's joining of personal morality and social justice, and how Isaiah keeps hope alive in the middle of judgment. Christians in different traditions read Isaiah 7:14 in different ways, and Marty insists that reading it first in Ahaz's day takes nothing away from Jesus.[^3] Come ready to listen as well as share.

### Ahaz and the Hardest Kind of Faith <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#ahaz-and-the-hardest-kind-of-faith}

*Ahaz's fear was real, and God's request was not to fight better but not to act at all.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 7:1–9 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Rezin of Aram and Pekah of Israel have allied to invade Judah and put a puppet king on the throne. Isaiah brings his son Shear-jashub ("a remnant shall return") and calls the two kings "two smoldering stubs of firewood." The Hebrew of verse 9 ties *believing* (being made firm) to *being established*.[^4] What images does the passage use for Ahaz's fear and for his enemies? Based on the text, what exactly is Isaiah asking Ahaz to do?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The hosts warn against judging biblical kings too easily from a safe distance, since Ahaz faced a genuine military threat. They also note that God was asking him *not* to act: no defensive alliance, no battle, just trust.[^5] Why might doing nothing be harder than fighting, for a leader or for any of us?

*Read Isaiah 7:10–13 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** God invites Ahaz to ask for a sign "in the deepest depths or in the highest heights," which recalls Gideon's fleece. Ahaz refuses: "I will not put the Lord to the test." Marty argues this is avoidance, not humility. Israel in the wilderness *tested* God by demanding proof before obeying; here God *offers* proof to help Ahaz trust. Ahaz probably suspects God will come through, and then he would have no excuse.[^6] The notes suggest people do the same today, avoiding prayer, Scripture or wise advice because they suspect what it will tell them. Where are you tempted to avoid an answer because you think you already know what it would ask of you?

</div>

### A Young Woman, a Timeline and a Child to Come <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#a-young-woman-a-timeline-and-a-child-to-come}

*The hosts read the Immanuel sign in Ahaz's day first, then trace how the openly messianic hope of chapters 9 and 11 reaches toward Hezekiah and Jesus.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 7:14–16 and Isaiah 8:3–4 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The hosts note that the Immanuel sign is mainly about *timing*: before a child conceived now is old enough to choose right from wrong, the lands of the two feared kings will be laid waste. Two paragraphs later, Isaiah's wife ("the prophetess") bears Maher-shalal-hash-baz ("quick to the plunder, swift to the spoil") with the same "before the boy knows…" timeline.[^7] Compare the two passages. What phrases repeat, and what do you notice about how each child's name functions as a message?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The hosts call Isaiah 9 and 11 messianic in Isaiah's own setting: they look for one faithful king who could bring *shalom*, judging the poor with justice. They describe a layered fulfillment: partial in Hezekiah, whose reforms reached from Dan to Beersheba; incomplete, because those reforms didn't last; and fuller in Jesus, as if the Gospel writers were saying, "Remember Hezekiah? … Here is an even better obedience and a fuller kingdom."[^8] How does that layered picture help you hear "To us a child is born" (9:6)?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Robert Alter notes that the Hebrew *almah* in 7:14 means "young woman," not technically "virgin." The Septuagint used *parthenos*, a word for virgin, and that is the version Matthew quotes. Marty concludes the young woman was most likely Isaiah's wife, and stresses that this takes nothing away from Jesus: the Gospels make their own connections for their own reasons, but readers who jump straight to Christmas miss what God was saying to Ahaz.[^9] Traditions in this room may hold this differently, and that is welcome here. What do you gain by reading 7:14 first in Ahaz's setting, and what, if anything, feels at stake for you? How might it shape the way you hear these passages this Advent?

</div>

### Fear, Cynicism and Songs of Salvation <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#fear-cynicism-and-songs-of-salvation}

*Isaiah warns against fear-driven thinking and keeps returning to hope.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Isaiah 8:12–13 warns, "Do not call conspiracy everything this people calls a conspiracy; do not fear what they fear." Marty applies this pointedly to evangelical culture, which he says has become known for believing almost any conspiracy theory, usually driven by social and political fear.[^10] Whatever your own background, what fears tend to circulate in your circles, and what helps you ask whether you are fearing what the crowd fears or fearing God?

*Read Isaiah 7:4 and Isaiah 12:2 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Isaiah 12 uses the same trust-and-don't-fear language God offered Ahaz in chapter 7. Marty admits he is often tempted toward cynicism about the church, and says Isaiah reminds him: "God will win. Shalom is where this is all headed… God is putting the world back together. God is asking us to join him."[^11] Where are you most tempted toward cynicism right now? What would it look like to name the brokenness honestly and then deliberately remind yourself where God's story is going?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Sometimes the hardest kind of faith is doing nothing. Surrounded by two hostile kings, Ahaz hears God say: keep calm, trust the story and "let the man cook." The Immanuel sign, read in its setting, was a timeline of coming judgment, and the openly messianic hope of Isaiah 9 and 11 pictures a faithful king whose trust could bring *shalom*, partly realized in Hezekiah and more fully in Jesus. Isaiah warns against fear-driven thinking, joins personal morality and social justice, and stands with his people rather than condemning them from outside. Judgment is real, but the story is headed toward a world where the wolf lies down with the lamb and God's people draw water joyfully from the wells of salvation.[^12]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: where do you most need to step back and let God work this week?

</div>
