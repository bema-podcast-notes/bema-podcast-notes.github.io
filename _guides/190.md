### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** The Bible's final vision was not of God's people escaping earth but of God's dwelling coming down to be among them. The city of twelves had room for everyone, the tree of life stood open again, and the end turned out to be a new beginning.

**This week:** This is the final week of Session 4. The capstone episode steps back to walk through the whole biblical story, and we will use it to look back over our year in the first-century church, from Acts to Revelation, and to talk about what comes next for our group.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The capstone gives a sweeping overview of all four sessions of the BEMA journey, from the preface in Genesis 1–11 through Revelation.[^1] It frames the whole story as a Tale of Two Kingdoms: Empire, which uses force, coercion and self-preservation, and Shalom, which works through invitation, restoration and other-centeredness.[^2] It returns to Deuteronomy's command to remember being slaves in Egypt, which creates a "beautiful endless cycle" of caring for the alien, the orphan and the widow.[^3] And it urges listeners toward a working familiarity with the whole story rather than a "hopscotch awareness" of favorite passages.[^4]

For Session 4 in particular, the episode describes the New Testament as "Text and context": the living Text of Jesus Christ applied to one unique situation after another.[^5] Tonight's discussion walks back through that session, letter by letter, and then gives the closing extra time to ask how the year has changed the way we read the Bible and what our group should do next.

### One Story, Two Kingdoms <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#one-story-two-kingdoms}

*The capstone steps back from Session 4 to tell the whole Bible as one story.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Deuteronomy 24:17–22 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Look at the reasons this passage gives for its commands. What does Israel keep being told to remember? The capstone notes describe a cycle that grows out of Deuteronomy's command: remember where you came from, recognize that you were the alien, orphan and widow, notice and care for the alien, orphan and widow in your midst, and let that care remind you where you came from.[^6] How does this cycle work in the passage you just read?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes say biblical literacy means more than knowing favorite passages; it means being able to answer "who were God's people and what were they doing at this point?"[^7] They describe the story from Exodus to Revelation as a conflict between Empire, power through "force, coercion, the stick, imposing will, self-preservation," and Shalom, power through "invitation, word, voice, restoration, other-centeredness, putting things back together."[^8] How has learning to ask "who were God's people and what were they doing?" changed the way you read a passage, compared with how you read it before BEMA?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The capstone reads the repeating pattern in Judges as a redemption cycle rather than a sin cycle, choosing to emphasize "God's faithful response rather than human failure."[^9] Where in your own story would it help to see a redemption cycle instead of a sin cycle?

</div>

### Text and Context: Session 4 in Review <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#text-and-context-session-4-in-review}

*Session 4 followed the early church as it applied the living Text of Jesus to one city and community after another.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Revelation 21:3–5 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Back at the start of the session, the hosts called the New Testament letters "inspired midrash": authoritative applications of the same story to different contexts.[^10] Here is a short walk back through the movements of Session 4:

- **Acts:** the epilogue, in which the Hebrew Scriptures culminate in Jesus and find resolution in the empowered community of the early church.[^11]
- **Galatians:** Paul never questioned whether Jews should keep Torah; the question was whether Gentiles had to convert to Judaism, and both were "set free" in different ways.[^12]
- **Romans:** a pastoral letter to a blended family of Jewish and Gentile believers, where no group could claim superiority because all fall short of their own "measuring sticks."[^13]
- **Paul's other letters:** Corinth's broken body, healed by love;[^14] Ephesians' radical family membership, with Gentiles as full members of God's household, "not merely guests or second-class citizens";[^15] and joy, parousia and "faith works" in the shorter letters.[^16]
- **Hebrews and the general letters:** Jesus offering a "better version" of everything in the Levitical system without replacing Jewish faith, with perseverance as the dominant theme;[^17] James's call to *mitzvoth*, deeds or works of obedience, which does not contradict Paul's teaching on justification by faith.[^18]
- **Revelation:** "It is worth it. You have to overcome because we know how this story ends."[^19]

Which of these movements most changed the way you read the New Testament, and what had you assumed about that book before?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The capstone notes show the same gospel applied differently in Thessalonica, Corinth, Colossae and Rome, and conclude that "faithfulness isn't one-size-fits-all but requires wisdom to apply truth appropriately to specific situations."[^20] The traditions in this room have different, carefully reasoned ways of moving from what a letter said to its first readers to what the church practices today, in areas such as baptism, the Lord's Supper and the roles of women. Without trying to settle those questions, how does your tradition usually make that move, and where has Session 4's "Text and context" approach confirmed it, stretched it, or raised new questions?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> If Paul or John wrote a letter to our town or our congregation this year, what context would it need to address, and what might the living Text of Jesus say to it?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">35 min</span> {#closing}

> Session 4 began with Acts, the epilogue, where the story of Scripture finds resolution in an empowered community of Jesus followers. In Galatians and Romans we watched Paul insist that Gentiles were invited in as Gentiles, and that no group in God's blended family stands above another. Through Corinth, Ephesus, Philippi, Colossae and Thessalonica we saw the same gospel applied wisely to very different cities, as "inspired midrash." Hebrews and the general letters called a scattered and pressured church to persevere, to love and to obey. Revelation ended the journey with a word for people under the shadow of empire: it is worth it, because we know how the story ends. The end turned out to be a new beginning, and the same invitation now passes to us.
{: .guide-summary}

*Sit together in silence for a full 90 seconds before anyone answers the next question.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The capstone hopes listeners will carry a working familiarity with the whole story into their lives, so that the ancient story continues to speak "with relevance and power today."[^21] Looking back over this session, how has it changed the way you read the Bible, the way you understand it, or the way you relate to it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Where in your life this year have you seen the difference between Empire's power and Shalom's power, between forcing and inviting, and which one is God inviting you toward as this session ends?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room, with no comments until everyone has spoken: complete the sentence "Because I know how this story ends, I am sent this week to ______."

*To close, read Exodus 19:4–6 aloud together as a commissioning. The capstone names this calling, "You will be for me a kingdom of priests and a holy nation," as the one that shapes everything that follows.[^22]*

</div>
