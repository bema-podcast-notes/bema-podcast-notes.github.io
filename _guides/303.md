### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Jesus restored Peter with three questions that answered his three denials, met Peter's honest and limited love with grace, and sent him to feed and shepherd others.[^1]

**This week:** Having reached the end of John's Gospel, Marty Solomon and Brent Billings step back to look at how scholars have read John over two thousand years and at the distinctive way John tells his story.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Drawing mainly on Dr. Gary M. Burge's *Interpreting the Gospel of John*, the episode traces a long arc. For about 1,500 years John was the Gospel of highest honor; Enlightenment criticism then dismissed it as late, Greek, and Gnostic; in 1924 the Jewish scholar Israel Abrahams called it "the most Jewish of the four gospels"; and in 1957 J.A.T. Robinson's "new look" challenged five long-held assumptions about it.[^3] Modern scholarship now sees John drawing on several independent sources and reads its harsh passages as internal Jewish critique of specific leaders.[^4]

The episode also introduces John's style: misunderstandings, ironies, asides to the reader, hierarchies of meaning, and hidden glimpses of Christ. It models reading the text directly first and then using scholarship to deepen rather than diminish faith.[^5] The notes on this episode are brief, so this guide keeps to these two movements.

### Two Thousand Years of Reading John <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#two-thousand-years-of-reading-john}

*The episode shows that how we read John has changed dramatically, and that the changes matter.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes trace four stages: about 1,500 years when John was "the gospel of highest honor"; the Enlightenment, when John was dismissed as a "late, Greek, Gnostic composition"; 1924, when Israel Abrahams called it "the most Jewish of the four gospels"; and 1957, when J.A.T. Robinson challenged five assumptions, including John's dependence on the other Gospels, its Greek and Gnostic background, and its reliability as a historical witness.[^3] Which of these shifts surprised you most, and why?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Modern scholarship suggests John drew on multiple independent sources: eyewitness memories (if written by the Apostle John), early story collections from the Christian community, Hellenistic narrative traditions, possibly Mark, and testimonies from other witnesses.[^4] How does that picture compare with what you were taught about how the Gospels were written, and what questions does it raise for you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes say recognizing John's Jewish character "transforms interpretation of seemingly anti-Jewish passages." What looks like hostile rhetoric is "internal Jewish critique of specific leadership," like the prophets criticizing corrupt authorities while affirming Jewish identity.[^6] We saw something similar when Marty and Brent suggested hearing *Ioudaios* as "Judeans."[^7] How have passages in John that seem harsh toward Jewish people been read in the churches you have known, and how might this reading change the way you speak about them with others, including Jewish neighbors?

</div>

### Reading the Way John Writes <span class="guide-time">35 min</span> {#reading-the-way-john-writes}

*John is described as a storyteller who layers meaning and coaches readers toward belief.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read John 6:1–14 and John 6:35 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes use John 6 as an example of "hierarchies of meaning": the physical feeding of 5,000, then Jesus as spiritual bread, then Eucharistic symbolism, and finally Jesus as the source of eternal life.[^8] Looking at the passage, where do you see clues that John wants us to look beyond the basic level? Point to a specific word or detail.

*Read John 3:1–10 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes list John's literary motifs: misunderstandings (seven instances where characters misread Jesus), ironies (twelve examples where deeper reality contradicts the surface), asides (parenthetical coaching comments to readers), hierarchies of meaning, and hidden glimpses of Christ.[^9] Returning to the Nicodemus conversation we studied a few weeks ago, where do you see one of these motifs at work, and what is it doing to you as a reader?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes also mention John's distinctive phrasing, such as the double "amen" ("truly, truly I say to you"), addressing audiences as "children," and frequent transitions with "therefore" and "thus."[^10] Why might learning a writer's habits help us read him more carefully?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The episode models a way of reading: "First engaging the text directly and personally," then applying scholarship, holding both historical grounding and spiritual openness, and "Using criticism to deepen rather than diminish faith."[^11] Looking back on our weeks in John, what is one way you want to read Scripture differently because of what we have learned?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> John has been honored, doubted, and rediscovered. For centuries it was the church's favorite Gospel; then scholars called it late and Greek; then a Jewish scholar called it the most Jewish of them all. Today John is read as a skilled storyteller who layers meaning, uses misunderstanding and irony, and coaches readers toward belief. The episode invites us to meet the text first ourselves, then let careful study deepen what we find.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one thing about the Gospel of John you see differently now than when we began?

</div>
