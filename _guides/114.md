### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** The treasure and the pearl opened in two directions: a call to dig for the Kingdom like hidden treasure, and a picture (through Ezekiel 16) of God finding his people in a field and giving everything to acquire them.

**This week:** The last parable of Matthew 13, the net, pulls the whole discourse together and asks what our task is if the sorting belongs to someone else.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The parable of the net closely matches the parable of the weeds: good and bad stay together until the end, and angels, not the fishermen, do the separating. Drawing on Jewish interpretive tradition and on Ezekiel 47, the episode reads the fish as the Gentile nations, which turns the parable into a story about God's mission to bless all peoples.

The hosts then follow the "blazing furnace" back into the Hebrew Scriptures: Daniel's friends who refused to bow, Sodom's failure of hospitality set against Abraham and Sarah's welcome, and an ancient midrash (a traditional Jewish interpretive story) about Abraham in a furnace. The episode ends with Jesus' picture of a teacher who becomes a disciple and brings out treasures both new and old. One point touches on how traditions picture the end of the age, so we will treat it as something to explore rather than settle.

### All Kinds of Fish <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#all-kinds-of-fish}

*A net catches everything, and someone other than the fishermen does the sorting.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 13:47–50 aloud, then read Matthew 13:24–30 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes observe that the net catches "all kinds" of fish, yet the sorting has only two categories; that the angels perform the sorting, not the fishermen; and that the pattern mirrors the weeds, where separation waits for the harvest.[^1] Put the two parables side by side. What details do they share? What is the fishermen's job, and what is not their job?

*Read Ezekiel 47:6–12 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** In Jewish interpretive tradition, the episode explains, fish represent the Gentile nations. Rabbi Akiva taught that there were 153 kinds of fish, matching the 153 pagan nations known in his day, and Ezekiel 47 (where fresh water from the temple fills the sea with fish of many kinds and fishermen spread their nets along the shore) is the key hint behind the parable.[^2] What do you notice in Ezekiel 47 that echoes the net parable? If the fish are the nations, what would Jesus' disciples have heard in this story?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes point out that in both the weeds and the net, the bad are removed while the good remain, and they contrast this with "rapture theology," in which believers are taken away.[^3] Our traditions have pictured the end of the age in different ways. How were you taught to picture it, and what in Matthew 13:30 and 13:48–49 fits that picture or strains against it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The episode applies the parable to the church's habit of deciding who is "in" and who is "out," and offers this instead: "Focus on loving and serving all people rather than judging their spiritual status. Trust God with the ultimate outcome."[^4] Where do you notice yourself pre-sorting people? What would casting a wider net look like for you this week?

</div>

### The Furnace and the Mission <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-furnace-and-the-mission}

*The "blazing furnace" reaches back to stories about perseverance and about welcoming strangers.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 18:1–8 aloud, then read Genesis 19:24–28 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes say the first biblical use of "furnace" comes in the destruction of Sodom and Gomorrah. That story begins with Abraham and Sarah welcoming strangers (even making bread, which ties back to the yeast parable), while Sodom's sin was inhospitality and abuse of strangers.[^5] What do you notice about the way Abraham and Sarah receive their guests? What does it suggest that the furnace image is rooted in a story about how outsiders are treated?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Two more furnace stories stand behind the parable. In Daniel 3, Hananiah, Mishael and Azariah (Shadrach, Meshach and Abednego) refuse to bow to an idol. In an ancient midrash, Abraham is thrown into Nimrod's furnace for refusing to worship fire, while his brother Haran, who wanted to "hedge his bets" and see who won before choosing a side, perishes.[^6] What does the figure of Haran add to the picture? Where do you recognize the pull to hedge your bets?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The episode's *drash* (application) is that Jesus calls his followers not to judge between "good fish" and "bad fish" but to bless all nations, and that a people who sort instead of bless risk Sodom's judgment. The notes apply this to "radical hospitality to outsiders, strangers, immigrants, and those different from us."[^7] Who are the strangers near your door right now? What would Abraham-and-Sarah hospitality toward them look like this month?

</div>

### New Treasures and Old <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#new-treasures-and-old}

*Jesus ends the discourse with a picture of the teacher who becomes a learner.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 13:51–52 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes suggest a possible hint in Song of Songs 7:13, where the bride speaks of delicacies "both new and old" stored up for her beloved.[^8] Read that verse aloud. What does it add to Jesus' image that the treasures are stored up for someone loved?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The episode says teachers must become disciples, that those who "think they know" must be willing to "un-know" and relearn, and that old and new treasures both have value.[^9] Over these weeks of study, what is one old treasure you are keeping, and one new treasure you have picked up? Where might you need to "un-know" something in order to keep learning?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Matthew 13 ends with a net full of every kind of fish, a picture of all the nations. The sorting belongs to God's angels at the end of the age, which frees God's people for their real task: blessing everyone the net catches. The furnace stories remind us what is at stake. Sodom failed the stranger, while Abraham welcomed the stranger and stayed faithful under pressure. And the one who understands the Kingdom becomes a learner again, bringing out treasures old and new.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: who is one person you will try to bless this week without first deciding whether they deserve it?

</div>
