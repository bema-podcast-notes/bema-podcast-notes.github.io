### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** In the throne room, every piece of Caesar's arrival ceremony was handed back to the true King. The one worthy to open the scroll was the Lion of Judah, who appeared as a slain Lamb, and the message was plain: Domitian is not on the throne.

**This week:** The Lamb begins opening the seals. The episode shows that almost nothing here is new: the horses, the cries of the slain and the cosmic upheaval all come from Hebrew Scripture, and they describe the present reality of life under Rome.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode reads the four horses of Revelation 6 as the costs of empire that the first readers lived with every day: conquest, bloodshed, economic hardship and death.[^1] It points out that the "four horses of the apocalypse" first appear in Zechariah 6, and that John builds the chapter from Zechariah, Isaiah, Ezekiel, Jeremiah, Hosea and the Psalms, expecting his audience to recognize the connections.[^2]

The second half turns to the martyrs under the altar, whom the first readers knew by name, and to the 144,000 and the great multitude, which the notes read as the complete people of God with no one missing.[^3] Along the way the hosts deliver one of the series' sharpest lines: "We have mistaken the loss of privilege for persecution."[^4]

### Opening <span class="guide-time">10 min</span> {#opening}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-opening" markdown="1">

**Opening Question:** Think of a time you lost something you had always been able to count on: a routine, a position, a sense of being in the majority, an easy welcome. How did it feel at the time, and what word would you use for it now?

### Four Horses and the Cost of Empire <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#four-horses-and-the-cost-of-empire}

*The famous horsemen were already galloping through Israel's Scriptures long before John saw them.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Zechariah 6:1–8 aloud, then Revelation 6:1–8.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes say the four horses "originated in Zechariah 6, not Revelation," and that John was remixing existing apocalyptic imagery.[^5] Compare the two passages. What did John keep from Zechariah, and what did he change or add?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes trace Rome's ideology of conquest from Julius Caesar's "Veni, vidi, vici" to Augustus's slogan "Piety, war, victory, peace," the claim that true peace comes only through military conquest.[^6] They read the horses as aspects of imperial reality: the white horse as conquest, the red as bloodshed in both war and gladiatorial entertainment, the black as economic exploitation and grain shortage, and the pale horse as death itself.[^7] The grain prices in Revelation 6:6 match archaeological records from Asia Minor.[^8] What do you think John is saying about Rome's promise of "peace" when he describes it with these four riders?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Brent's visit to the Colosseum made the red horse tangible: violence there was not occasional but celebrated in public spectacle.[^9] Where do you see the promise of peace through winning, dominating or overpowering in your own world, and what does it cost the people around it?

</div>

### Under the Altar <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#under-the-altar}

*The fifth seal shows the souls of those who were killed for their faithfulness, and the first readers would have known their faces.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Revelation 6:9–11 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes stress that the souls under the altar were "people they knew by name": brothers, sisters, uncles, cousins.[^10] Read verse 10 again with that in mind. How does knowing that these were the readers' own family members change the way the cry "How long?" sounds?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say the martyrs died for *kiddush HaShem*, "hallowing the Name," following their rabbi Jesus in self-sacrifice, which "stands in stark contrast to protecting privilege or pursuing comfortable lives."[^11] What do you think it meant for these believers to see their deaths as hallowing God's name rather than as simple defeat?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The hosts contrast what the first readers faced, "systemic, premeditated extermination of their fellowship," with modern Western anxieties over things like Starbucks cups, bathroom signs and wedding cakes. Their conclusion: "We have mistaken the loss of privilege for persecution."[^12] People in this room may hear that line differently, depending on what they have experienced and what they care about. Without settling the cultural questions themselves, where do you find this distinction helpful, and where does it make you want to push back? How might it change the way you respond to the losses in your own life?

</div>

### Sealed and Countless <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#sealed-and-countless}

*Before the last seal opens, John pauses to show who belongs to God.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Revelation 7:4 and 7:9–17 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Notice the order: John hears a number, then turns and sees "a great multitude that no one could count." The notes read 144,000 as twelve tribes times twelve thousand, "the entirety of God's people with no one missing," and say the multitude that follows emphasizes "inclusivity and completeness rather than exclusivity or limitation."[^13] What were you taught about the 144,000, if anything, and what happens to the passage when you hear the number and the multitude as two views of the same complete people?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The palm branches in verse 9 connect to Sukkot, the Festival of Tabernacles, and to Zechariah's vision of all nations coming to celebrate it together.[^14] The notes say the first readers understood "the great tribulation" as their current experience, and that the promises of no more hunger, thirst or tears draw on Isaiah 25, 35, 51 and 65.[^15] Read Isaiah 25:6–8. What would it mean to a grieving church to hear these old promises applied to their friends under the altar?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes summarize the number this way: no one missing.[^16] Who in your life most needs to hear that they are not missing from God's people, and how might you tell them this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The horses John saw were Zechariah's horses, and the costs they carried were the costs his readers paid every day under Rome: conquest, bloodshed, hunger and death. Under the altar were people the church knew by name, crying "How long?" And then John sees God's people sealed and complete, with no one missing, a multitude no one could count waving palm branches as at the Festival of Tabernacles. Revelation 6–7 is not a riddle about distant events but encouragement for people who were losing loved ones, and a challenge to those of us who have never had to.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one thing from this week's passage you want to carry for someone who is suffering right now?

</div>
