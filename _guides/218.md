### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We followed Isaac to Beer Lahai Roi and watched him meet Rebekah in a field and find comfort, while the midrash traced his later "bad eye" back to the trauma of Mount Moriah.[^1]

**This week:** We back up to see how Rebekah came to that field. The midrash finds radical hospitality at the well, greed at her family's table, and a young woman who chooses God's purposes with three words: "I will go."[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Genesis 24 is one of the longest chapters in the Bible, and it tells the story twice: once by the narrator and again in the servant's retelling.[^3] The episode focuses on the servant (called Eliezer in the notes) and his prayer for a sign of extraordinary hospitality, answered when Rebekah offers to water ten camels, an estimated 100 to 200 trips to the well.[^4]

At her family's house, the midrash notices that Laban runs out "as soon as he had seen the nose ring," that the gifts go to "her brother and to her mother" but not her father, and concludes that Bethuel died at dinner because of his greed.[^5] The servant gives the valuable gifts directly to Rebekah, the family is asked "Let's call the young woman and ask her about it," and she answers, "I will go."[^6] Marty proposes that Jesus's question "Who are my mother and my brothers?" hints back to this story.[^7]

### The Test at the Well <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-test-at-the-well}

*The servant's prayer looks for character, not just the right family connection.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 24:10–27 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Listen for exactly what the servant asks for and how quickly the answer comes. The notes point out that God answered "before he had even finished praying," and that each thirsty camel "would require approximately 10-20 trips to be fully watered."[^4] What details in the passage show how much Rebekah is taking on? What did you notice about the servant's response afterward?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say the servant understood that finding the right wife "wasn't merely about family connections or beauty, but about character alignment with Abraham's household values," because Abraham's family "was defined by hospitality."[^4] They apply this as "character over credentials."[^8] Where have you seen communities (churches, workplaces, families) choose credentials over character, and what happened?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes describe Rebekah's act as "extensive, sacrificial service for complete strangers," not "offering someone a glass of water."[^9] What would watering ten camels look like in your own setting this month: a kind of hospitality that costs real time and effort for someone who cannot repay it?

</div>

### Dinner at Bethuel's House <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#dinner-at-bethuels-house}

*Read closely, the dinner scene shows a household where the gifts matter more than the guest.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 24:28–33 and 24:50–55 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Listen for who runs, who speaks and who receives what. The notes observe that Rebekah tells "her mother's household," that it is Laban who runs out "as soon as he had seen the nose ring, and the bracelets on his sister's arms," and that the gifts go to "her brother and to her mother" with no mention of the father.[^5] What did you notice about Bethuel's place in the scene?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The midrash concludes that Bethuel's greed "was so extreme that God struck him down during the dinner itself," pointing to his name (which "can mean 'God destroys'"), his absence at the gift-giving and Laban's sudden authority.[^5] Midrash like this fills gaps in the text with a story. How do you weigh a reading like this: what makes it persuasive, and what makes you cautious? What does it add to the passage even if you hold it loosely?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The servant gives "gold and silver jewelry and articles of clothing" directly to Rebekah, and to her brother and mother "costly gifts," which the midrash specifies as fruit.[^10] The notes say this "empowered Rebekah to make her own decision, as the wealth would travel with her regardless of family pressure."[^10] Why might that choice have mattered so much for her freedom?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes say the story "ultimately challenges us to examine our own motivations - are we pursuing God's purposes or merely seeking what we can gain?"[^11] Where do you notice a "nose ring" (something attractive you might gain) quietly shaping how you treat a person or a decision right now?

</div>

### I Will Go <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#i-will-go}

*Rebekah's answer becomes the climax of the chapter, and Marty hears an echo of it in the Gospels.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 24:56–61 aloud, then Matthew 12:46–50.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** In a culture where women "rarely had direct say in marriage arrangements," the question "Let's call the young woman and ask her about it" is itself remarkable, and Rebekah answers immediately: "I will go."[^6] Marty proposes that the pairing "mother and brothers" (without a father) points back to this story, so that Jesus's question "Who are my mother and my brothers?" works as a *remez* ("hint").[^7] What connections do you hear between the two passages?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The hosts deliberately placed this episode after the Jacob study. The notes say understanding Rebekah's character here "reframes her later actions": "She is not a deceiver motivated by favoritism, but someone who understands God's calling and acts accordingly."[^12] How does this change the way you see Rebekah in Genesis 27, which we discussed in Week 8?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes describe the call in both stories as prioritizing God's purposes "even when it creates tension with family expectations," which "doesn't mean rejecting family, but establishing proper priorities when conflicts arise."[^13] Where are you being invited to say your own "I will go," and what makes it hard?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The servant prayed for someone whose character matched Abraham's household, and Rebekah answered with ten camels' worth of hospitality. Her family's table, by contrast, was ruled by the shine of the gifts, and the midrash says Bethuel did not survive the dinner. The servant placed the wealth in Rebekah's own hands so she could choose freely, and she chose God's purposes: "I will go." Jesus, the episode suggests, points back to this story when he asks who his mother and brothers really are.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one "camel" you could water for someone this week?

</div>
