### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** 3 John contrasted Gaius, whose hospitality hallowed God's name, with Diotrephes, who loved to be first and shut the door. Love is what makes truth real, and the faithful are rarely alone.

**This week:** Jude closes the general letters. Like 2 Peter, it confronts teachers who made compromise sound like freedom.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode reads Jude alongside 2 Peter, which it closely resembles. The false teachers are not mainly teaching wrong doctrine but wrong practice: they use God's grace and forgiveness as permission to join in the idolatry and sexual immorality of the Roman guild system, the path to financial security for working people. Jude answers with a pattern of threes. He names two sets of three groups from Israel's story (the golden calf generation, the fallen angels, and Sodom and Gomorrah; then Cain, Balaam, and Korah), and three themes run through them all: idolatry, sexual immorality, and the desire for wealth and influence.

Jude draws heavily on Jewish tradition beyond what became the Bible, quoting 1 Enoch and referring to the Testament of Moses. The episode also observes that Jude is the only biblical text that ties Sodom and Gomorrah directly to sexual immorality, while other Old Testament references stress pride, wealth, and neglect of the poor. Jude ends by calling believers to purity and obedience, respect for God's authority, and love and hospitality.

### Grace Turned into a License <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#grace-turned-into-a-license}

*Jude writes urgently because some inside the community have bent grace into permission.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Jude 3–4 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes say the false teachers were "using God's grace and promise of forgiveness to justify engaging in Roman cultural practices," perverting grace into a "license for immorality."[^1] What do you notice about how Jude describes these teachers and how they got in? What does he say they are doing to grace?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode describes the guild system as "a combination of a workers' union and a college fraternity," providing economic protection and brotherhood but requiring participation in feasts centered on idolatry and sexual immorality. "This was not a sideshow in Roman culture but status quo."[^2] We met the guilds in 2 Peter as well. Why might a teacher who said grace covers all of this have found a ready audience among working believers?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The hosts note that "contemporary culture struggles with Hellenism perhaps more than any time since the Greco-Roman Empire," with pressure to compromise for advancement, acceptance, and financial security. The notes ask, "what compromises are required to 'succeed' in modern culture, and are they worth the spiritual cost?"[^3] Where do you see grace being used, in your own life or in the church, as permission for ongoing compromise rather than power for change?

</div>

### Two Sets of Three <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#two-sets-of-three}

*Jude builds his case from Israel's story, and from what Jewish tradition said about that story.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Jude 5–7 and 11 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The episode lists Jude's two sets of three: the golden calf idolaters, the fallen angels, and Sodom and Gomorrah; then Cain (whose name is linked to "to acquire"), Balaam, and Korah, who rebelled against God's appointed authority. It argues that three themes (idolatry, sexual immorality, and the desire for wealth and influence) "appear in every biblical example Jude cites."[^4] Look at the six examples. Where can you see each of the three themes? Which examples fit the pattern most clearly, and which are harder to see?

*Read Jude 9 and 14–15 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes explain that Jude quotes 1 Enoch directly and refers to the Testament of Moses for the dispute between Michael and the devil over Moses' body. This "raises questions about the use of extra-biblical sources," but shows that biblical authors "drew from the broader Jewish literary context to communicate effectively."[^5] How does it affect you that a book in your Bible quotes a book that is not in it? What questions does that raise about how Scripture came to us?

*Read Ezekiel 16:49–50 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The episode observes that Jude's is "the only biblical reference connecting Sodom and Gomorrah directly to sexual immorality," while the other Old Testament references "link these cities to pride, wealth, and failing to care for the poor." It says Jude makes this connection because of his cultural setting, and that his larger point is still "neglecting the true calling of love and hospitality."[^6] What does Ezekiel name as Sodom's sin? Our traditions have often taught Sodom mainly as a story about sexual sin. How does holding Ezekiel and Jude together widen or challenge what you were taught?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Cain acquiring, Balaam selling his gift, Korah grasping for position: the episode sees the desire for wealth and influence as "the underlying motivation driving the compromise."[^7] Which of Jude's three themes do you find easiest to excuse in yourself? What is one way you could loosen its hold this week?

</div>

### Keep Yourselves in God's Love <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#keep-yourselves-in-gods-love}

*After the warnings, Jude turns to what faithfulness looks like.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Jude 17–25 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes say Jude calls believers to three things, each the opposite of what the false teachers promote: "Purity and Obedience," "Respect for God's Authority" (honoring the teaching of the apostles rather than rebellious teachers), and "Love and Hospitality" (remaining committed to others rather than self-advancement).[^8] Where do you see each of these in the passage? Which verse describes something you want more of?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode describes the false teachers as following "mere natural instincts" rather than living by the Spirit, not listening to the Genesis story about humanity's calling but following "beastly appetites."[^9] What does it look like in everyday life to live from instinct and appetite rather than from your calling as someone made in God's image?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes close with this: "faithfulness matters more than advancement," and "God's grace is not permission for ongoing compromise but empowerment for faithful living."[^10] Think of one decision in front of you this month (at work, with money, in a relationship) where advancement and faithfulness might pull apart. What would choosing faithfulness look like?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Jude, like 2 Peter, writes against teachers who made compromise sound like grace. The pressure was real: the guilds offered security and belonging at the price of idolatry and immorality. Jude answers with Israel's story, told in threes, and with the Jewish tradition his readers knew, showing that the pull toward idols, immorality, and getting ahead is nothing new. His call is just as clear: purity and obedience, respect for God's authority, and love and hospitality toward others. Grace, rightly received, empowers faithful living.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each: where do you most need grace this week to be power for faithfulness rather than permission to compromise?

</div>
