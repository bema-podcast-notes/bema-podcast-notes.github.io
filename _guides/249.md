### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Elle Grover Fricks showed us a God who spins for joy over his people, then asked us to read Lot's wife with a good eye instead of the layers of commentary piled onto her silence.[^1]

**This week:** Michael Burns, a teaching minister in Minneapolis and St. Paul and author of *Escaping the Beast*, asks how people whose first allegiance is to Jesus should engage politics.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Burns names two extremes in Christian political engagement: the culture-war approach that has marked much of evangelicalism for five decades, and a pietistic withdrawal that focuses only on "kingdom matters" while ignoring injustice. He argues both fall short and proposes a third way rooted in allegiance to Jesus as King.[^3] Drawing on scholars including Matthew Bates, he explores the Greek word *pistis* (usually "faith") as carrying the sense of allegiance, so that confessing "Jesus is Lord" was a public, political act in a world that said "Caesar is Lord."[^4]

He reads Romans 12 and 13 together, with Romans 13:8 returning to the vocation of love, and grounds everything in Genesis 1: every person is made in the image of God, and the slide into labeling and "othering" begins with a lie of superiority.[^5] He also warns about "anti-discipling," formation by media and culture that can shape us more than Jesus does.[^6] Politics touches deep loyalties, and people in this room may vote and think differently. The episode's own concern is not which party is right but how Kingdom allegiance shapes everything, so try to listen across differences the way it invites.

### Faith as Allegiance <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#faith-as-allegiance}

*Burns asks what it meant for the first Christians to say "Jesus is Lord."*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Acts 17:5–7 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** What exactly are the believers in Thessalonica accused of? Notice the words the crowd uses and who they say is being defied. The notes connect this charge of "proclaiming another king" to the early baptismal confession "Jesus is Lord," which directly challenged "Caesar is Lord."[^4] What does the accusation tell you about how outsiders heard the gospel?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** Burns explores *pistis* as allegiance and loyalty, which historically meant switching sides from one kingdom to another and making all other allegiances secondary. He links it to *metanoia* ("repentance"), a word he says came from military settings where enemies were called to switch sides.[^4] How does hearing "faith" as "allegiance" change what it means to you to believe in Jesus? What feels clarifying, and what feels uncomfortable?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes put it bluntly: if the early church had only announced a new God who died for your sins so you could go to heaven, "Rome wouldn't have cared." What caught Rome's attention was proclaiming Jesus as Lord, upending economic injustice, blurring status lines, and living as family across social boundaries.[^7] Why might a purely private gospel be easier to live with, for an empire and for us?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** If your first allegiance is to Jesus as King, every other allegiance becomes secondary.[^4] What is one loyalty in your own life (national, political, cultural, even religious) that sometimes competes for first place? What would it look like this week to hold it as genuinely second?

</div>

### Romans 12 and 13 Together <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#romans-12-and-13-together}

*Burns challenges the habit of reading Romans 13 on its own.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Romans 12:9–21 and Romans 13:1–8 aloud, without stopping at the chapter break.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** What do you notice when you hear these as one passage? Make a quick list of what chapter 12 asks of believers, what chapter 13 says about governing authorities, and where verse 8 lands. The notes summarize Burns: chapter 12 is Kingdom vocation, chapter 13 acknowledges governmental authority, and 13:8 returns to the Kingdom calling, "let no debt remain outstanding except to love."[^8]

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Burns frames the Kingdom role as showing an alternative to governmental systems while holding those systems accountable to their calling of justice and peace.[^8] He describes the tensions this creates: standing up for the oppressed while loving enemies, refusing to exercise power over others while advocating for justice.[^9] Which of these tensions do you find hardest to hold? Why?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> In his book, Burns invents a "discovered" manuscript of Peter and Paul arguing over whether Nero or Claudius was the better emperor, then admits he made it up and asks whether we can imagine such an argument. The notes add that we can easily imagine modern disciples having exactly that argument.[^10] What does the exercise expose? Why does it feel so different to imagine Peter and Paul doing it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Marty shares that someone once confronted him about the privilege behind his own pietistic stance that "Kingdom and politics are separate things," pointing out that stepping away did not really affect him.[^11] The traditions in this room have taught different things about church and politics, so treat this as a diagnosis rather than a verdict: what has your tradition taught you about Christians and political engagement, and what has that teaching protected or neglected? Where are you personally being invited to move, toward more engagement or toward less partisanship?

</div>

### Image-Bearers, Not Labels <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#image-bearers-not-labels}

*Burns grounds his whole approach in the first chapter of the Bible.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 1:26–27 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** Burns stresses that humans are made "in" the image of God, not "with" it, so image-bearing is not an add-on. He describes a progression: believe in your group's superiority, make others inferior, "other" them, label them, and turn an "I-you" relationship into an "I-it" relationship (Martin Buber's terms). Once someone becomes "it," violence and oppression become possible.[^12] Where do you see this progression in public life today, on any side? Where do you catch it in yourself?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Burns offers a diagnostic question: can you predict where someone will land on issues, and how they will treat people, more accurately from the news media they watch than from whether they follow Jesus?[^6] What do you think the honest answer is for Christians in our communities, and what is forming you most right now?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q11:** The notes suggest that before assigning a political label to someone, we remember they are made in God's image: "The category we place them in should be 'image-bearer' and 'you' before any political designation."[^13] Think of one person whose politics frustrate you. What is one concrete way you could treat them as "you" rather than "it" this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> This week Michael Burns asked us to hear faith as allegiance: confessing Jesus as Lord meant switching sides, and it made every other loyalty secondary. He pointed to a third way between withdrawing from politics and baptizing a party, a way that reads Romans 12 and 13 together and keeps returning to the one debt we always owe, which is love. He reminded us that every person bears God's image, and that labels quietly turn people into things. And he asked what is actually forming us. The question for Kingdom people in every political situation is less "which party is right?" than "what does love look like here?"
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, name one way you want your allegiance to Jesus to shape how you speak about or treat someone this week.

</div>
