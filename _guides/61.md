### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Joel used a devastating locust plague to picture the Babylonian conquest and called the people to "rend your heart and not your garments." He also marked the turn in the prophets from woes toward hope, promising that God would repay the years the locusts had eaten.

**This week:** We move into exile itself. Ezekiel, whose name means "God strengthens," tells a defeated people that God's presence did not die with the temple but went with them to Babylon.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This episode turns to Ezekiel, a prophet to the people of Judah in Babylonian exile. His name comes from the Hebrew *chazak* ("to strengthen"), and the hosts read his whole message as strength for people asking whether God had forsaken them.[^1] Ezekiel was less an author than a performer, staging what the notes call "guerrilla theater" in public places.[^2]

The episode also introduces apocalyptic literature, which the notes define as using images and symbols to convey hope to the present day rather than mapping out future events.[^3] At the center is a journey: God's glory leaves the destroyed temple, travels east with the exiles to Babylon, and will return west with them when they rebuild.[^4] Our traditions have taught prophecy and the end times in different ways, so expect a good conversation about how we read strange texts.

### Strength for the Exiles <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#strength-for-the-exiles}

*The time for warning has passed, and the exiles need a different kind of word.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Ezekiel 4:1–3 and Ezekiel 5:1–4 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes describe Ezekiel as "not primarily an author but a performer," someone who ran into busy public areas, enacted shocking prophetic messages, and left quickly while the audience processed what they had seen.[^5] In these chapters he builds a model of Jerusalem (the notes suggest something like Legos), cuts his hair with a sword, burns some of it in the model, and chops the model up to picture Jerusalem's destruction.[^6] What do you notice about what Ezekiel does with his props and with his own body? What might an onlooker in a crowded street have felt watching it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes say the exilic period "required a fundamentally different prophetic approach." The time for preventive repentance had passed, and the people faced new questions: "Are we going to be here forever?" "What did we do to deserve this?" "Has God forsaken us?" "Is God even real?"[^7] Ezekiel's name answers them. It comes from *chazak*, the same word found in Joshua's commission, *chazak va'chamatz* ("be strong and courageous").[^8] The notes say this strength worked on two levels: Ezekiel needed strength for his own difficult calling, and his message was meant to strengthen the people.[^9] Why might people already living with consequences need strength more than another warning? What does that suggest about how we speak to someone in that place?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> According to the notes, Ezekiel strengthened the exiles in three ways: partial explanation (Ezekiel 16–17 give reasons for the exile), assurance of God's continued presence, and a vision of future restoration. Hurting people need "both honest acknowledgment of causes and assured hope for the future."[^10] Think of someone you know who is living through a kind of exile right now. Which of those three do they most need, and what would offering it look like this week?

</div>

### Wheels, Eyes, and Present Hope <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#wheels-eyes-and-present-hope}

*Ezekiel's opening vision is strange on purpose, and the strangeness carries a message.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Ezekiel 1:15–21 aloud. If time allows, read all of Ezekiel 1:4–28.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Before interpreting anything, simply report what you hear. The notes point to creatures with four faces looking in four directions, eyes covering the creatures and the wheels, everything moving together in perfect coordination, and an unusual amount of attention to wheels.[^11] Which details stand out to you? Which would you expect in a vision of God's throne, and which surprise you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes explain the symbols. Four is the language of the four corners of the earth, "the most inclusive, expansive language possible," and the eyes mean nothing goes unseen. The wheels are revolutionary because Jewish belief located God's presence in the temple, the temple had been destroyed, and the wheels say that "God is mobile and not confined to a specific location."[^12] If you had grown up believing God lived in one building and then watched that building burn, what would a throne on wheels say to you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes define apocalyptic literature as using "images and symbols to convey hope to their present day." The word means "to uncover" or "to reveal," so these texts are about "peeling back the curtain" on present reality, not providing blueprints for end-times scenarios.[^13] The notes are blunt that Western interpretation "has done significant damage" by treating apocalyptic literature as a detailed roadmap of future events.[^14] Many of us grew up with charts, sermon series, or novels about the end times, and our traditions have handled these texts differently. Without deciding who was right, what changes when you bring the notes' question to a strange passage: "What hope is this symbolic language conveying about present circumstances?"[^15] Where in your own life would you most like the curtain peeled back?

</div>

### The Glory Goes East <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#the-glory-goes-east}

*The vision of wheels prepares us for the episode's central claim: God went into exile with his people.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Ezekiel 10:18–19 and Ezekiel 43:1–5 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes trace a five-step arc: God's presence was in the temple; the people were exiled east to Babylon; God's presence departed and went east with them; the people will return west to rebuild; and God's presence will return west with them.[^16] As you listen to the two passages, what do you notice about direction, about where the glory pauses in chapter 10, and about what the prophet does when it comes back in chapter 43?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes observe a parallel with the Tower of Babel. In both stories people move east and God wants them to return west, but in the exile the movement east is "involuntary punishment rather than willful disobedience."[^17] What does it say about God that his presence follows his people even into a place they were sent because of their own failures?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes put the central revelation simply: "God is not trapped in destroyed buildings or limited to specific geographic locations." Divine presence "is not confined to our structures, traditions, or expectations, but moves with us through every circumstance."[^18] The notes add that believers in spiritual dryness, displacement, or personal crisis still ask the exiles' questions.[^19] Where are you, or someone you love, living east of the temple, so to speak, right now? What would it mean to believe that God's presence went there too?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Ezekiel spoke to people who had lost their land, their temple, and their confidence that God was still with them. He answered with strength, the very meaning of his name. He acted out the destruction they had lived through, and then showed them a throne on wheels, full of eyes, able to go anywhere. The glory of God left the ruined temple and went east into exile with the people, and one day it would come home with them. Their story was not finished, and neither is ours.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, where do you most need to hear "God strengthens" this week?

</div>
