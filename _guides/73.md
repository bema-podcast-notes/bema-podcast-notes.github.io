### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We finished Session 2 with the capstone, which traced the Hebrew Scriptures as one story of Empire and Shalom, from creation through exile and return, and noticed that the Tanakh ends not with resolution but with a question: how should God's people live under foreign empires?

**This week:** Session 3 begins. Before opening the Gospels, Marty and Brent review where the story has been and explain why the centuries between Malachi and Matthew matter so much for understanding Jesus.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This short episode is a threshold. It announces that Session 3 will study the Gospels, using Matthew as a roadmap while looking at all four accounts, and it reviews Sessions 1 and 2: Torah as partnership, the history books and the "redemption cycle," wisdom literature as tools for survival, the prophets' warning, woe and hope, and the remnant's returning, yearning and learning.[^1] Its central claim is that the period traditionally called "the silent years" was not historically silent. Empires rose and fell, Alexander brought Greek culture, the Maccabees revolted, and Rome occupied the land, and all of it shaped the world Jesus entered.[^2]

The episode closes with a prayer from guest Jim Feicht, a BEMA group facilitator in Irvine, inviting listeners into stillness and into experiencing God's love "right where you're at right now."[^3] Because the episode itself is short, this guide gives extra time to reading two texts from the end of the Hebrew Scriptures, the last voices before the long quiet.

### Looking Back: The Story So Far <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#looking-back-the-story-so-far}

*Before the Gospels, the episode pauses to remember the story that leads to them.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The episode reviews Torah as five movements of one partnership: Genesis sets the stage, Exodus chooses the partner, Leviticus defines the partnership, Numbers shapes the partner, and Deuteronomy calls the people to remember.[^4] The notes sum it up this way: "Torah represents a missional call to partnership with God for the sake of the world."[^5] Which of the five movements do you remember most clearly from Session 1, and what is one thing it taught you about God or about God's people?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode describes four wisdom books as tools for surviving the struggle: Psalms for art and song, Proverbs for "Wise sayings that are generally true," Ecclesiastes for the "reason to get out of bed" each morning, and Song of Songs for relationships and intimacy.[^6] Which of these four tools have you leaned on most in a hard season, and which one have you tended to neglect?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** In the review of Session 2, Marty describes Chronicles as showing how God's people became "the very opposite of what they were called to be," the "anti-story" instead of God's story for the world.[^7] Without naming anyone, where have you seen a group of people (a church, an organization, a movement, a family) drift into the opposite of what it was formed to be? What do you think made that drift hard to see from the inside?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes say the redemption cycle "reflects the realistic human experience of trying to walk faithfully with God," with both failure and faithfulness in the same period.[^8] Looking at your own life with God, what part of the cycle do you feel you are in right now, and what has helped you return before?

</div>

### Returning, Yearning, Learning <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#returning-yearning-learning}

*The Hebrew Scriptures end with a remnant back in the land, rebuilding, longing and adjusting to a new world.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Haggai 2:1–9 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes connect Haggai to the "yearning" of the return: "Those who remembered Solomon's temple looked at the new temple and wept - it was a shadow of former glory."[^9] Listen to verse 3 again, and then to verses 4–9. What do you notice about how God speaks to people who are disappointed by what they see? What does God ask of them, and what does God promise?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes call this yearning the "already but not yet" tension that would continue through Jesus's time "and into our own."[^10] What is something in your faith or your community that feels like a smaller version of what you once knew or hoped for? How do you tend to respond to that gap: grief, nostalgia, effort, something else?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode says Malachi and Esther show that "the post-exile world demanded new ways of being faithful" and that the old approaches "wouldn't simply translate to this new reality."[^11] When has a change in your circumstances (a move, a new stage of life, a cultural shift) forced you to find a new way of being faithful? What did you have to let go of, and what did you keep?

</div>

### The Not-So-Silent Years <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#the-not-so-silent-years}

*Session 3 will begin with the four centuries between the last prophet and the first Gospel.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Malachi 4:4–6 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** These are the last words of the prophets in most Christian Bibles, and then the pages turn to Matthew. The notes explain that the following period is called "the silent years" because there were no prophetic voices, but that "this label is profoundly misleading historically."[^12] Read the passage again. What do these closing verses ask the people to remember, and what do they tell them to expect? If you were living in the generations that followed, what would you be waiting for?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** Marty describes learning the history of this period in Israel and Turkey as "one of those pieces that changed everything" for him, and he says readers of Scripture often "don't quite have the handles, where to hang it or where to put it in our understanding."[^13] The notes list what filled those centuries: the rise and fall of empires, Alexander the Great, the spread of Greek culture, the Maccabean revolt and Roman occupation.[^14] What is one Gospel story you have heard many times where you suspect you are missing the "handles"? What questions would you want answered before reading it again?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> When Brent asks for recommended resources, Marty credits his teachers, mentions one resource with a balanced evaluation, and admits that the history is "debated" and "a little tricky."[^15] What helps you trust a teacher who is honest about the limits of what they know? How might that honesty shape the way this group studies together over the coming months?

*Read Psalm 46:10 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q11:** Jim Feicht's prayer closes the episode: "My heart, I would honestly say, is to be still and to know and to experience God in your silent times."[^16] His words seem to echo the verse you just heard. The notes describe the BEMA approach as combining "rigorous intellectual study of historical context with contemplative spiritual practice," adding, "Both are necessary; neither is sufficient alone."[^17] Which comes more naturally to you, study or stillness? What is one small way you could make room for the other during this session?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> We have arrived at the doorway of the Gospels. Behind us is a story of partnership, struggle, warning, woe, hope, and a remnant trying to be faithful in a new world. Ahead of us is Jesus, but the notes remind us that "Jesus didn't arrive in a vacuum." He came to a specific people with specific expectations, fears, hopes and wounds, shaped by four hundred years that were anything but silent. Session 3 asks for patience before rushing to familiar stories, and it invites us to keep hearing the call to "Come back. Come follow me."
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one hope or question you are bringing with you into Session 3?

</div>
