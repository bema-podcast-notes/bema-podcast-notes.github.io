### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Chronicles showed us the story behind the story: David's temple request and Solomon's horses, gold, and forced labor reveal a lust for Empire that comes before the moral failures. When Rehoboam refused to "loosen the yoke," the kingdom split.

**This week:** In the divided north, the prophet Elijah confronts King Ahab, Queen Jezebel, and the prophets of Baal. He wins a spectacular victory on Mount Carmel, and then learns that fire does not change hearts.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The northern kingdom begins with Jeroboam's golden calves, a decision the notes call politically sensible and "spiritually catastrophic." Ahab then marries Jezebel, a priestess of Asherah from Phoenicia, bringing Baal worship into the royal house. Into this world comes Elijah, a prophet with *chutzpah* (audacity) who binds God to his own promises in Torah, sets up a contest on Baal's home court, and calls down fire from heaven.

Yet after the victory no one defends him, and he runs for his life. At Horeb, God is not in the wind, the earthquake, or the fire, but in a gentle whisper, and he sends Elijah to make a disciple. The episode draws a hard lesson for passionate believers: winning the argument is not the same as winning the heart. It also touches on the crude, sexual edge of Elijah's taunts, which the notes explain plainly.

### Elijah's Chutzpah <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#elijahs-chutzpah}

*Elijah starts a drought on his own initiative, holding God to his word.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read 1 Kings 17:1–6 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Listen for the order of events in the first two verses. The notes point out that Jewish tradition teaches God did NOT tell Elijah to announce the drought; the evidence is that "Then the word of the Lord came to Elijah" comes AFTER his declaration.[^1] What do you notice about who speaks first? How does that change your picture of Elijah?

*Read Deuteronomy 11:16–17 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes call this "binding God to His Text," which means "calling God to fulfill promises He wrote in Torah." They add that "Jewish tradition teaches God loves this wrestling, Jacob-like, chutzpah faith and honored it even though He wasn't ready to withhold rain."[^2] How does this picture of prayer compare with how you were taught to pray? What is attractive or uncomfortable about it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> God feeds Elijah through ravens, unclean scavengers that bring him "food stolen from others." The notes see a symbol: when it does not rain, "innocent people suffer," and "Prophets with fire in their belly for truth and justice can pursue rightness 'at all costs' without realizing the innocent bystanders they hurt along the way."[^3] When has your pursuit of something right cost people who were never the target? What did you learn?

</div>

### The Contest on Carmel <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#the-contest-on-carmel}

*Elijah turns a simple message about rain into a showdown.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read 1 Kings 18:20–39 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes describe the contest as "Baal's Home Court Advantage": Mount Carmel was Baal's mountain, the bull was Baal's animal, lightning was Baal's weapon, and rain was Baal's domain.[^4] God's actual instruction was simply to go tell Ahab it would rain.[^5] What details in the passage show Elijah stacking the deck against himself? What does that tell you about his personality?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes explain that "waver" in Elijah's question "means 'sexually dance,' a direct reference to the erotic nature of Baal worship," and that his taunt about Baal being "busy" has crude overtones. The three pourings of water, they suggest, allude to the Sukkot water ceremony "where Israel asked God for rain."[^6] How does knowing the culture behind these words change the tone of the scene for you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The people cry, "The Lord, He is God!" Yet when Jezebel threatens Elijah, no one defends him. The notes conclude that the contest "won the argument but didn't transform the people," and they apply this to apologetics and to Christians who pursue "Winning culture war battles while losing the battle for hearts."[^7] They also say "The church needs both apologetics and discipleship."[^8] Thinking of your own experience rather than anyone else's, when have you seen a spectacular display of truth fail to change hearts? What did change hearts instead?

</div>

### The Whisper at Horeb <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-whisper-at-horeb}

*God answers Elijah's despair with a whisper, a correction, and a new assignment.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read 1 Kings 19:1–18 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Elijah travels "40 days and 40 nights to Horeb (Mount Sinai) - back to where it all began." The notes list the wind, the earthquake, and the fire, and "After the fire: a gentle whisper (still small voice)."[^9] They add that Jewish tradition connects this whisper, called *sod*, to the Song of Songs: "the whisper of two lovers in bed together."[^10] What do you notice about what God does and does not say to Elijah in this scene? What does the whisper teach about how God works?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Elijah says, "I am the only one left." God replies that he has reserved seven thousand who have not bowed to Baal. The notes translate the tone bluntly: "Shut up, Elijah. You're not the only one."[^11] When have you felt like the only faithful one, at work, at church, or in your family? What might God's correction to Elijah say to you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes record an "Early Retirement Theory" in some Jewish tradition: that Elijah's chariot of fire was God retiring him early "because he was so full of chutzpah, so stubborn, so committed to his methods that he wouldn't soften his heart."[^12] What do you make of this reading? What does it suggest about the cost of refusing to learn God's way of working?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** God's new commission is "Go make a disciple." Jewish tradition sees Elijah and Elisha as the beginning of the Rabbi-disciple relationship.[^13] The notes put the challenge simply: "Just love someone well enough, long enough, to show them who God really is."[^14] Who is one person you could love that way in this season? What would "long enough" ask of you?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Elijah had real courage: he held God to his promises, faced down a king, and called fire from heaven. But the fire did not change a single heart, and he ended up alone in a cave, sure he was the last faithful person on earth. God met him in a whisper, corrected him gently, reminded him of seven thousand he could not see, and sent him to make a disciple. Mount Carmel moments may have their place, but hearts change in the patient, intimate work of Horeb. For those of us with fire in our bellies, that is both a warning and a relief.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, where is God inviting you to trade a Carmel method for a Horeb method this week?

</div>
