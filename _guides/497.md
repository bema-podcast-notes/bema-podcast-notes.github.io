### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Session 10 opened in a season of mourning for Josh Bossé. We talked about *shiva*, the practice of simply being present with the grieving, and about trusting "the slow work of God."

**This week:** Brad Gray and Brad Nelson argue that the Lord's Prayer is too familiar to be noticed and too powerful to be set aside. Its first line holds a picture of a God who reigns above everything and still comes near when his children cry out.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Brad Gray and Brad Nelson, of Walking the Text, have spent years on a film, a book (*Bringing Heaven Here*), and a television series about the Lord's Prayer. In this conversation they make the case that the prayer is Jesus' own summary of who God is, why he came, and what his followers are for, and that it was meant to be prayed, not just admired.

The heart of the episode is the opening line, "Our Father in the heavens." The guests explain why "heavens" is plural, what that says about God being both far above us and close at hand, and how praying it can lift our eyes in hard seasons. The episode also touches on the earliest church praying the prayer three times a day, on its echoes of the Jewish Kaddish (a prayer also recited by mourners), and on a moving story about what it means to become part of the prayer's answer.

### Familiar Words, Fresh Eyes <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#familiar-words-fresh-eyes}

*The guests argue that the prayer we know best may be the one we use least.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 6:9–13 aloud, slowly.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Brad Gray says that rereading Matthew 6:9 made him realize Jesus meant the prayer to be prayed regularly, not just admired.[^1] Look at the passage again as if for the first time. What does the prayer actually ask for? Whose needs does it name, and in what order? Report what you notice.

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The guests call the Lord's Prayer "the most powerful thing Jesus ever gave us that is the most underutilized," and they say that familiarity is part of the problem: because we know it so well, we assume we understand it and set it aside.[^2] Our traditions handle this prayer very differently; some pray it every week, others rarely. How was the Lord's Prayer used in the church that formed you, and what did that teach you about it, for good or ill?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The *Didache*, an early church discipleship manual from the late first or early second century, contains the earliest known text of the prayer outside the Bible and tells believers to pray it three times a day, echoing the Jewish rhythm of morning, afternoon, and evening prayer.[^3]

*Read Daniel 6:10 and Psalm 55:17 aloud.*

What would it take for you to try a fixed rhythm like this for a week, and what do you imagine it would change?

</div>

### Our Father in the Heavens <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#our-father-in-the-heavens}

*One plural word opens up a picture of a God who is both above everything and near.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Brad Nelson's biggest surprise was that "heavens" is plural, both in Matthew's Greek and in the Hebrew *shamayim* ("heavens"). In the ancient Israelite picture of the cosmos, God rules from the highest heaven, yet the heavens reach all the way down to where people live. The guests point to scenes where God comes near when his children cry out, including Hagar and Ishmael in the wilderness.[^4]

*Read Genesis 21:14–19 aloud.*

Who cries out in this passage, who hears, and what happens next? What does the scene suggest about how far "the heavens" reach?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes say this picture holds two truths together: transcendence (God reigns above everything and is fully in control) and immanence (God can come near).[^5] Which of the two do you find easier to believe, and which do you need more in this season?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty mentions a chapter connecting the Lord's Prayer to the Kaddish, an ancient Jewish prayer that sanctifies God's name and asks for his kingdom to come; a form of it is recited as the Mourner's Kaddish. Marty read that chapter shortly after his father died and found it especially moving.[^6] What might it mean to you that lines like "hallowed be your name" and "your kingdom come" have long been prayed by people in grief?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Nelson notes that people in crisis become "hyper-focused on what's wrong." Praying "Our Father in the heavens" invites us to "zoom out," much like the shift astronauts describe after seeing Earth from space. The notes say John does something similar in Revelation, repeatedly lifting readers to the heavenly throne room, as if to say: *when things are hard down here, look at them from up there.*[^7] When has a wider view helped you through something hard? When might "zoom out" feel like it comes too soon, and how could we say it to someone with care?

</div>

### Becoming the Answer <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#becoming-the-answer}

*The prayer is something we pray, and also something we are sent to live.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Brad Gray calls the prayer "the greatest distillation in the entire Bible" of who God is, why Jesus came, and what our purpose is. He adds that it "animated his daily life" for Jesus himself.[^8] Of those three (who God is, why Jesus came, our purpose), which does the prayer make clearest for you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** While Nelson was writing, his wife kept asking "so what?" One of her questions stood out: "How can this prayer help me pray for our daughter?" Later, reading the book's picture of God as a Father who comes "crashing in, knocking things over" when his children cry, she realized, "I am one of the ways God does that in the world."[^9] What shifts when the Lord's Prayer moves from words we say to God to a role we play for others?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** When someone who was hurting texted her and skipped an event, Nelson's wife saw herself as "a representative of the Father," called to show up the way God does.[^10] Think of one person in your life who is hurting. What would it look like this week to show up for them as a representative of the Father?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The Lord's Prayer is easy to recite and easy to overlook. Brad Gray and Brad Nelson invite us to hear its first line again: a Father who reigns above every layer of the heavens and still bends down to the ground where his children cry. Praying it lets us see our troubles from the throne room without pretending they are small. And the prayer does not stop with us. The same Father who hears the cry sends his people to answer it.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, which line of the Lord's Prayer will you carry into this week, and where do you hope to pray it?

</div>
