### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We read Matthew 24–25 as Jesus' answer to the disciples' question about the temple's fall. Three parables said the same thing: be ready, expect to give an account, and care for the least, because the priests were not doing it.[^1]

**This week:** We enter the passion narrative itself. The episode reads Matthew 26 and 27 straight through and sets the stage for the next few weeks on Jesus' final days.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This episode takes an unusual approach. Instead of verse-by-verse commentary, it reads Matthew 26 and 27 in their entirety, and Marty speaks candidly about his struggle to treat the crucifixion as "merely an academic exercise."[^3] He describes the story stirring several responses at once: love, solidarity, anger, mystery, and the sense of death itself submitting to something greater.[^4]

The notes also gather background that helps us read: the timing of Passover week, the value of reading all four Gospel accounts together, a brief introduction to the different ways Christians have understood the atonement, and the Roman use of crucifixion as political theater aimed at insurgents.[^5] Our traditions may emphasize different parts of this story, and that variety is welcome here. Because this is sacred ground for many of us, today's guide leaves extra room for quiet and for the heart as well as the head.

### The Shape of the Week <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-shape-of-the-week}

*The calendar and the four Gospel witnesses give the passion its shape.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 26:17–30 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes lay out a likely timeline. Jesus ate the Passover meal with his disciples on Thursday night, a day early because of Sabbath logistics; he was crucified on Friday "while Passover lambs were being sacrificed"; he lay in the tomb on the Sabbath; and he rose on Sunday, the Festival of First Fruits. In Jewish reckoning, any part of a day counts as a day.[^6] As you listen to this meal, what do you notice that ties it to Passover? What does it add to know that the whole week runs on the festival calendar?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The hosts encourage reading all four Gospel accounts of the final week together, because "Different authors include different details" and "The fuller picture emerges from considering all perspectives together."[^7] Their example is the sponge lifted to Jesus on the cross: John mentions a hyssop branch, and the notes suggest this detail reveals a degree of humiliation that Matthew's account alone does not show.[^8] When you have read the Gospels side by side, what has that practice opened up for you, and what has it unsettled?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes say there are several major frameworks for understanding Jesus' death, including the ransom theory (the earliest), *Christus Victor* (Christ's victory over evil powers), and penal substitutionary atonement (the one most familiar to modern evangelicals). Scot McKnight compares these theories to a bag of golf clubs: "each has its place and proper use depending on the situation."[^9] Our traditions have handed us different ways of talking about the cross. Without arguing for one, which "club" did your church hand you most often, and when has another way of seeing the cross helped you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q4:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty models holding questions about why Jesus had to die while "Being comfortable with mystery and not having all answers."[^10] What question about the cross are you still carrying, and what helps you carry it without either forcing an answer or letting go of faith?

</div>

### Theater of Empire <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#theater-of-empire}

*Crucifixion was not only an execution. It was a message.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 27:27–44 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes say crucifixion was reserved almost exclusively for insurgents, terrorists, and zealots, designed to humiliate victims and terrify would-be rebels, with a message to occupied peoples: "This is what happens when you stand up against Rome." The crown of thorns and the "King of the Jews" sign were part of this political theater.[^11] Go back through the passage. List the details that mock Jesus specifically as a king. What do you notice about who joins in?

*Read Matthew 27:45–50 aloud, then read Psalm 22:1 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Jesus cries out "Eli, Eli, lama sabachthani," the opening line of Psalm 22. The notes suggest that in his dying state he was probably gasping and hard to understand, and that bystanders may have heard the word for God and thought he was calling for Elijah. The response was not compassion but more mockery: "Let's see if Elijah comes to save him."[^12] What does it mean to you that, in this moment, Jesus reaches for the words of a psalm of lament?

*Read Matthew 27:57–61 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe Joseph of Arimathea as wealthy and connected to the Sanhedrin, someone whose influence let him approach Pilate and who placed Jesus in his own newly hewn tomb, "a sign of devotion and resources." Pilate did not care what happened to the body, since crucifixion victims were usually tossed into shallow graves.[^13] Joseph used his position on Jesus' behalf when others had fled. What resource, access, or influence do you have that could be offered the same way?

</div>

### Were You There? <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#were-you-there}

*Some texts are meant to transform us, not just inform us.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes list the responses the crucifixion stirs in Marty, all at once: "Profound love," "Divine solidarity" (God joining humanity in sorrow), "Righteous anger" at the injustice, "Mystery," and "Victory over death."[^14] Think back to hearing Matthew 27 read today or reading it this past week. Which of these rose in you most strongly, and which feels hardest for you to hold alongside the others?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** Marty describes a Good Friday service where he found himself alone, "weeping uncontrollably for reasons I still don't really understand," and says the hymn "Were You There When They Crucified My Lord?" still moves him profoundly.[^15] The notes say this shows that "the transformative power of Scripture often works at emotional and spiritual levels beyond mere cognition."[^16] When has a biblical story reached you somewhere deeper than understanding? If that has rarely happened, what do you think gets in the way?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes end by saying the crucifixion narrative "calls for both head and heart - rigorous study and spiritual receptivity, critical thinking and humble wonder."[^17] Our group spends a lot of time on the head. What is one way you could make room for the heart as you read the passion story over the coming week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> This week we read the passion story whole. We saw the shape of the week: a Passover meal, a death at the hour the lambs were sacrificed, a Sabbath in the tomb, and a resurrection on First Fruits. We saw crucifixion as Rome's theater of humiliation, and heard Jesus pray the first line of a psalm of lament while the crowd mocked. And we heard a teacher admit that this story is too sacred to be "merely an academic exercise." It asks for our minds, and it also asks for our hearts.[^18]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what word or image from Matthew 26–27 do you want to carry with you this week?

</div>

It distinguishes the Jewish people, who loved Jesus, from a small, corrupt group of chief priests, explains the difference between the formal and informal Sanhedrin, and traces how Pilate was pressured into the execution. It also names the terrible damage done by the false teaching that "the Jews killed Jesus."[^19]

**What to bring next week:** Read Matthew 26:3–5, Matthew 27:1–2, and John 19:12–16. Write down every person or group the text names as acting against Jesus, and every place it mentions "the people" or "the crowd."
