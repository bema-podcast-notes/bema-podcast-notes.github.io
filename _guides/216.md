### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Genesis ended with Joseph choosing his father's family over a comfortable Egypt, and with a story of dispossessed kings laying down their weapons at Jacob's casket: "If Joseph can make it back, perhaps there's hope for them too."[^1]

**This week:** We step out of Genesis for a guest conversation. Porshe Chiles talks honestly about loving BEMA's Eastern perspective while feeling unseen in the biblical story as it is usually taught, and Marty and Brent listen.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Porshe Chiles, Associate Director of Global Research at Wake Forest University, describes how BEMA's Eastern perspective decentered the US lens through which she had always heard the Gospel.[^2] She also names the pain of not seeing people like herself in the narrative, even though the biblical world was centered in the Middle East and North Africa and people of African descent appear throughout it.[^3] Marty acknowledges specific missed opportunities in BEMA's own teaching: Hagar, Zipporah, the Ethiopian eunuch, the Egyptians who left with Israel and the many peoples at Pentecost.[^4]

The conversation also takes on the harmful "curse of Ham" teaching, reframes Babel as God's design for learning empathy across difference, and asks why the diversity of Revelation 7:9 should wait for heaven.[^5] Porshe shares how her own family history makes it impossible for her to read Scripture apart from current and past events.[^6]

*A note for our group:* this conversation touches race and personal history. Speak from your own experience, listen without rushing to explain, and share only what you choose to share.

### The God Who Sees <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#the-god-who-sees}

*The conversation begins with a listener's honest feedback and a host's willingness to receive it.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes say BEMA's Eastern perspective gave Porshe "scales-off-the-eyes moments," helping her see the Bible in "enlightened geographical ways and enlightened cultural ways," while also naming that she has felt unseen in the story.[^2] The notes say Marty "acknowledges missed opportunities and apologizes."[^7] What do you notice about how this conversation was held? What makes honest feedback like this possible between people?

*Read Genesis 16:7–14 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** Listen for who Hagar is, where she is, and what she calls God. The notes highlight that Hagar is "an Egyptian servant who encounters God and declares Him 'the God who sees me,'" and that "Egypt is in Africa, yet Western teaching often disconnects it from the African continent."[^4][^3] What details in this passage stand out to you when you read it with that in mind?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes list other figures the conversation names: Zipporah, whose father Jethro "according to Midrash, came from deep Egypt"; the Egyptians who left with Israel ("Their stories matter"); and the Jewish people from many backgrounds at Pentecost.[^4] Which of these would you most like to study more closely, and why?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Hagar's story "speaks powerfully to anyone who has felt unseen, discarded, or marginalized."[^8] Think about the stories and voices you usually hear in Bible teaching. Whose stories have you rarely heard told, and what would it take for you to go looking for them?

</div>

### What Can Stand in the Way? <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#what-can-stand-in-the-way}

*The Ethiopian official's question becomes a question about belonging, both in Acts and in this conversation.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Acts 8:26–39 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes describe the Ethiopian official as asking, "What can stand in the way of my being baptized?" and say his question "was fundamentally about belonging: 'Is this story for me or just for Jews?'"[^4] Philip's answer connects to a passage in Isaiah promising eunuchs and foreigners "a name better than sons and daughters."[^9] What do you notice in the passage about what the official is reading, what he asks, and how Philip responds?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty addresses the "curse of Ham" teaching, which "falsely taught black skin was a curse." In the episode's reading, the biblical and Jewish conversation points the other way: these people "were mistreated because they looked different from the majority," and the lesson is "solidarity with the oppressed."[^5] Why do you think a misreading like this could take hold and last so long? What does it teach us about how we read?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Porshe says, "I cannot separate Biblical Texts from my current events, past events, social issues of today and yesterday."[^6] She holds a copy of her ancestors' slave manifest, and those ancestors "clung to the Exodus story, believing the God who freed Israel could and would bring freedom to them."[^6] The notes add that removing race and social issues from biblical interpretation "is a luxury afforded to those whose identity and experience align with the dominant culture."[^6] What is your honest response to that last sentence? What would change in how you read the Exodus if you read it alongside her ancestors?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Brent names Porshe's *chutzpah* ("bold courage") in reaching out, the same boldness as the official's question.[^9] Without speaking for anyone else, what helps a person feel they can ask "Do I belong?" in a church or a group like ours? What is one thing you could do to make that question easier to ask?

</div>

### Why Wait for Heaven? <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#why-wait-for-heaven}

*The episode reads both the beginning and the end of the biblical story as celebrating difference.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 11:1–9 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Brent and Marty suggest the scattering at Babel was not a curse but God's intervention, since uniform humanity accomplishing whatever it wanted would have been "unbelievably destructive."[^10] By creating many languages and cultures, God pushed humanity to "learn empathy and curiosity" and to "work together across differences."[^10] What do you notice in the passage that fits or challenges that reading?

*Read Revelation 7:9 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes say every biblical glimpse of heaven emphasizes diversity first, a "kaleidoscope of people and humanity," and Porshe asks "why wait for heaven?"[^11] Porshe's suggestions include amplifying diverse voices, being specific about geography and ethnicity, and highlighting intercultural relationships in Scripture.[^12] Which of these could you practice in your own reading or in this group over the next month, and what would be the first step?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Porshe Chiles came to the conversation with gratitude and with a hard truth: a story that is deeply diverse has often been taught in a way that left her unseen. Hagar names the God who sees her; an African official asks whether anything stands in the way of his belonging; Babel scatters people so they must learn to listen. Every glimpse of heaven puts the nations, tribes and tongues first. The episode asks us to start seeing people that way now, not someday.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one thing you heard today that you want to keep listening to?

</div>
