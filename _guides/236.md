### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** The New Testament has "no being apart from its relation to the Scriptures of Israel," and Jesus affirmed the temple, ritual purity and the Sabbath rather than starting a new religion.[^1]

**This week:** Two more essays ask whether Paul abandoned Jewish practice and how Hebrews understands Jesus's sacrifice in light of the whole Levitical process.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Marty and Brent discuss chapters 4 and 5 of *Understanding the Jewish Roots of Christianity*. David Rudolph argues that some texts carry more weight on a question because they were written to settle it, and he names three on Paul and Jewish law: 1 Corinthians 7:17–20, Acts 15 and Acts 21:17–26. Read together, they suggest Paul's "rule in all the churches" was that Jews should remain Jewish and Gentiles should remain Gentiles, and that Paul himself lived as a Torah-observant Jew.[^3]

David M. Moffitt then argues that Levitical sacrifice was a whole process, not just a death: animals were slaughtered away from the altar, and the altar was where the offering was brought into God's presence. Hebrews, he says, sees Jesus completing every part of that process, including bringing his sacrifice into the heavenly Holy of Holies. The hosts also wrestle with "new covenant," preferring to hear it as a renewed covenant within the same story.[^4]

### Weightier Texts and Paul's Rule <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#weightier-texts-and-pauls-rule}

*Rudolph asks which passages were written specifically to settle the question of Paul and Jewish law.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read 1 Corinthians 7:17–20 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes say circumcision works here as a metonymy (a part standing for the whole) for complete Jewish identity and lifestyle, so Paul's "rule in all the churches" means Jews should remain Jewish and Gentiles should remain Gentiles. The idea of removing the marks of circumcision recalls the Maccabean period, when some Jews, in the words of 1 Maccabees, "abandoned the holy covenant."[^5] What does Paul tell each person to do, and how often does he repeat the instruction? What is he guarding against?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Rudolph's criterion for a "weightier text" is that "the text itself or its context explicitly indicates its purpose to set the record straight on a controversial matter." The notes say the Jerusalem Council in Acts 15 explicitly exempted Gentiles from circumcision and full Torah observance, and that a growing number of scholars see an equally important implication: Jewish believers were expected to keep theirs.[^6] Why might it be wise to give more weight to passages written to settle a question than to isolated verses pulled from elsewhere?

*Read Acts 21:17–26 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Paul arrives in Jerusalem to a rumor that he teaches Jews to abandon Moses and stop circumcising their children. James and the elders reject the rumor and ask Paul to purify himself in the temple and pay for the sacrifices of four men under a vow. The notes say the text makes two points plain: the rumor is false, and Paul himself lives in observance of the law. Rudolph counts seven places in Acts where Paul defends himself against charges of apostasy.[^7] What do you notice about the rumor, the request and Paul's response? If this is the lens, how might it change the way you read Paul's letters? The hosts add that they "read Paul through Jesus rather than Jesus through Paul."[^8]

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes sum up Paul's rule this way: "the Gospel meets people where they are without demanding they become something else first."[^5] Think diagnostically about churches you have known, including your own. Where have newcomers been expected to take on a particular culture or style before they fully belonged? What would it look like for you to meet someone where they are this week?

</div>

### Sacrifice as Process, Not Just Death <span class="guide-time">35 min</span> {#sacrifice-as-process-not-just-death}

*Moffitt argues that Levitical sacrifice, and Jesus's sacrifice in Hebrews, is more than a death.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Moffitt describes the full Levitical process: slaughter away from the altar, collecting the blood, butchering, bringing portions to the altar, burning them, and applying blood to the altar. In this process, "altars were places of offering, not slaughter." He adds that sacrifice worked within a relationship, aimed at drawing near to God, and within a covenant.[^9] The notes observe that many believers picture Jesus on the cross as being "on the altar."[^10] What picture of sacrifice did you grow up with? What does seeing it as a whole process add or change?

*Read Hebrews 9:11–12 and 9:24 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Moffitt argues that just as the high priest had to take blood into the Holy of Holies to complete atonement, Hebrews portrays Jesus taking his sacrifice into God's presence in the heavenly Holy of Holies, serving as both sacrifice and priest. "Jesus's work is not reducible only to his death."[^11] What does this passage say Jesus does, and where does he go? Report what you notice about the movement in these verses.

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The hosts prefer to hear "new covenant" as "renewed covenant": the same God, the same people, the same story, with an improved mechanism. The Abrahamic covenant of promise remains foundational, and Jesus serves as sacrifice, priest and temple.[^12] How does "renewed" sound different to you from "new"? What does each word suggest about God and about Israel?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The hosts' friend Moshe in Jerusalem says Jewish sacrifice is "fundamentally about drawing near to God, not about substitution or transaction," a way to approach God's presence, "to cleave to the divine."[^13] Traditions in this room may explain atonement in different ways, and this is not a call to give any of them up. But if sacrifice is first about drawing near, how might that shape the way you approach God after you have failed, this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> This week's essays reread two things many of us thought we understood. Paul, read through the passages written to settle the question, remained a Torah-observant Jew who taught Jews to stay Jewish and welcomed Gentiles without asking them to become Jewish. Sacrifice, read through the whole Levitical process, was never only a death but a way of drawing near to God's presence, and Hebrews sees Jesus completing every step as both priest and offering. In both, the God of Israel keeps one story going and keeps inviting people to come near.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one way you want to "draw near" to God this week?

</div>
