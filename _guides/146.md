### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This episode works through Galatians 3, where Paul argues that justification comes by faith rather than by "works of the law." The notes explain that phrase (*Miqsat Ma'ase haTorah*) as the practices that made someone ethnically Jewish, not Torah observance in general. The "curse of the law," in this reading, is the burden of trying to find justification through ethnic identity rather than through trust.[^2]

Paul's argument is deeply Jewish. He points out that Abraham was declared righteous in Genesis 15, two chapters before his circumcision in Genesis 17. He uses an advanced rabbinic technique called *gezerah shavah* (connecting passages that share a word or phrase), and he describes the law as a *paidagōgos*, a guardian hired to protect a young heir until he could claim his inheritance. The notes stress that Paul is not attacking Jewish law or practice, and that reading him this way helps us avoid supersessionist interpretations.[^3]

### Foolish Galatians <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#foolish-galatians}

*We hear the tone of Paul's opening and the pressure behind it.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Galatians 3:1–5 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Listen to how Paul speaks in these verses. How many questions does he ask? What does he want the Galatians to remember about how they first received the Spirit?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** According to the notes, "foolish Galatians" played on a Roman stereotype of Galatians as backwoods, uneducated people. Paul was effectively telling people who prided themselves on being learned that they were "acting like the stereotypical ignorant locals."[^4] Why might Paul choose such a stinging way in? What does his frustration tell you about how much he thinks is at stake?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe the Galatian believers as squeezed from two directions: rejected by the dominant Shammai Jewish community, and exposed to Roman persecution without the protection of the "Jewish exception."[^5] When have you felt pressure from two sides at once because of what you believe? What helped you stand, or what do you wish had helped?

</div>

### Abraham Before Circumcision <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#abraham-before-circumcision}

*We read Abraham's story in order and follow Paul's argument from it.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 15:1–6, then Genesis 17:9–11, then Galatians 3:6–9 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** What is the order of events in Abraham's story? What did Abraham have, and not have, at the moment God credited him with righteousness? The notes point out that at that time Abraham "had no Torah rules to follow - only a relational covenant based on trust."[^6]

*Read Galatians 3:10–18 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes read "works of the law" as "the practices that distinguish Jews ethnically from Gentiles," and the "curse" as the burden of relying on those markers for justification. They also point to Paul's contract analogy: a later agreement does not cancel an earlier promise, so the covenant through Moses does not void the promise to Abraham.[^7] How does this reading change what you hear in verse 10 and in verses 15–18? What is Paul defending, and what is he not attacking?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> If the burden Paul warns against is trying to secure your standing with God through identity markers, what markers do people in our churches lean on to feel secure? Which one tempts you most?

</div>

### The Guardian and the Heirs <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#the-guardian-and-the-heirs}

*We look at the law's role in the story and at who now counts as Abraham's children.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Galatians 3:23–29 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes explain that a *paidagōgos* was not a teacher but "a guardian hired to protect a young heir until he's mature enough to claim his inheritance." They also describe three categories in the Jewish world: brothers (born Jews), children of Abraham (proselytes), and *theosabes* (God-fearing Gentiles). Paul argues that faith, not conversion, makes Gentiles *b'nei Avraham*, "children of Abraham."[^8] In verses 23–29, what changes when the heir comes of age, and who is now named an heir?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** This chapter is often read as Paul declaring the law finished and Judaism replaced. The notes insist Paul's position "isn't anti-Jewish but represents a different stream within Jewish thought," and that understanding the "curse" correctly helps readers "avoid supersessionist interpretations."[^9] How might the guardian image be misused against Jewish people, and how does the notes' reading guard against that? What questions does it leave you with?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Paul pictures believers as heirs coming into an inheritance rather than servants earning a place. Where in your life do you still relate to God more like a hired hand than an heir? What would it look like to live as an heir this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Paul's harsh words to the Galatians come from love for people being told they were not fully in. He answers from the beginning of the story: Abraham trusted God and was declared righteous before he was circumcised and before any law was given. The law guarded God's people like a guardian protects an heir, until the promise to bless the nations could be claimed. Through faith, Gentiles are named children of Abraham, and no one needs to carry the burden of proving they belong.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one burden of proving yourself that you want to set down?

</div>
