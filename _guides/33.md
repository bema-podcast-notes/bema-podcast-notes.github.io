### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We finished Session 1 with the Torah capstone, which gathered Genesis through Deuteronomy into two frameworks: a Tale of Two Kingdoms (Empire and Shalom) and a partnership story told like a marriage, from betrothal to the edge of the promised land.

**This week:** Before Israel crosses the Jordan, Marty and Brent pause to review the whole Torah story so that the history, prophets and wisdom books of Session 2 have solid ground to stand on.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This short episode is a bridge. It announces that Session 2 will explore the history, prophets and wisdom literature of the Hebrew Bible, beginning with Joshua, and it reviews the Torah as one connected story.[^1] Two frameworks carry the review. The first is a tale of two kingdoms: Empire, driven by fear and scarcity, and Shalom, driven by trust and abundance.[^2] The second is a five-part partnership: Genesis gives the basis, Exodus the choice, Leviticus the definition, Numbers the shaping, and Deuteronomy the remembering.[^3]

The episode also argues that God's people should be able to tell this story as a seamless narrative, the way you would tell it to a friend over coffee, and that regular review matters because a culture built on entertainment makes it easy to forget.[^4] Because the episode itself is short, this guide gives extra time to reading two Torah passages and practicing the story out loud.

### Two Kingdoms <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#two-kingdoms}

*The episode frames the whole Bible as a contest between two ways of living.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes describe Empire as "Driven by fear and a scarcity worldview" and operating "through coercion rather than invitation," with the motto "if I don't look out for myself, everything falls apart." Shalom is "Driven by trust and an abundance worldview," characterized by "everything in its right place," and operating "through invitation rather than coercion."[^5] Read those descriptions slowly. Which phrase in each list best captures the kingdom for you, and where have you watched one of these phrases play out in an ordinary place like a workplace, a family or a congregation?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes offer a set of questions for testing everyday decisions, including "Is this choice driven by fear or trust?" and "Does this build walls (empire) or tables (shalom)?"[^6] Which of these questions would be most useful to you this week, and why that one?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes connect Shalom to "the inherent goodness of creation established in Genesis 1-11."[^7] Empire assumes there is not enough to go around; Shalom assumes God provides enough when it is needed. Think about one recent decision you made about time, money or relationships. Which assumption was underneath it, and what would the other assumption have changed?

</div>

### A Partnership in Five Movements <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#a-partnership-in-five-movements}

*At Sinai, God invites a newly rescued people into a partnership, and the rest of the Torah teaches them what that means.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Exodus 19:3–6 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes call Mount Sinai "the marriage ceremony" where God proposes partnership and Israel says, in effect, "I do." They also stress one small word in this passage: Israel is called to be "Not a kingdom *with* priests (a special class within a nation)" but "a kingdom *of* priests (an entire nation functioning in priestly role)."[^8] Listen to verses 5 and 6 again. What do you notice about the order of things (what God has already done, what God asks, what God promises)? What changes if the whole nation, not a special class, is meant to be priests?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The episode reviews the Torah as five movements of one partnership: Genesis is the basis, Exodus the choosing, Leviticus the definition, Numbers the shaping, and Deuteronomy the remembering.[^9] The notes add that Leviticus establishes "we are good with God" first, which "prevents making religion about earning favor through obedience."[^10] Looking back over Session 1, which of the five movements changed the way you read the Bible the most, and what changed?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say the Tabernacle and the Levitical priests served as "a daily object lesson": by watching the priests work, all Israelites learned their own priestly calling as go-betweens for God and the world, blessing all nations.[^11] What "daily object lessons" have taught you most about what faith looks like in practice?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes point out that Israel left Egypt knowing very little about God. Like a bride and groom in an arranged marriage, they entered covenant with a God they did not truly *yada* ("know intimately"), which "calls for grace rather than judgment when reading their story."[^12] The notes call this "Green People Grace" and apply it to new believers, churches recovering from poor theology, people reconstructing faith, and "including ourselves."[^13] Where are you a "green" person right now, still starting from square one, and what kind of patience would help you there?

</div>

### Remembering and Retelling <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#remembering-and-retelling}

*Deuteronomy ties memory to action, and the episode ties both to telling the story well.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Deuteronomy 24:17–22 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes summarize Deuteronomy's central principle this way: "We were slaves in Egypt; we were aliens, orphans, and widows" and therefore "We take care of the alien, the orphan, and the widow (AOWs)."[^14] Read the passage again and count how many times the alien, the orphan and the widow appear, and how many times Israel is told to remember. What do you notice about how memory and action are connected in these verses?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The episode introduces "Message," the ability to tell "the cohesive story from beginning to present day in a conversational setting." The notes give a sample beginning: "God created partners and placed them at the crossroads of the earth. He asked them to bless all nations. That became a struggle, so God chose a specific family to demonstrate partnership..."[^15] Turn to one or two people near you and take three minutes to tell the Torah story in your own words, from creation to the edge of the promised land. Then come back together: what was easy to tell, and where did you get stuck?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes observe that Jewish practice reads Torah every year and the rest of the Tanakh every three years, and that "Western culture's addiction to entertainment and novelty works against internalization."[^16] What has helped you keep returning to Scripture when it no longer feels new?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q11:** The notes list modern equivalents of the AOWs: refugees and immigrants, children in foster care or from broken homes, single parents, and "Any marginalized group experiencing powerlessness."[^17] Remembering your own seasons of vulnerability, who in your actual life is in one of those places right now, and what is one small way your memory could turn into care?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Session 1 gave us the Torah as one story: a good creation, a God who chose a partner, rescued her, married her at Sinai, defined her calling, shaped her in the desert, and told her to remember. Running underneath it is a contest between Empire and Shalom, fear and trust, walls and tables. Israel stands now at the Jordan, a kingdom of priests who barely know their God, about to be placed in the middle of the world. Session 2 will follow how that partnership plays out in real places with real failures. We start the way Deuteronomy does, by remembering where we came from.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one thing from the Torah story you want to carry into the land with you?

</div>
