### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Jude warned against teachers who turned God's grace into a "license for immorality" and urged compromise with Roman guild culture. The problem was practice more than doctrine, and Jude called believers back to purity and obedience, respect for God's authority, and love and hospitality.

**This week:** We begin a sixteen-episode journey through Revelation. Before any beasts or bowls, the episode asks who first heard this book, what kind of book it is, and what John sees when he turns toward the voice in chapter 1.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode lays the foundation for the whole Revelation series. It calls the book apocalyptic literature, written primarily to a first-century church being persecuted by Rome rather than primarily about the end of the world.[^1] The hosts apply the same approach BEMA uses everywhere: ask what the author meant and what the first audience understood.[^2] They place the book in the later reign of Domitian, among believers who were watching brothers and sisters die and wondering whether faithfulness was worth it.[^3]

The episode then walks through Revelation 1, showing how John builds his vision of Jesus from Daniel, Zechariah, Ezekiel and Isaiah, and how the seven stars and seven lampstands would have sounded to people living under Rome.[^4] It closes on a conviction that runs through the series: what we believe about "the end" shapes how we live now.[^5]

### A Letter to People Under Pressure <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#a-letter-to-people-under-pressure}

*Before decoding any images, the episode asks who first heard this book and what they were facing.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Revelation 1:1–11 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes point out that the book is "Revelation," singular: John had one apocalypse, not many.[^6] The hosts read it the way BEMA reads all of Scripture, seeking what the author meant and what the first audience understood, which they call the "inspired conversation."[^7] Go back through verses 1–11 and list every detail you can find about who is writing, who is receiving it, and where the writer was. What do those details suggest about who this book was written for first?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes describe the first listeners as Christians facing execution, believers running for their lives, a community watching brothers and sisters be killed, and "People questioning whether standing against the empire was worth it."[^8] John wrote as one who shared their suffering. Because he says "I was on the island of Patmos" in the past tense, the notes conclude he wrote after his exile, even though tour guides show visitors a cave where he supposedly wrote.[^9] The notes also estimate that about one in five people in Asia was Jewish, so many in these churches would have recognized Old Testament allusions immediately.[^10] How does picturing these first listeners change what you expect the rest of the book to be about?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe apocalyptic literature as highly symbolic, using cosmic imagery to describe earthly events, drawing on earlier apocalyptic texts, and written in times of persecution "to encourage perseverance and faithfulness."[^11] The episode sends listeners back to Episode 68 on Zechariah as preparation.[^12] What changes when you read a book as encouragement for suffering people instead of as a coded roadmap to future events?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes sum up the book's core message in one line: "It is worth it. You have to overcome because we know how this story ends."[^13] Where in your life right now do you most need to hear that faithfulness is worth what it costs?

</div>

### The One Who Holds the Seven Stars <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-one-who-holds-the-seven-stars}

*John turns to see the voice speaking to him, and nearly every detail of what he sees comes from earlier Scripture.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Revelation 1:12–20 aloud, then Daniel 7:13–14 and Daniel 10:5–6.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes trace the images in Revelation 1 to earlier prophets: "coming with the clouds" to Daniel 7, "those who pierced him" to Zechariah 12, the golden sash, blazing eyes and glowing bronze feet to Daniel, the voice like rushing waters to Ezekiel, and the double-edged sword to Isaiah.[^14] Compare Revelation 1:12–16 with the Daniel passages and list what matches. The notes observe that "Very little material in Revelation is actually new."[^15] Why might John build his picture of Jesus almost entirely out of older prophets?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes give several reasons John quotes earlier apocalyptic books: to establish his genre, to connect present suffering to earlier experiences of God's faithfulness, and to create solidarity between his audience and ancient oppressed communities.[^16] Then they explain the seven stars. In Greco-Roman thought these were the sun, moon, Mercury, Mars, Venus, Jupiter and Saturn, bodies that moved differently from the other stars and were tied to the gods, so "To hold the seven stars meant controlling the universe and destiny."[^17] The lampstands, or menorahs, picture God's presence dwelling among his people, a common image after the temple was destroyed in AD 70.[^18] What would it mean to a believer facing Rome's power to hear that Jesus holds the stars in his hand and walks among the lampstands?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> If the lampstands are communities where God dwells, the notes connect that image to a world in which the Jerusalem temple no longer stood.[^19] How does that picture shape the way you think about what a local church is for?

</div>

### What We Believe About the End <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#what-we-believe-about-the-end}

*The hosts insist that eschatology, what we believe about the end, is not a side topic but something that shapes daily life.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Revelation 1:7–8 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes list common modern misreadings of Revelation: treating it as a coded roadmap, matching it to current political events, reading it mainly as the end of the world, ignoring its first audience, and approaching it with sensationalism. The series promises to "remove the crazy" from Revelation study.[^20] The traditions in this room have taught about Revelation and the end times in different ways, and some of us have learned more than one view. Without trying to settle which is right, what approach to Revelation did you first learn, and what questions did it leave you with?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The Patmos cave, the notes suggest, shows how a popular tradition can obscure what the text actually says.[^21] Where have you held a tradition about a passage that a closer reading complicated?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes describe four ways beliefs about the end shape life. If we believe the world is doomed, we may disengage; if God's kingdom is advancing, we invest in it now; if suffering is meaningless, we may despair; if God is sovereign over history, we persevere with hope.[^22] Which of these do you see most in your own choices, and what would living from the last one look like for you this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Revelation begins not with a timetable but with a voice, and with a man who shared the suffering of the people he wrote to. The churches of Asia were watching friends die and asking whether standing against the empire was worth it. John answers with a vision built from Daniel, Zechariah, Ezekiel and Isaiah: the one who holds the stars that Rome thought ruled destiny is walking among his lampstands. The message for them, and the challenge for us, is the same: it is worth it, because we know how this story ends.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one question about Revelation you are bringing into this series?

</div>
