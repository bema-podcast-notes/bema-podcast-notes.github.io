### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Philadelphia, the door to Asia Minor, had been shaken by earthquakes and renamed by emperors. Through Isaiah 45, Isaiah 62 and the named pillars of Solomon's temple, Jesus promised an open door no one could shut and a new name no empire could impose.

**This week:** The last of the seven letters goes to wealthy, self-sufficient Laodicea. The famous "lukewarm" line turns out to be about water, and the rebuke turns out to be an act of love.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The notes explain that Laodicea sat between Hierapolis, with its hot healing springs, and Colossae, with its cold fresh spring, and its own water arrived lukewarm, mineral-laden and useless for drinking.[^1] The lukewarm metaphor, the episode argues, "is not about spiritual apathy or sitting on the fence; it is about being useless rather than useful for God's purposes."[^2] The city was rich, home to a major mint and bank, and after the AD 60 earthquake it refused Rome's bailout and minted coins that said "We did it ourselves."[^3]

The notes show how nearly every phrase of the letter touches the city's life, from gold and black wool to its famous eye salve and soldiers knocking at doors to demand meals, while also drawing on Isaiah, Proverbs, Hosea and Leviticus.[^4] The episode ends with good news: the Laodicean church apparently received the rebuke and became a significant church in Asia Minor.[^5] This week also closes the seven letters, so the final segment looks back across all of them.

### Neither Hot Nor Cold <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#neither-hot-nor-cold}

*The letter's best-known image becomes much clearer once we know about Laodicea's water.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Revelation 3:14–16 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes say Brent noticed that the phrase runs "cold nor hot" and then "hot nor cold," and wondered whether the reversal is significant.[^6] Look closely at verses 15–16. What do you notice about the order, and what does Jesus say he wishes the church were?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes describe the three cities: Hierapolis had hot mineral springs with healing properties; Colossae had "a cold, refreshing freshwater spring"; Laodicea, in between, received lukewarm water so full of minerals that a fountain there had to be replumbed several times as white deposits clogged the pipes.[^7] They contrast a "Common Youth Group Teaching," "God would rather have you against Him than lukewarm. Get off the fence! Stop being apathetic!" with the contextual reading: "God wants you to be useful. Hot water brings healing (like Hierapolis). Cold water brings refreshment and life (like Colossae)."[^8] How does this reading change what Jesus is asking of the church?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes trace the language of being spit out to Leviticus 18:28 and 20:22, where the land will "vomit you out" if it is defiled.[^9] Read Leviticus 18:28. What does that background add to the seriousness of Jesus's words?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** If being hot means bringing healing and being cold means bringing refreshment, which do you think the people around you most need from you right now, and where might you be offering neither?

</div>

### We Did It Ourselves <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#we-did-it-ourselves}

*Laodicea's wealth and independence were its pride, and the letter addresses each one.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Revelation 3:17–18 aloud, then Hosea 12:7–8 and Isaiah 55:1–2.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes describe a city with a major mint and banking institution, famous black wool garments called *tramata*, and an eye salve made from Hierapolis's mineral mud that was renowned for healing certain kinds of blindness.[^10] After the AD 60 earthquake, Hierapolis took Rome's money, Colossae received none and eventually vanished, and Laodicea rejected the bailout, rebuilt on its own, and minted coins reading "We did it ourselves."[^11] Read verse 17 beside Hosea 12:7–8. What does the merchant in Hosea say that sounds like Laodicea?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes match each offer in verse 18 to the city: gold refined in the fire against its mint and banks, white clothes against its black wool, and eye salve against the salve it exported, which suggests "they are spiritually blind despite their cure for physical blindness."[^12] They draw on Isaiah 55:1–2, "Come buy wine and milk without money," spoken "to those who are wealthy but spiritually poor."[^13] Why might a wealthy, self-sufficient church find it harder than a poor one to see its own need?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes observe that Laodicea's self-sufficiency "was apparently viewed as respectable rather than insulting to Rome."[^14] In our culture, self-reliance is often praised. Where is the line between healthy responsibility and the "We did it ourselves" attitude the letter warns against?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes say this "mirrors how material success and self-sufficiency can create spiritual complacency in any era."[^15] In what area of your life are you most tempted to say, in effect, that you "do not need a thing," and what would buying from Jesus look like there?

</div>

### A Knock at the Door <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#a-knock-at-the-door}

*The letter ends not with a threat but with an invitation, and the episode looks back across all seven letters.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Revelation 3:19–22 aloud, then Proverbs 3:11–12.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes explain that the Roman military presence in Laodicea meant soldiers often invoked *angaria* law to demand meals from citizens, who had to comply and "would never dine with the soldiers as equals."[^16] Against that background, "Jesus knocks but makes no demands" and "offers to dine with anyone who opens the door."[^17] The notes also connect the promise of sitting on Jesus's throne to Zeno, a famous Laodicean orator whose three children all married royalty.[^18] How does this background change the way you hear "I stand at the door and knock"?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes report that the Laodicean church "ultimately responded well to this rebuke," became a significant church in Asia Minor, and later honored Polycarp in a mosaic.[^19] They conclude that "Sharp correction does not mean failure or abandonment" and that God's discipline is "an expression of love, not rejection."[^20] How have the traditions you have belonged to taught you to hear God's correction, and how does Proverbs 3:11–12 shape the way you hear it now?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q11:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> We have now read all seven letters, and the notes suggest comparing the churches that received sharp corrections "to understand patterns in how self-perception differs from God's perception."[^21] Looking back from Ephesus to Laodicea, which church's letter has stayed with you most, and why?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q12:** Jesus knocks as a guest, not a soldier. What is one area of your life where you sense him knocking right now, and what would it mean to open the door this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Laodicea had everything: a mint, a bank, fashionable black wool, a famous eye salve, and the pride of having rebuilt without anyone's help. What it did not have was good water, and Jesus uses that daily frustration to tell the church it has become neither healing nor refreshing. He offers what its money cannot buy, then stands at the door like a guest rather than a soldier and asks to share a meal. The notes tell us this church listened. A sharp word received in love can turn a lukewarm church into a useful one.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one way you want to be useful, healing or refreshing, to someone this week?

</div>
