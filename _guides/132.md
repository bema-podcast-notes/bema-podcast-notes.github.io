### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We watched two disciples fail Jesus on the same night. Judas decided there was no coming back; Peter heard "tell his disciples and Peter" and was given a new tomorrow. The only true betrayal, the episode said, is believing there is no way back.[^1]

**This week:** The empty tomb and the Great Commission. This is the last teaching episode of Session 3 before next week's capstone.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode reads the resurrection as "God's definitive answer that the order of life triumphs over the order of death," and Marty argues that it deserves the central place in how we understand Jesus, possibly even more than the crucifixion or the incarnation.[^3] He then follows the disciples north. The notes argue the Great Commission was given on a mountain in Galilee, likely Mount Hermon, which meant the disciples walked past nearly every place Jesus had taught, thinking it all through again in light of the resurrection.[^4]

Two details stand out. Matthew says that when the disciples saw Jesus, "they worshiped him, but some doubted," and Jesus commissioned them all together.[^5] And Marty offers what he calls his most controversial position: that "make disciples" is a call to intensive, rabbinical, come-follow-me discipleship that is "for someone, but not for everyone."[^6] Our traditions understand the cross, baptism, and the call to make disciples in different ways. Today is a chance to listen, notice, and talk honestly rather than to settle those differences.

### Which Kingdom Wins? <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#which-kingdom-wins}

*The resurrection answers the question the whole Bible has been asking.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 28:1–10 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Listen for the emotions in this passage: in the guards, the women, and the angel's and Jesus' words. What do you notice about how fear and joy appear together, and what the women are told to do?

*Read Ezekiel 37:1–3 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes frame the whole biblical story as a conflict between two orders. "The Order of Death (Empire)" includes cancer, greed, corruption, fear, and death itself; "The Order of Life (Shalom)" includes love, mercy, forgiveness, healing, and second chances. Moses set life and death before the people, Joshua asked Israel to choose, and Ezekiel was asked, "Can these bones live?" The resurrection, the notes say, "finally answers this question definitively - death is not as real as we thought."[^7] How does reading the empty tomb as the answer to that long question change the way you hear Matthew 28?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Marty contrasts several ways of centering the story of Jesus. Much of evangelical Christianity, emphasizing penal substitutionary atonement, puts the crucifixion at the center (the notes cite the line from *The Passion of the Christ*, "Dying was His reason for living"). Tony Jones emphasizes the incarnation as God joining us in solidarity, and Greg Boyd sees the cross as the lens for all theology. Marty argues for "the preeminence of resurrection."[^8] Our traditions have put the weight in different places, in our hymns, our calendars, and our preaching. Without ranking them, where did your tradition place the center of gravity, and what has that emphasis given you or left out?

*Read 1 Corinthians 15:12–14 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q4:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say the order of death appears dominant in our world, yet the empty tomb declares that "death is not as real as it appears," which lets believers face suffering "not with denial, but with confidence that the order of life gets the last word."[^9] Where in your life or your community does the order of death seem to be winning right now? What would it look like to face that place with resurrection confidence rather than denial?

</div>

### The Mountain in Galilee <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#the-mountain-in-galilee}

*Jesus sends his followers on a long walk before he sends them to the nations.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 28:16–17 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes point out that the Ascension happened on the Mount of Olives near Jerusalem, but the Great Commission was given on "the mountain" in Galilee, a week's journey north. Marty suggests Mount Hermon, the likely site of the Transfiguration and overlooking Caesarea Philippi, where Jesus said he would build his church. To get there and back, the disciples had to "Pass by virtually every location where Jesus taught and ministered" and "Process all of Jesus's teachings post-resurrection."[^10] Imagine walking that road after Easter. Which places or teachings from our weeks in Matthew do you think would have looked completely different to them now?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Verse 17 says, "When they saw him, they worshiped him, but some doubted." The notes observe that Jesus "doesn't separate the believers from the doubters" and commissions them all together, concluding that "doubt is acceptable for commissioned disciple-makers of Jesus."[^11] What do you notice about the order of events in verses 16–18? What does it mean to you that the doubters were not sent home first?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say the walk to Galilee "models how understanding deepens with new revelation" and that "Believers today can similarly revisit familiar teachings with new eyes as understanding grows."[^12] After sixty weeks in the Gospels, which familiar teaching of Jesus do you now hear with new eyes, and how has it changed the way you live?

</div>

### Make Disciples <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#make-disciples}

*Three instructions, under an authority Jesus never hands off.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 28:18–20 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes unpack the instructions. To teach is to open people's eyes to God's story all around them, which means disciples must "get the Text in us." To baptize is described as an act of *t'shuvah* (repentance, returning home), marking "entrance into a better Kingdom with a new King," and the notes distinguish this baptism from John's *tavilat t'shuvah* (a ritual immersion of repentance).[^13] Baptism is understood and practiced differently across our traditions. Without debating the practice, what does the image of baptism as returning home and entering a new kingdom add to the way you think about your own baptism or the baptisms you have witnessed?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** Marty argues that "make disciples" is not mainly a call to make converts or to mentor everyone, valuable as those are, but a call to rabbinical, come-follow-me discipleship. In the first century, disciples were rare, less than 1% of the population, and the other 99% of faithful Jews "didn't feel inferior for it." He believes discipleship is "for someone, but not for everyone."[^14] The notes acknowledge this "disrupts common interpretations."[^15] What do you find compelling in this reading, and what pushes back in you? What would be gained, and what might be lost, if churches took it seriously?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes say that unlike typical rabbis, who passed their authority to their disciples, the risen Jesus keeps all authority, so disciples operate "under Jesus's ongoing, living authority." When mistakes happen and fear overwhelms, the promise remains: "I am with you always, to the very end of the age (olam)."[^16] Where do you most need to hear "I am with you always" this week, and how might it change what you are willing to attempt?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The women ran from the tomb afraid and full of joy, because the question the whole Bible had been asking was finally answered: the order of life gets the last word. Then Jesus sent his followers on a long walk north, past every place he had taught, to a mountain in Galilee. There they worshiped, and some doubted, and he sent them all. Teach, baptize, make disciples, he said, under an authority he keeps and a presence he promises: "I am with you always."[^17]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: where is Jesus sending you, doubts and all, as this session comes to its end?

</div>
