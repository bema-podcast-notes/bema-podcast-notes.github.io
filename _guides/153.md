### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Paul pictured our freedom through slavery and marriage, named the real struggle between image-bearer and appetite, and ended in the assurance that there is no condemnation and nothing can separate us from God's love.

**This week:** Paul takes up the hardest questions his blended family was asking: what predestination means, whether God may show mercy to outsiders, and what becomes of Israel now that Gentiles are coming in.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This is the longest episode in the Romans series, and it covers some of the most debated chapters in the Bible. The episode reads predestination in Romans 8 through the first-century oracle system, where a predestined outcome depended on the worshipper's obedience.[^1] It reads the potter and the clay in Romans 9 as a picture of God's prerogative to show mercy, not to reject, and it argues that Romans 9–11 is primarily a defense of Gentile inclusion in God's covenant family.[^2]

The episode then turns to the olive tree of Romans 11. It argues firmly against replacement theology, the idea that the church replaced Israel: Gentiles are wild branches grafted into Israel's tree, and God's gifts and calling to Israel are irrevocable.[^3] Our traditions have taught different things about election and predestination, so this session is a place to hear one another, not to settle the debate.

### Predestined: A First-Century Hearing <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#predestined-a-first-century-hearing}

*The episode asks how Paul's first readers would have heard the word "predestined."*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Romans 8:28–30 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Trace the chain of verbs in verses 29 and 30. What does God do at each step, and where does the chain end? What purpose does verse 29 give for it all?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes explain that first-century people consulted oracles, such as those at Delphi or Didyma, and that the formula was conditional: "If you offer X sacrifice, and if you do Y, then it is predestined that Z will happen."[^4] In that world, "predestination was contingent upon obedience," so the episode concludes that Paul's discussion "assumes the believer's choice to follow God's plan."[^5] How does hearing "predestined" in that setting change the way verses 28–30 sound to you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Each of our traditions has a history with this word. Some emphasize God's sovereign choice, others human response, and many hold both together in tension. What were you taught about predestination, how did that teaching shape your faith, and what in the oracle reading do you find helpful, troubling or unconvincing?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Verse 28 is often quoted on its own in hard seasons. Read in light of verse 29, what kind of "good" is God working toward? Where do you need to trust that purpose right now?

</div>

### The Potter's Mercy <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-potters-mercy}

*Paul answers the objection that God has no right to welcome the outsider.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Romans 9:19–24 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Who seems to be raising the objection in verse 19? In verses 22 and 23, what does the potter do with the vessels "prepared for destruction," and what does he do with the vessels of mercy? Who does verse 24 say the vessels of mercy include?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes say the potter metaphor "doesn't focus on God's rejection but on His prerogative to show mercy," and that "Vessels prepared for destruction" refers to pagan Gentiles whom God bore "with great patience" rather than destroying, ultimately showing them mercy.[^6] They sum up: "Paul's argument focuses on God's acceptance and mercy, not rejection or exclusion."[^7] Many of us learned this passage as being about God choosing some individuals and not others. What happens to the passage if its main question is whether God may show mercy to the outsider?

*Read Romans 10:9–13 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say Paul appropriated a Roman imperial slogan, "If you proclaim with your mouth that Caesar is Lord, you will be saved," and turned it toward Jesus, showing how the gospel "directly challenges political and cultural power structures."[^8] Notice who verses 12 and 13 include. What would it have cost a believer in Rome to say "Jesus is Lord" out loud?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes describe God as "the divine Potter who specializes in showing mercy to vessels others might consider worthless."[^9] Who are the people you, or your church, might be tempted to treat as vessels for destruction? What would God's patience toward them look like in practice?

</div>

### Grafted In, Not Replacing <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#grafted-in-not-replacing}

*Paul uses an olive tree to describe how Gentiles relate to Israel.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Romans 11:1–5 aloud, then Romans 11:17–29.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** In verses 17–24, which branches are natural and which are wild? What supports what (look at verse 18)? What warning does Paul give the wild branches in verses 18–21? Then notice how verses 28 and 29 describe Israel.

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes point out that the grafting "goes against nature - wild branches would typically harm a cultivated tree's productivity," which shows the scandal of God's grace.[^10] The episode states that "replacement theology is incorrect" and that Gentiles "are grafted into the existing covenant community of Israel," adding that the translation "in this way all Israel will be saved" (rather than "and so") is crucial.[^11] Where have you heard the church described as having replaced Israel, and how does Paul's picture of a single tree with one root challenge that?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q11:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Paul recalls Elijah and the seven thousand who had not bowed to Baal. The notes draw out the principle that "God always maintains a faithful remnant" and is "always working through people willing to partner with His redemptive purposes."[^12] Where have you seen a faithful remnant keep something alive when it looked lost?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q12:** The episode encourages "examining our own religious arrogance and exclusivity," and the notes say God's people are called to be "ambassadors of this radical inclusion rather than gatekeepers of exclusive privilege."[^13] Paul tells the grafted branches not to boast. Where are you most tempted to act as a gatekeeper, and what would one act of welcome look like this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> In Romans 9–11, Paul is not writing an abstract treatise on who is chosen; he is defending God's freedom to show mercy to outsiders in a divided church. Predestination, heard through the oracles of his day, assumes a people who choose to follow God's plan. The potter shows patience and mercy to vessels others had written off. And the Gentiles are wild branches grafted into Israel's own tree, supported by its root, with no grounds to boast, because God's gifts and calling to Israel are irrevocable.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: who is one person or group you want to stop gatekeeping and start welcoming?

</div>
