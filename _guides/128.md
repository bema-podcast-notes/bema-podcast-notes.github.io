### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Jesus pronounced seven woes where Isaiah had six, and we learned that a *hupocritos* is an "actor," someone performing righteousness for an audience. The episode argued that self-righteousness is worse than greed, because "at least people know greed when they see it."[^1]

**This week:** Jesus' disciples marvel at the temple, and he tells them it will fall. We read his long answer in Matthew 24 and the three parables of Matthew 25 as one teaching about how to live faithfully when the systems around you are crumbling.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Matthew 24–25, often called the Olivet Discourse, is usually read as a roadmap to the end of the world. This episode reads it differently. It argues that the whole discourse answers the disciples' question about when the temple will be destroyed, an event that came in 70 AD, and that Jesus' line "this generation will certainly not pass away" points to events within his hearers' lifetimes.[^3] Along the way it turns the familiar "left behind" image on its head.[^4]

The episode then walks through three parables (the bridesmaids, the talents, and the sheep and goats) using *p'shat* (the plain meaning), *remez* (a hint pointing back to an earlier text), and *drash* (interpretation). All three reinforce the same message: be ready, expect to give an account, and take care of the oppressed, because "The priests are not doing their job, so you will have to."[^5] Our traditions hold a range of views on the end times. This guide does not ask anyone to adopt a new system, only to read the passage closely and talk honestly about what we notice.

### Not One Stone Left <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#not-one-stone-left}

*The discourse begins with a question about buildings, and the answer stays tied to that question.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 24:1–3 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes describe Herod's Temple Mount as an engineering marvel, with massive stones fitted without mortar and construction still going on in Jesus' day, so the disciples' amazement was "well-founded."[^6] Jesus answers that "not one stone here will be left on another."[^7] Look closely at verse 3. What exactly do the disciples ask, and what do you think they had in mind when they asked it?

*Read Matthew 24:32–35 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The episode calls verse 34 the critical verse: "Truly I tell you, this generation will certainly not pass away until all these things have happened." The notes say this statement "anchors the discourse in the first-century context."[^8] The notes also sum up Jesus' main points in Matthew 24: do not believe rumors of false messiahs, the world will get crazy and you cannot stop it, you will have to persevere, and being ready means taking care of God's people.[^9] If you read the chapter as a warning to the disciples about their own generation, what in it becomes clearer, and what still feels hard to place?

*Read Matthew 24:37–41 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes point out that in the flood story the wicked were "taken away" by the waters while Noah and his family remained, and that in Jesus' parables of the wheat and weeds and the net, the bad are always the ones taken away. So in this passage, they argue, being "left behind" means "surviving God's judgment, not missing his salvation."[^10] Many of us were taught to read these verses in another way, and the teaching we received has shaped what we hear. Without settling who is right, what did you learn about these verses growing up, and what happens in you as you hear them read this way?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q4:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Among the modern parallels the hosts draw is "The temptation to focus on predicting the future rather than faithful living in the present."[^11] The episode's closing question is not "when will these things happen?" but "will you be found faithful when crisis comes?"[^12] Where in your life do you notice yourself trying to predict what is coming instead of living faithfully in the present?

</div>

### Ready and Accountable <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#ready-and-accountable}

*Two parables press the same point from two angles: readiness and responsibility.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 25:1–13 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes explain that in a Jewish wedding, after betrothal the groom left to build onto his father's house, which could take weeks, months, or even years, and he could return at any time, "even in the middle of the night."[^13] The *remez* points to Jeremiah 25, where among the losses of Jerusalem's destruction would be "the voices of bride and bridegroom, the sound of millstones and the light of the lamp."[^14] What do you notice about what the foolish bridesmaids lacked? How does the Jeremiah echo change the feeling of the story?

*Read Matthew 25:14–30 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes offer a reading of the numbers: five talents for complete knowledge of Torah (the five books of Moses), two for basic understanding (the two tablets), and one for "Only awareness of God (echad - God is one)." The message is that "Whatever level of understanding you have, God expects you to use it faithfully to serve others."[^15] Whether you hold five, two, or one in this picture, what does it look like to put what you know of God to work for others, rather than bury it?

*Read 2 Kings 22:3–7 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The second *remez* is 2 Kings 22, during Josiah's reforms, when workers repairing the temple did not have to give an account of the money "because they are honest in their dealings." The notes add that the priests had lost the book of the Law, "but the workers were faithful."[^16] Where have you seen ordinary, faithful people quietly carry work that leaders had dropped, and what would it take for you to be one of them this month?

</div>

### The Least of These <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#the-least-of-these}

*The third parable names the standard by which the faithful are known.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 25:31–46 aloud, then read Ezekiel 34:17–19 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes say the criteria for judgment is how we treat "the least of these," the hungry, thirsty, stranger, naked, sick, and imprisoned. They connect the parable to Ezekiel 34, where God judges between sheep and goats, condemning those who trample the pasture and muddy the water others need, in a chapter that condemns corrupt shepherds who "feed themselves but do not care for the flock."[^17] Listen to both passages side by side. What do you notice about what the judge asks about, and what he never asks about?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes sum up all three parables in one line each: "Be ready," "You will have to give an account," and "Take care of the oppressed and do what the priests are not doing."[^18] Among the practical applications is "Independence from corrupt leadership: Do what is right even when religious authorities fail to do so."[^19] Why might Jesus tie readiness so tightly to caring for the hungry, the stranger, and the prisoner, rather than to knowing the timeline?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes call believers to use whatever God has given us, much or little, to bless others, and to "Actively care for the hungry, thirsty, stranger, naked, sick, and imprisoned."[^20] Of the six named in the parable, which one is closest to your daily path right now, and what is one concrete thing you could do for that person or group before we meet again?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The disciples marveled at stones, and Jesus told them the stones would fall within their generation. His answer was not a timetable but a way to live: do not chase false messiahs, persevere, and be ready. Three parables say it again. Be ready like the wise bridesmaids. Use what you have been given, whether five talents, two, or one. Care for the hungry, the stranger, the sick, and the prisoner, because the shepherds were not doing it. The question that remains is not "when will these things happen?" but "will you be found faithful when crisis comes?"[^21]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what does "being ready" mean for you this week?

</div>
