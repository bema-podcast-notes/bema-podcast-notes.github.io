### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Isaiah closed with a renewed world of justice and welcome, and with Marty's picture of a father who disciplines his son and then invites him to "say yes to building a new world with Dad."[^1]

**This week:** Reed Dent opens a new series on the Psalms, arguing that they are poetry meant to be experienced and that they give us honest words for every season of life.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode starts with a confession: Marty and Brent both find the Psalms hard to connect with, because years of study have trained them to dissect rather than experience. Reed's answer is to read the Psalms as poetry, which "applies greater pressure per word," and not as a source of proof texts. He sets Psalm 51:5 beside Psalm 139:13–14 to show why single lines should not be turned into doctrine.[^3]

Reed then describes the Psalms as a vocabulary for the soul, giving words for grief and doubt and, in a cynical age, for joy and praise as well. He offers two ways of grouping the Psalms, ending with Walter Brueggemann's framework of orientation, disorientation and new orientation, which will guide the rest of the series. The main point is that God is the right conversation partner in every season.[^4]

### Poetry, Not Proof Texts <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#poetry-not-proof-texts}

*The Psalms are poetry from start to finish, and poetry works differently from explanation or argument.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Proverbs 29:11 aloud in every translation the group has on hand.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The episode compares four translations of this verse, from the NIV to Robert Alter's. As a teenager Reed took it as a reason always to stay rational and calm, which later made a psalm like Psalm 137 feel uncomfortable. Through college apologetics he became "a brain in a jar," and reading poetry helped reconnect him with his emotions.[^6] What differences do you notice between the translations you just heard? What happens when one wisdom principle like this is applied to every part of life?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** Thomas Long, quoting Laurence Perrine, says poetry has "a higher voltage than other literary forms" and "applies greater pressure per word." Long adds that poems change us "by making finely tuned adjustments at deep and critical places in our imagination." The notes put it simply: prose is discursive and propositional, while poetry is evocative and expressive.[^7] If the Psalms are meant to create experiences rather than make claims, how might that change the way you read them?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Brent says that once he opens his Bible he goes into "study mode," and that studying the Psalms on a podcast is a bit like "dissecting a swallow."[^8] What mode do you tend to go into when you open the Bible, and where did you learn it? What is one thing that helps you experience a text rather than only study it?

</div>

### A Vocabulary for the Soul <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#a-vocabulary-for-the-soul}

*The Psalms do not supply answers so much as ways to ask the questions.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Psalm 51:5 aloud. Then read Psalm 139:13–14 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes call this "the Psalm 51 vs. Psalm 139 tension." Psalm 51:5 is often used as a proof text for original sin, while Psalm 139 praises God for being "fearfully and wonderfully made." Rather than asking which one is "true," Reed suggests each honestly expresses what the psalmist is living through, and that the two gain more by speaking to each other.[^9] What do you notice when you hear these two lines back to back? What might each psalmist have been going through to say what they said?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The traditions in this room have taught Psalm 51:5 in different ways, and some have built a great deal on it. Marty separates theology in the broad sense, "the study and expression of experiencing God," from doctrine built by proof-texting, and says the Psalms belong to theology rooted in concrete experience.[^10] How was Psalm 51:5 used in the church you grew up in, if it was used at all? What would change, and what would stay the same, if you heard it first as an honest cry from one moment of a life?

*Read Psalm 13:1–2 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Reed, a campus minister, describes a student who feels "God is nowhere" but was taught God is always present. Praying Psalm 13 turns those forbidden feelings into prayer. The episode borrows King Lear's line that the Psalms invite us to "speak what we feel, not what we ought to say," because God already knows what is going on inside us.[^11] What feelings have you, at some point, believed you were not allowed to bring to God? Share only what you are comfortable sharing.

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Reed argues that in a cynical culture, where any hopeful statement draws an instant "yeah, but," the Psalms of praise and wonder may now be the harder and more necessary voice. In his words, honesty includes being honest about hope.[^12] Right now, which is harder for you to say honestly to God: lament or joy? Why do you think that is?

</div>

### Orientation, Disorientation, New Orientation <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#orientation-disorientation-new-orientation}

*Brueggemann's three modes describe seasons of life rather than tidy categories of content.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Reed gives each mode a seasonal image. Orientation is a summer evening on the porch with lemonade; disorientation is winter, "when it plunges below zero"; new orientation is the first warm day after a long winter. He also insists that no categories capture the Psalms neatly. They form "a really big, messy Venn diagram," just as human life does.[^13] Which of these images feels closest to the season you are in now? What makes it hard to place yourself in only one?

*Read Psalm 8:3–4 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** Psalm 8 is one of Reed's examples of reflection and wonder. Psalms of orientation remind us that it is "okay to be okay." Marty admits that "How are you doing?" frustrates him when life is simply fine, and the Psalms of orientation give that season its own song.[^14] What do you notice about the tone of these verses compared with Psalm 13? Why might people of faith feel they need to explain away a season when life is simply good?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> New orientation is "not just a return to the old normal" but a changed outlook shaped by having been rescued, pictured as the "Mario star": nothing can touch me now. Reed also suggests that moving into disorientation mirrors crucifixion, and moving into new orientation mirrors resurrection. Two simple practices come up in the episode: praying one psalm a day, and reading things that aren't the Bible, especially poetry.[^15] Which of those two practices could you try this week, and what would make it realistic?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The Psalms are poetry, words that carry greater pressure per word and reach places in us that argument cannot. They do not hand us doctrines to defend so much as honest words for grief, doubt, anger, hope, praise and joy. Whether we are on the summer porch, stuck in a bitter winter, or feeling the first warm day of spring, there is a psalm for that season. The deepest invitation is that God is the right conversation partner in all of them, and that we can bring God what we actually feel.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, name the season you are in right now and one honest word you want to say to God about it.

</div>
