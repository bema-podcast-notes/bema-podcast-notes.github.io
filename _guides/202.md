### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Camp meetings, the Stone-Campbell Restoration Movement and the Social Gospel each tried to recover something lost, and the Scopes Trial launched a culture war that may have misidentified the enemy.[^1]

**This week:** The history arrives in our own lifetimes: fundamentalism and liberalism, World War II, modern evangelicalism, the Jesus Movement, Jacob Neusner, postmodernity and today.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

By 1925, Marty says, Christianity had split into fundamentalism and theological liberalism, "two equally bad options" in which valuable elements on each side could not find each other. World War II shattered secular humanism's optimism, and in the opening that followed, Billy Graham and Bill Bright launched a modern evangelical movement that put simple gospel language into the mouths of ordinary believers. The 1970s brought the Jesus Movement, in which Brent's mother came to faith in 1975.[^3]

Meanwhile, largely unseen by popular Christianity, the Jewish scholar Jacob Neusner brought Jewish and Christian academic worlds into conversation for the first time in roughly 1,800 years. Marty says plainly that BEMA "would not happen without the work of Jacob Neusner." The episode ends with quantum science, postmodernity, the Emerging Church and our present moment, describing BEMA itself as a bringing back together of worlds that took separate paths. Many of us inherited faith through the movements named here, so this guide invites gratitude and honest evaluation at the same time.[^4]

### Two Equally Bad Options and a New Evangelicalism <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#two-equally-bad-options-and-a-new-evangelicalism}

*The twentieth century began with a split and then found an unexpected opening after the world's worst war.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Read this list aloud. According to the notes, fundamentalism emphasized:[^5]

- biblical inerrancy and literal interpretation;
- separation from cultural engagement deemed "worldly";
- a defensive posture against modern scholarship;
- certainty in doctrine and theology.

Theological liberalism appeared to many as "a weak take on humanism with Jesus slapped on the label," accommodating modern thought at the expense of orthodox belief and distinctive Christian identity. Yet the notes say those grounded in fundamentalism received a deep love for Jesus and Scripture, while those in liberal Christianity engaged seriously with critical scholarship and cultural questions.[^6] What do you notice each camp was trying to protect, and what was each afraid of? Which list sounds more like the church you grew up in?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The nineteenth and early twentieth centuries believed in inevitable human progress. World War II, with the Holocaust, Stalin's oppression and nuclear weapons, revealed that educated, cultured societies could commit atrocities. Marty sums it up: "Simply put, humanity isn't as great as we thought it was." That collapse, he says, gave Christianity a renewed cultural relevance.[^7] Why might a catastrophe open a door to faith? Where have you seen something similar happen in a person's life?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Billy Graham's crusades, Campus Crusade for Christ and Bill Bright's Four Spiritual Laws democratized evangelism, moving it from clergy to "normal, everyday parishioners." Marty compares the era to the printing press and admits theological limits in the Four Spiritual Laws ("That's all I'm going to say about that"), but credits them with a framework where "college students, co-workers, and soccer moms could articulate the movement of Jesus in simple language." The Jesus Movement then made personal relationship with Jesus and testimony central, and many of our parents came to faith in it.[^8] Thinking of the faith that was handed to you, what do you still treasure in it, even if you would say some of it differently now?

</div>

### Two Worlds Brought Back Together <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#two-worlds-brought-back-together}

*A Jewish scholar reopened a conversation that had been closed since the early Gentile church divorced its Jewish roots.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** For about 1,800 years, since the early Gentile church expelled Jewish believers, Christian scholarship had proceeded without Jewish thought. Jacob Neusner, a Jewish literary scholar with Orthodox roots, brought Christian scholars (mostly Catholic at first) into conversation with Jewish scholarship, and they discovered interpretive frameworks "that had gone missing some 1800 years ago." Marty is emphatic: "BEMA is not just indebted - this whole discussion would not happen without the work of Jacob Neusner, period."[^9] Looking back over our whole BEMA journey, which insight from Jewish context most changed the way you read a particular passage or story?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Read this list aloud. The notes describe the traditional path from an academic discovery to the pew:[^10]

- Discovery occurs in scholarship.
- It is vetted for academic viability.
- It is integrated into seminary curricula.
- It is taught to future pastors.
- Those pastors get positions.
- They rise to teaching and preaching roles.
- They finally communicate it to congregations.

That process took twenty to thirty years; the internet has shortened it to almost nothing, though Marty notes it distributes "all information, good and bad." What do you notice about who guarded each step of the old path? Now that much of that path is gone, how do you sort good information from bad?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Brent asks about Vatican II and Neusner's work: "If they had a new council today, in light of all of this interaction with Judaism, how much would happen?" N. T. Wright, the notes say, "stands on the shoulders of a conversation that started just right in front of him."[^11] Imagine your own tradition gathering today to reconsider its teaching and practice in light of what it has learned about Jesus's Jewish world. What is one thing you would hope it would revisit, and what is one thing you would hope it would hold onto?

</div>

### Postmodernity and Today <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#postmodernity-and-today}

*Certainty gave way to questions, and the episode asks how to see our own moment.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Quantum mechanics revealed a less predictable universe than Newton's, and by the late 1990s the cultural question became, "What do we truly know anyway?" The Emerging Church tried to bring the gospel into that conversation and was seen by conservative critics as "the den of iniquity and the spawn of Satan himself"; it is no longer viable in its original form but started conversations that continue. Marty describes today's culture wars as either "the awkward phases of a modern evangelicalism that is going through a sociological puberty" or "the final gasping breaths of a movement coming to a very unflattering end."[^12] Which of those two descriptions rings truer to you, and why?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Marty describes BEMA as a synthesis: "BEMA is, 100 years later, a bringing back of these two worlds, where these two worlds took two separate paths for a while to navigate some of these nuances." He names decentralized consumerism, social networking and uncertainty about the future of Christianity, yet concludes that "we find ourselves - as every generation has - perfectly placed for this future."[^13] What would it mean for you, in your actual life, to live as someone "perfectly placed" for this moment rather than someone born at the wrong time?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The last hundred years began with two inadequate options, a defensive fundamentalism and a liberalism that seemed to lose its distinctive faith. World War II humbled humanism, and in the opening that followed, ordinary believers learned to share Jesus in simple words, many of our parents met him in the Jesus Movement, and a Jewish scholar named Jacob Neusner quietly reopened a conversation the church had closed 1,800 years earlier. Postmodernity and the internet have shaken our certainties and sped up everything we learn. Marty sees BEMA as a bringing back together of love for Jesus and Scripture with serious scholarship and honest questions, and he insists that we, like every generation, are perfectly placed for the future in front of us.[^14]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, name one way you want to be "perfectly placed" for someone in your life this week.

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 203 — You Are Here](/guides/203)** (22:05): With the history complete, Marty summarizes the whole BEMA journey through four pillars present in Scripture and especially in the first-century church: Text, Community, Discipleship and Wrestling. He describes how the church lost each one when it rejected its Jewish heritage, how modern believers can reclaim them, and why change begins with small, realistic steps of *t'shuvah* ("return" or "repentance").[^15]

**What to bring next week:**
- Read Isaiah 55:10–11 and Genesis 32:22–32 before the meeting.
- Consider which of the four pillars (Text, Community, Discipleship, Wrestling) is strongest in your life and which is weakest.
