### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We saw that Genesis keeps choosing the one who was not the firstborn, and we learned to ask of any Midrash, "Why is the Midrash trying to show me this?"[^1]

**This week:** Marty and Brent look at Midrashic traditions around the Nephilim and the troubling scene between Noah and Ham, holding the traditions loosely and looking for what they teach.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Genesis 6:4 says the Nephilim were on the earth "in those days, and also afterward," which raises an obvious puzzle if only Noah's family survived the flood. The Midrash offers several answers, from giants building Babel above the waterline to a giant named Og riding out the flood on the ark.[^3] The episode then turns to Genesis 9, where Ham "saw his father naked" and Noah curses Ham's son Canaan, and surveys several traditions about what happened in that tent.[^4]

The hosts' conclusion is that "regardless of which interpretation you follow, the core teaching remains constant": the danger of lashing out in insecurity, and the way revenge and curses reverberate across generations.[^5] The episode also notices the unnamed wives of the flood story, and it includes a rare moment when the hosts stopped recording and started over because Marty realized he did not understand the material well enough.[^6]

### Noticing the Problems <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#noticing-the-problems}

*Midrash begins where careful readers notice that something in the text does not add up.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 6:1–8 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Look at verse 4. The notes call the phrase "in those days, and also afterward" a textual puzzle "since only Noah's family survived the flood."[^3] What other questions does this short passage raise for you as you read it slowly?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The Midrash proposes several answers: the Nephilim survived on a Tower of Babel begun before the flood; a giant named Og clung to the ark (or straddled it and steered it to Ararat); or the same thing happened again after the flood.[^3] Which answer surprises you most? What do these answers show about how seriously the rabbis took small details in the text?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes say Midrash helps Western readers "notice textual problems and details they typically overlook or explain away," and that it is "meant to teach us how to think, not what to think."[^7] When you meet a puzzle in the Bible, what is your instinct: explain it away, skip past it, or stay with it? Where did you learn that instinct, and what would it look like for this group to hold such puzzles "with humility and open hands"?

</div>

### What Happened in the Tent <span class="guide-time">35 min</span> {#what-happened-in-the-tent}

*The vineyard scene is strange and uncomfortable, and the rabbis refused to look away from it.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 9:18–29 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Notice who acts, who speaks, who is cursed and who is not. The notes ask, "Why curse Canaan instead of Ham?" and point out that Canaan is Ham's fourth son in Genesis 10 and that a producing vineyard takes four to five years to plant.[^8] What do these details suggest about the time between leaving the ark and this scene? What questions does the text leave unanswered?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes survey several readings of "saw his father naked": a Genesis Rabbah tradition that it is an idiom for castration; another tradition in which an animal on the ark injured Noah and Ham mocked him; one in which Noah denied Ham a wife and Ham took revenge; and a suggestion from some textual critics, "not Jewish tradition," of a sexual violation.[^4] The traditions disagree, yet the hosts say "the core teaching remains constant." Why might Jewish tradition be content to let contradictory explanations stand side by side? What do they all share?

*Read Genesis 7:7 and Genesis 8:15–18 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes track the order of boarding and leaving the ark: going in, Noah and his sons come before his wife; God's command to exit pairs Noah with his wife; the actual exit returns to the first order.[^9] The wives are mentioned several times but never named, and rabbinic tradition identifies Noah's wife as Naamah, with one stream seeing her as a temptress and another as "Noah's greatest partner." What do you make of these details, and of the rabbis noticing them?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes trace a pattern: someone is violated or embarrassed, they "lash out in insecurity and curse another," the curse "reverberates through generations," and "massive human suffering results," which the hosts connect to the later conquest of Canaan.[^10] Without naming anyone, where have you seen a cycle of hurt passed from one generation to the next, in a family, a church or a community? What do you think it takes to break a cycle like that?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The Midrash does not smooth over Genesis. It notices the giants who should not have survived, the curse that falls on the wrong person, and the wives who are present but never named, and it lets several answers stand side by side. Whatever happened in Noah's tent, the hosts find the same lesson underneath: hurt that lashes out in insecurity becomes a curse that echoes for generations. Marty and Brent modeled the posture they taught by stopping the recording to learn more before speaking. We can hold the text the same way, with humility and open hands.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: where could you choose this week to absorb a hurt instead of passing it on?

</div>
