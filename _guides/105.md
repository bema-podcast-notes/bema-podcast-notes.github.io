### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Jesus sent the Twelve to "the lost sheep of Israel" with no provisions, warned them that opposition would come from within the community of faith, and called them to be shrewd as serpents and innocent as doves.

**This week:** John the Baptist, now in prison, sends Jesus a pointed question. Jesus answers with healing, honors John in front of the crowd, and describes a Kingdom that has been bursting out like sheep through an opened gate.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

From Herod's prison, John the Baptist sends his disciples to ask Jesus, "Are you the one who is to come, or should we expect someone else?"[^1] The episode reads this less as a crisis of faith and more as a challenge rooted in two different Jewish expectations about how God's Kingdom would arrive: suddenly, with judgment on God's enemies, or gradually, like a seed or yeast.[^2] Jesus answers by pointing to what John's disciples can see and hear, and he leaves out one line John might have hoped to hear.[^3]

Jesus then turns to the crowd and gives John the highest honor, calling him the Elijah who was to come.[^4] The episode proposes that a difficult verse about the Kingdom "suffering violence" is better heard through the Hebrew word *pratz*, "to break out, burst forth," and connects it to the gate-breaker of Micah 2.[^5] It ends with Jesus's picture of children in the marketplace who sing songs and miss what is in front of them.[^6]

### A Question From Prison <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#a-question-from-prison}

*John the Baptist, sitting in Herod's prison, hears what Jesus is doing and sends a sharp question.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 11:1–6 aloud, then Isaiah 61:1.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Jesus tells John's disciples to report what they "hear and see," and the notes trace his list to Isaiah 35:5–6, 26:19, 29:18–19 and 61:1.[^7] Look closely at Jesus's list in Matthew 11:5, then at Isaiah 61:1. What appears in Isaiah that is missing from Jesus's answer? The notes suggest the omission was "a gentle way of telling John he would die in prison."[^8] Put yourself in John's place. What would it be like to receive that answer?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes describe two Jewish views of how history would turn. In two-part eschatology, *olam hazeh* ("this age") gives way abruptly to *olam haba* ("the age to come") through "Immediate catastrophic judgment on God's enemies," and John's images of the axe at the root and the winnowing fork fit this view.[^9] In three-part eschatology, which the notes associate with some Pharisees and with Jesus, the Kingdom arrives and overlaps with the present age, growing "like a mustard seed" or like yeast in dough.[^10] The notes add that John had heard Jesus healed a Roman centurion's servant, and "From a two-part eschatology perspective, the Messiah should be destroying Romans, not healing them."[^11] How does this background change the way you hear John's question in verse 3?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes report that "Many scholars suggest John the Baptist may have been Jesus's rabbi," and they wonder whether John's question is "the chiding of a former Rabbi" rather than doubt.[^12] They paraphrase Jesus's closing line in verse 6 as "I hope you're not offended, but you have the wrong eschatology."[^13] If this is a disagreement between a teacher and a former student who respect each other, what does it suggest about how faithful people can disagree well?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** John expected God to act one way, and Jesus was acting another. Where have you found God working in your life more slowly or more quietly than you expected, and how did you live with that gap?

</div>

### The Gate Kicked Open <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-gate-kicked-open}

*As John's disciples leave, Jesus turns to the crowd and honors the man who just challenged him.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 11:7–15 aloud, then Micah 2:12–13.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Go back over verses 7–11 and list the questions Jesus asks the crowd and the answers he gives. The notes observe that "Jesus honored John even while correcting his theology."[^14] What do you notice about how Jesus speaks of John once John's disciples have gone?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes call verse 12 "notoriously difficult to translate." They propose that behind the Greek stands the Hebrew *pratz* ("to break out, burst forth, breach"), the root of the name Perez, who "burst out" of the womb in Genesis 38, and they suggest Jesus likely said: "the Kingdom of Heaven has been explosively advancing, and explosive people take hold of it."[^15] They connect this to Micah 2, where "The one who breaks open the way will go up before them," and picture sheep penned up all winter bursting through the opened gate; John is the one who "kicked open the gate," and Jesus is the King leading the flock through.[^16] How does the picture of sheep leaping out of the pen change your sense of what the Kingdom is doing in this passage?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes argue that "Many modern Christians unknowingly hold to John the Baptist's two-part eschatology," shown in phrases like "The world is getting worse and worse," while three-part thinking says "The Kingdom is already here and advancing."[^17] The traditions in this room have taught about the end times in different ways. Without trying to settle who is right, what did your tradition teach you to expect about the direction of the world, and how has that expectation shaped the way you live day to day?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes contrast "Explosive people," who are "At the front of the flock, eager to burst through the gate," with passive people "At the back of the flock, waiting for everyone else to go first."[^18] The original notes ask whether there is a place where "the kingdom of God is accessible to me if I want it however I'm simply not ready."[^19] Where in your life are you hanging back at the gate right now, and what would it look like to step through?

</div>

### Children in the Marketplace <span class="guide-time">10 min</span> {#children-in-the-marketplace}

*Jesus closes with a picture of a generation that sings about salvation and misses it.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 11:16–19 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes link these verses to a fable of a piper who plays to lure fish into his boat before their lake dries up; when they lie dying on the beach, he says, "I played a happy song and you didn't dance; I played a sad song and you didn't mourn."[^20] The notes say this generation criticizes whatever God sends: "John (too ascetic), Jesus (too indulgent)."[^21] What pattern do you see in the crowd's response to both John and Jesus?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes ask of churches today, "are we actually participating in the reality we're singing about?"[^22] Think of a song your church sings often. What would it look like this month to live the reality it describes, not only sing it?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> John the Baptist expected the Messiah to swing the axe and clear the threshing floor. Jesus healed a Roman's servant instead, answered John with the signs of Isaiah, and left out the line about captives going free. Then he turned to the crowd and called John the greatest of those born of women, the Elijah who kicked open the gate. Since then, the Kingdom has been bursting out like sheep released to spring pasture, small and gradual in appearance but unstoppable. The question Jesus leaves us is whether we will be at the front of the flock or still waiting at the back.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: where is one place you have seen the Kingdom quietly advancing in the past month?

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 106 — Jury Duty in Sodom](/guides/106)** (33:43): Jesus pronounces judgment on Chorazin, Bethsaida and Capernaum, the most devoted Jewish towns of the Galilee, and says pagan Tyre, Sidon and even Sodom will fare better. The episode asks why sincere, scripture-saturated communities needed to repent, and then hears Jesus's invitation, "Come to me," as three bold claims about who he is.

**What to bring next week:** Read Ezekiel 16:49–50 and write down every sin Ezekiel names for Sodom. Then read Matthew 11:20–30 and mark the word Jesus uses for what these towns failed to do.
