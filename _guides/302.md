### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Brent and Reed showed Jesus meeting Thomas's honest doubt without condemnation, and noticed that the disciples who believed were still behind locked doors, which raised the difference between cognitive belief and lived belief.[^1]

**This week:** Marty Solomon and Brent Billings look at John 21, where Jesus restores Peter after his denial with three questions, a charge, and a hard prediction.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode notices a pattern of threes: Peter's three denials are answered by three restorations, this is the third appearance of the risen Jesus to the disciples, and later in Acts Peter sees the sheet three times. It then explores the Greek words for love in Jesus's questions: *agape* (often described as divine, unconditional love) and *phileo* (brotherly, friendship love). Marty favors the traditional view that the shift matters, and the notes also record the view of many modern scholars that the words are interchangeable.[^3]

If the distinction holds, Jesus's final question comes down to Peter's level, which gives the episode its title: "Do you even like me?" The episode also looks at the difference between "lambs" and "sheep," Jesus's prediction of Peter's martyrdom, and John's closing words about all that Jesus did that could not be written down.[^4] The notes on this episode are brief, so this guide stays close to a few central moments.

### Do You Love Me? <span class="guide-time">35 min</span> {#do-you-love-me}

*The episode reads Jesus's three questions as a careful answer to Peter's three denials.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read John 18:15–18 and John 18:25–27 aloud, then John 21:15–17.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes say Peter's three-part restoration "directly corresponds to his earlier three-part denial of Jesus," and that the repeated threes suggest "completeness and thoroughness."[^3] Placing the two scenes side by side, what lines up between them? Name one detail you notice.

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes lay out the conversation this way: twice Jesus asks if Peter loves him with *agape*, and twice Peter answers with *phileo*; the third time Jesus asks with *phileo*, and Peter, hurt, answers with *phileo* again. Read with the distinction, the progression shows "Peter's honest self-assessment of his limited capacity to love after his failure" and "Jesus's gracious descent to meet Peter where he is emotionally." The notes also record that many modern scholars see no real difference between the words.[^3] What does each reading highlight about Jesus and about Peter, and what do you make of a teacher holding his preferred view with that kind of openness?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Jesus says "lambs" (*arnia*) once and "sheep" (*probata*) after that. The traditional view sees stages of spiritual maturity. Marty's theory sees Gentiles and Jews, based on reading the 153 fish earlier in the chapter as a sign of Gentile inclusion.[^5] What difference would each view make to how you hear Peter's new calling?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes conclude that "God's grace meets us where we are, not where we think we should be," and that Peter's honest "I like you" rather than a claim of perfect love "becomes the foundation for renewed ministry calling."[^6] Where do you find it hard to give God an honest answer rather than the answer you think you are supposed to give, and what might the honest answer be?

</div>

### Follow Me <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#follow-me}

*Restoration in John 21 leads straight into a calling and a cost.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read John 21:18–25 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes say Jesus foretells Peter's future martyrdom, "indicating that discipleship may require ultimate sacrifice," and they ask how this works as both warning and encouragement.[^7] What do you notice about how Jesus describes Peter's future and what he says immediately afterward?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> John ends by "emphasizing the selective nature of his account and the impossibility of recording all of Jesus's works."[^8] Why do you think John chose to end his Gospel this way rather than with a summary of what Jesus taught?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Peter's restoration ends with a task, not just forgiveness. The feeding and shepherding language in John 21 is about caring for people "at different spiritual stages."[^9] Who has God placed near you right now who needs to be fed or cared for, and what is one specific way you could do that this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Peter denied Jesus three times, and Jesus answers with three questions, not three accusations. Whether or not the shift from *agape* to *phileo* carries the weight the episode suggests, Jesus receives the love Peter can honestly offer and builds on it. Restoration leads at once to a calling (feed my lambs, shepherd my sheep) and to an honest word about the cost. John ends by reminding us that his story is a selection from far more than any book could hold.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what honest word would you want to say to Jesus if he asked you whether you love him?

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 303 — The History and Style of the Gospel of John](/guides/303)** (46:48): Drawing mainly on Dr. Gary Burge's *Interpreting the Gospel of John*, Marty and Brent trace how scholars have viewed John over two thousand years, from the most honored Gospel, to Enlightenment doubts that it was late and Greek, to the 1924 claim that it is "the most Jewish of the four gospels." They also introduce John's style, including misunderstandings, ironies, asides, and layered meanings.[^10]
