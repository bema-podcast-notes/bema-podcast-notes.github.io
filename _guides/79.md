### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We followed the Essenes into the desert, admired their devotion to knowing and walking the path, and watched John the Baptist carry that devotion out to the people.

**This week:** The Zealots refused both compromise and withdrawal. They took up the sword, and the episode holds their tragic error and their necessary passion side by side.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The notes present the Zealots as the fourth Jewish response to Hellenism. Like the Pharisees, they came from the *Hasidim* ("pious ones") who moved north to the Galilee, but they believed violence could defend God's honor and win back Israel's freedom.[^1] Their theology drew on the Hebrew word *qanah*, "zeal" or passionate jealousy, and on heroes like Phinehas and Elijah.[^2] The episode tells hard stories: the Sicarii assassins, the Caves of Arbel, and the siege of Gamla, where the notes report that 4,000 died in the fighting and 5,000 more died at the cliff rather than be captured.[^3]

The hosts name the Zealots' error as belief in the "myth of redemptive violence," but they warn against judging too quickly from a place of safety.[^4] They also insist the church needs Zealot passion: Jesus called Zealots among his disciples, and zeal can be channeled into kingdom work with kingdom methods.[^5] This session includes stories of violence and loss; anyone may pass on any question.

### Qanah: Zeal and Its Heroes <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#qanah-zeal-and-its-heroes}

*The Zealots believed their passion reflected God's own.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes explain that *qanah* describes God's jealousy for his people, "not petty envy, but the passionate, protective love of a devoted spouse."[^6] When God says "I am a jealous God," the notes hear exclusive devotion that "tolerates no rivals."[^7] How does this description of jealousy compare with the way you usually hear that word? What does it suggest about how God feels about his people?

*Read Numbers 25:1–13 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** This was one of the Zealots' foundational stories. Read it again carefully. What does Phinehas do, what is happening among the people at the time, and how does God respond? Notice the kind of covenant God makes with him. The notes call it a "troubling text" that "doesn't provide a blanket endorsement of religious violence, but it does complicate simplistic readings that assume God always condemns forceful action."[^8] What makes this passage hard to read? What do you think the Zealots heard in it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Alongside Phinehas, the Zealots admired Elijah, who confronted corrupt kings and the prophets of Baal. The notes say these precedents created "a genuine theological dilemma: How should faithfulness be expressed when God's people are compromised?"[^9] The Zealots answered with action rather than study or withdrawal. How does your own tradition tend to read the violent stories of the Hebrew Bible, and what would you say to a Zealot who quoted Numbers 25 to you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q4:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The original notes list several "words of caution," including "The zeal of the abused" and "The zeal of the father protecting his family."[^10] Where have you seen zeal that grew out of pain or out of love for someone vulnerable? How does knowing its source change how you respond to it?

</div>

### From Arbel to Gamla <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#from-arbel-to-gamla}

*The history of the Zealots is a cycle of oppression, resistance and retaliation.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes describe a pattern that runs through history: "oppression leads to violent resistance, which leads to brutal retaliation, which leads to more violence, in an endless cycle that ultimately consumes everyone involved."[^11] They trace it from the Maccabean victory, after which the priesthood the Zealots fought for became corrupt within twenty years, to the Caves of Arbel, where Rome smoked families out of cliffside caves, to the fall of Gamla.[^12] Where do you see that cycle in the world today, or on a smaller scale, in families, workplaces or churches?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** At Gamla, Marty's teacher Ray Vander Laan grabbed his collar and demanded he consider what the Romans would do to his daughter Abigail. The notes conclude that this "forces privileged interpreters to acknowledge their distance from the Zealots' reality."[^13] They add that the critique of the Zealots "comes from a position of relative safety and privilege."[^14] When have you judged someone's reaction to a situation, only to realize later you had never faced anything like it? What changed when you realized that?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes report that the Zealot commander Joseph was captured and, in the notes' words, "adopted by Vespasian," becoming Josephus Flavius, the historian whose writings preserve much of what we know about this period, and that most historians believe he revealed Gamla's weak point to Rome.[^15] How does it affect your trust in a history when its main source is also one of its most controversial figures?

</div>

### Zealots Among the Twelve <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#zealots-among-the-twelve}

*Jesus did not avoid Zealots. He called them.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Luke 6:12–16 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Read the list of names again slowly. What do you notice about how Simon is identified? Who else is on the list, and what kinds of people would have been sitting next to each other? The notes say Jesus called at least two Zealots, Simon the Zealot and possibly Judas Iscariot, and that he "almost certainly" taught in Zealot synagogues like Gamla's.[^16] What does this choice of disciples suggest about Jesus?

*Read John 18:10–11 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes point to Peter's sword in Gethsemane as a sign of "Zealot tendencies" among the disciples.[^17] Read the passage again. What does Peter do, and what does Jesus say to him? The notes describe Jesus as "neither wholly endorsing nor completely condemning" the Zealots' passion for God's honor.[^18] How do you see that balance, or tension, in this short scene?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode's discussion notes ask Zealot-minded people, "do you ever struggle to feel like there is a place for you?"[^19] The original notes answer plainly: "God has a place for the zealous" and "We will need Zealots if we're going to impact the world."[^20] Who are the passionate, intense people in your church or community, and how well do you make room for them?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q11:** Marty once counseled a young disciple named Megan, who burned with zeal for justice in the clothing industry, to "let your flames die down to burning embers," keeping a "low-grade fire" that gives steady heat without consuming everything it touches. Her passion never faded, but she learned to express it in ways that "invited others into change rather than burning them with anger."[^21] Think of the passion you named at the opening. What would it look like for that fire to become embers this week, still burning but inviting rather than consuming?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The Zealots loved God with a fierce, jealous love and could not bear to see his honor trampled. They found warrant in Phinehas and Elijah, won a miraculous victory, and then watched it decay, until the cycle of violence ended in caves, cliffs and ruins. The episode names their error clearly: the myth of redemptive violence. It refuses, though, to look down on them from safety, and it insists that God has a place for the zealous. Jesus called Zealots to follow him and told Peter to put away his sword. The fire was never the problem; the weapons were.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one thing you are zealous about that you want to offer to God with kingdom methods?

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 80 — Silent Years — Pharisees](/guides/80)** (24:37): The final group is the Pharisees, who took the same zeal as the Zealots and poured it into absolute devotion and obedience to God's commands. The episode explores their communities in the Galilean "triangle" of Chorazin, Capernaum and Bethsaida, why Jesus spent most of his ministry among them, and the one piece the notes say they were missing.

**What to bring next week:** Read Matthew 11:20–24 and notice which towns Jesus names and what he says they failed to do. Think about how the Pharisees were usually described in the church where you first learned about them.
