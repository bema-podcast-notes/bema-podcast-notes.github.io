### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Eugenia Ortega let us look into her family's life with severe autism, and 1 Corinthians 12 reminded us that the members who seem weaker are indispensable to the body.[^1]

**This week:** Josh Bossé reads the book of Esther, where God is never named, and argues that the silence is the point: Esther's people are saved through wisdom, strategy and community.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Josh offers psychological portraits of the main characters. King Ahasuerus is insecure and easily led, doing whatever anyone suggests; Mordecai is a patient, long-term strategist whose people actually follow him; Haman combines unbridled ambition with dangerous insecurity, and in the end is completely alone.[^3] Josh notes that his reading of the king differs from Marty Solomon's earlier one.[^4] Behind the Mordecai and Haman conflict lies an older, unfinished story: Saul the Benjamite spared Agag, king of the Amalekites, and Haman is an Agagite.[^5]

The heart of the episode is Esther's plan. Rather than simply walking bravely into the throne room, she creates urgency, refuses to say what is wrong, and invites the king and Haman to private feasts, letting the king's insecurity and Haman's overconfidence do her work.[^6] Josh argues that if the king's sleepless night is read as a direct act of God, it undermines the book's message, and that the name of the festival, Purim ("lots" or "dice"), says the dice of fate are loaded toward community and against isolated ambition.[^7]

### An Old Feud and Three Characters <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#an-old-feud-and-three-characters}

*Esther's original audience would have heard two tribal names and known a conflict was coming.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Esther 3:1–6 and 1 Samuel 15:1–9 aloud. (Esther 2:5 names Mordecai's tribe.)*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** What do you notice when you hear these two passages side by side? Look at how Haman is identified, what Saul was told to do, and what Saul actually did. The notes say that for the original audience, hearing "Benjamite" and "Agagite" "would immediately signal inevitable conflict."[^5] What does knowing that backstory add to the scene where Mordecai will not bow?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** Josh reads King Ahasuerus as insecure and "intellectually limited" rather than cunning: he displays his wealth (and his wife) to impress, does whatever his advisors suggest, and celebrates with Haman while the city is in uproar. For Mordecai, the notes suggest, the king's pliability is valuable: "better a malleable fool than an ambitious tyrant like Haman."[^3] How does this portrait change the way you hear the rest of the story?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Mordecai's name derives from Marduk, the patron god of Babylon, "reminding readers this is not his first exile," and the notes call his refusal to bow a critical error that shows "even wise strategists can overplay their hand."[^8] What do you make of a hero who is wise and also wrong? Why might the book keep his mistake in plain view?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Haman's name may mean "glorious" or "to be alone," and both fit: he gathers followers only with money, and when his fortunes turn, even his wife abandons him.[^9] The notes call leadership built on power rather than relationship "not merely morally problematic but strategically foolish."[^10] Where in your own work, family or church are you tempted to rely on position or leverage instead of relationships? What would building real connection look like there?

</div>

### Esther's Brazen Silence <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#esthers-brazen-silence}

*Josh argues Esther's courage was matched by a carefully designed plan.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Esther 5:1–8 and Esther 6:1–3 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes point out that Esther uses the phrase "if it will be good for the king," language used elsewhere in Esther when someone is manipulating the king: his advisors use it to remove Vashti, and Haman uses it to sell out the Jewish people.[^11] (Your translation may read "if it pleases the king"; compare Esther 1:19 and 3:9.) What does Esther actually ask for in chapter 5, and what does she hold back? What do you notice about what the king does that night in chapter 6?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** In Josh's reading, Esther breaks protocol to signal urgency, then refuses to explain, so the king's mind goes back to the last time she brought him urgent news (the assassination plot). Inviting Haman feeds his ambition while planting the idea that the threat is someone close to the king. The king cannot sleep because he is trying to work out who is plotting against him, and Esther "never has to directly accuse Haman."[^6] What does this reading gain over the familiar picture of Esther simply being brave, and how does it change the way you see her?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes observe that Esther mirrors and reverses Vashti: Vashti was commanded to come to the king and refused; Esther was not commanded to come and went anyway.[^12] What does placing these two women side by side suggest about the book's view of power and courage?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes describe Esther's techniques as "value-neutral," appearing throughout politics and organizations, and list the ethics of her strategy as an open question: is manipulation justified when saving lives?[^13] Where do you draw the line between wise strategy and manipulation in your own life? Think of a situation where you have had to influence someone more powerful than you.

</div>

### Loaded Dice and the Silence of God <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#loaded-dice-and-the-silence-of-god}

*The book never names God, and Josh thinks that is exactly what it wants us to notice.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** Josh argues that if the king's sleeplessness is attributed to direct divine intervention, the book's message becomes "You can't do anything, just wait for God to show up and fix things." In a Joseph-style story, God would have sent a dream.[^7] Instead, the book shows salvation coming through wisdom, agency and community, and the notes connect this to modern believers who also live in an age when God seems silent.[^14] How does this land for you? What is gained, and what (if anything) feels at risk, in reading Esther this way?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Purim means "lots" or "dice," the instrument Haman used to pick the day of destruction. The notes call it dark humor to name a celebration after it, and say the book's message is that "the dice are actually loaded," not by direct intervention but "by the structure of reality": evil that isolates itself falls, and connected community becomes the channel for God's purposes.[^7] Where have you seen that pattern hold true?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q11:** Throughout Esther we know the names of the servants who carry messages, and the people fast together while Esther goes to the king.[^15] If community is how God's purposes move when he seems quiet, what is one connection in your life (a relationship, a group, a neighbor) you want to strengthen this month, and why that one?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> This week we read a book in which God is never named. Josh helped us see an insecure king, a patient strategist who overplayed his hand, and an ambitious man who was finally and completely alone. Esther did not wait for a miracle; she used wisdom, timing and a carefully kept silence, and her people fasted and stood with her. The dice Haman cast turned out to be loaded, not by a voice from heaven but by the way God's world works: isolation fails, and community endures. For anyone who feels that God has gone quiet, Esther offers a way to keep acting faithfully together.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, where do you most need to act with wisdom and community this week instead of waiting for God to fix something?

</div>
