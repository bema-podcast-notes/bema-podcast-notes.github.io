### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Hebrews showed us several pictures of the cross, all held together by one concern: the old sacrifices could not cleanse the conscience, and Jesus' sacrifice does. God gave sacrifice for our sake, not his.

**This week:** We turn to James, a letter many have read as arguing with Paul. The episode suggests James and Paul were simply having two different conversations.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode starts with who wrote James and to whom. It identifies the author as the leader of the Jerusalem church and the audience as Jewish believers "scattered among the nations," trying to keep their covenant identity while living among Gentiles. That setting changes how the famous faith-and-works passage reads.

The key distinction is between two kinds of "works." James speaks of *mitzvoth* (deeds of obedience to God's covenant), while Paul's "works of the law" refers to the markers that make someone Jewish, such as circumcision and the food laws. Paul asks whether Gentiles must become Jewish; James asks whether Jewish believers should keep living faithfully as Jews. The episode also suggests James's concern with the tongue may be about how Jewish believers spoke of Gentile believers, and it notes his care for the poor. Our traditions have read James 2 in different ways, which makes this a rich week to listen to one another.

### Twelve Tribes Scattered <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#twelve-tribes-scattered}

*The episode argues that knowing who James is and who he is writing to is the key to the whole letter.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read James 1:1 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes stress that James writes "to the twelve tribes scattered among the nations," a distinctly Jewish audience living in Gentile territory and struggling to keep their covenant identity in mixed settings.[^1] What do you notice about how James introduces himself and his readers? If you read the rest of the letter with this audience in mind, what kinds of pressures might they have faced day to day?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode describes three "pillars" after the resurrection: Peter leading the wider movement, James leading the Jewish church in Jerusalem, and John later pastoring the churches of Asia. It identifies this James as James son of Alphaeus and notes that the Hebrew sense of "brother" (Greek *adelphos*) can mean "kinsman" rather than a biological sibling.[^2] Christians have identified this James in more than one way. How does picturing him as the leader of a Jewish congregation in Jerusalem shape the way you hear his voice?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes describe these believers needing to keep dietary laws and Sabbath while living among Gentiles, "without creating walls against Gentile believers."[^3] Where do you feel the tension between keeping your own faithful practices and staying open to people who do not share them? What helps you hold both?

</div>

### Two Conversations About Works <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#two-conversations-about-works}

*The same English word, "works," may be naming two different things in James and in Paul.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read James 2:14–26 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes highlight three pictures in this passage: Abraham, whose faith was "made complete" in his willingness to offer Isaac; Rahab, a Gentile whose faith led her to protect God's people; and the person who offers only a verbal blessing to someone without food or clothes.[^4] Look back at the passage. What do the three have in common? Why might James pair the patriarch of Israel with a Gentile woman?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The episode contrasts James's *mitzvoth* ("deeds/acts of obedience to God's covenant") with Paul's *erga nomou* ("works of the law"), the practices that make one Jewish. In the notes' summary, "Paul addressed whether Gentiles needed to become Jewish; James addressed whether Jews should maintain their distinctiveness."[^5] How does that distinction change the way James 2 and Paul's letters to the Galatians and Romans sound side by side?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Our traditions have leaned on this passage differently. Some have stressed that faith is shown in obedient response, others that we are saved by faith apart from anything we do. The episode concludes that James "is not contradicting Paul's message of justification by faith," and that both are calling for authentic, lived-out faith.[^6] How has your tradition taught James 2? What does the episode's reading affirm in that teaching, and what does it unsettle?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** James's example of the person who says "go in peace" without meeting a real need is uncomfortably ordinary. Where is your faith most at risk of staying verbal? What is one concrete deed of obedience you could do this week for someone who needs more than words?

</div>

### Tongues, Tables, and the Poor <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#tongues-tables-and-the-poor}

*James's warnings about speech and wealth may have a specific community in view.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read James 3:5–10 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The episode suggests James's central concern may be "how Jewish believers spoke about Gentile believers": controlling speech so it does not undermine God's inclusive work, and using words that build unity rather than division.[^7] Read the passage again with that setting in mind. What images does James use for the tongue, and how do they sound if the danger is one group talking down another?

*Read James 2:1–7 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say James's teaching on wealth reflects Jewish covenant values: equal treatment regardless of income, preference for the poor, and critique of systems that favor the wealthy at others' expense.[^8] What do you notice about where the rich and poor are seated in James's example? Where do similar seating charts show up in churches today?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The episode describes hospitality that "extends beyond tribal boundaries."[^9] Think about the groups you belong to (church, family, workplace, political side). What is one thing you say, or let others say, about people outside your group that James would ask you to stop? What could you say instead?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> James wrote to Jewish believers scattered among the nations, people trying to stay faithful to the covenant without building walls against their Gentile brothers and sisters. His "works" are *mitzvoth*, the deeds that show faith is real, which is a different conversation from Paul's argument that Gentiles need not become Jewish. Abraham and Rahab stand side by side as people whose faith acted. For James, faith shows itself in how we feed the hungry, seat the poor, and speak about those outside our circle.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each: what is one deed, not just a word, that your faith is asking of you this week?

</div>
