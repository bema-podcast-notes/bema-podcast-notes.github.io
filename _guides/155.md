### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Paul's "therefore" turned eleven chapters of God's mercy into a call to be people of love: one living sacrifice, with love reaching fellow believers, enemies and even governing authorities.

**This week:** The Romans series ends with Paul's wisdom for living together across disputable matters, his own mission to the Gentiles, the women and men who worked beside him, and the shape of the whole letter.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This final Romans episode begins with the "weak" and the "strong" of Romans 14, which the notes say describe the scope of a person's convictions, not their spiritual maturity.[^1] It argues that the stumbling block principle is about how we treat one another, not a tool for enforcing our convictions on others.[^2] It then follows Paul's mission to show that Gentiles could worship the God of Israel alongside Jewish believers, including the collection from Gentile churches for Jewish believers in Jerusalem.[^3]

The closing greetings of Romans 16 name many co-workers, among them Phoebe, Priscilla and Junia, and the episode draws conclusions about women in Paul's ministry that our traditions weigh differently.[^4] Finally, the episode shows how the letter is bracketed by the phrase "obedience of faith" and centered on "no condemnation."[^5]

### Weak, Strong and Disputable Matters <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#weak-strong-and-disputable-matters}

*Paul gives practical wisdom for a church that disagrees about food and holy days.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Romans 14:1–13 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** What do the two groups in this passage do differently? What does Paul tell each group not to do in verse 3, and what reason does he give in verse 4? The notes say "weak" and "strong" are "terms describing scope of conviction, not spiritual maturity," and that "Neither position indicates greater or lesser spiritual maturity."[^6] How does that change the way the labels sound?

*Read Romans 14:19–23 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes say that, properly understood, "stumbling blocks are not about the disputable matter itself but about our treatment of each other." They name a common misapplication, using the idea "to enforce personal convictions on others," and say the principle "applies both ways - neither camp should create obstacles for the other."[^7] Where have you seen the phrase "stumbling block" used well, and where have you seen it used to win an argument?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say "Not everything fits into neat black-and-white moral categories" and that "The key is not the specific decision but how we treat others who decide differently."[^8] They mention food, special days and entertainment, and also church decisions about alcohol and holiday observances.[^9] Without debating any one issue, how do you tell the difference between a disputable matter and a matter where Scripture is clear?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes encourage "Keeping personal convictions 'between yourself and God'" and "Seeking to understand rather than judge those who make different choices."[^10] Think of a fellow believer whose convictions differ from yours. What is one way you could build them up this week rather than judge them?

</div>

### Paul's Mission and Paul's Co-Workers <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#pauls-mission-and-pauls-co-workers}

*Paul explains his calling and greets the people who share his work.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Romans 15:8–12 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Look at the four quotations in verses 9–12. If your Bible has cross-references, find where each comes from. What do they have in common? The notes say Paul "quotes from all sections of the Hebrew Bible (Law, Prophets, Writings) to show God's plan was always inclusive," and that his life mission was "to demonstrate that Gentiles could worship the God of Israel alongside Jewish believers."[^11]

*Read Romans 15:20–28 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe the collection as "Gentile churches supporting Jewish believers in Jerusalem as practical demonstration of unity."[^12] Why might money from Gentile churches carried to Jerusalem say something that a letter alone could not?

*Read Romans 16:1–7 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Notice the names and the descriptions attached to them. What roles and actions does Paul credit to the women he names? The notes describe Phoebe as a "Deacon and benefactor who likely carried the letter to Rome," Priscilla as a "Co-worker who risked her life for Paul," and Junia as "Outstanding among the apostles," adding that church history "attempted to masculinize this name."[^13] The episode concludes that this evidence "challenges interpretations that paint Paul as anti-women," and that his restrictive passages "must be understood in specific local contexts."[^14] Our traditions read Paul on women in different ways. How has your tradition understood names like these alongside Paul's other passages, and what do you notice in Romans 16 that you had not noticed before?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Paul aimed to go "where Christ has not yet been proclaimed," and the notes invite each of us to find "your unique calling within God's broader mission."[^15] Paul did not work alone; dozens of people shared his mission. Who has been a co-worker in your faith, and where might God be asking you to join someone else's work?

</div>

### The Shape of the Whole Letter <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#the-shape-of-the-whole-letter}

*The episode steps back to show how Romans is built.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Romans 1:5 aloud, then Romans 16:25–27.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** What phrase appears near the very beginning and the very end of the letter? The notes say the whole letter forms an *inclusio* (a literary bracket) around "obedience of faith," and that "The 'obedience of faith' involves unity between different groups, not earning salvation through works."[^16] How does that definition fit with everything we have read in Romans these past weeks?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes lay out the letter as a chiasm, a mirror-shaped structure. The front half shows what obedience is not: all fall short (1–3), all you do is trust (4–5), the struggle of flesh (6–7). The back half shows what it is: the struggle of clay (9–11), all you do is love (12–14), all can instruct (15–16). At the center stands Romans 8:1: "no condemnation for anyone in Christ Jesus."[^17] Which pairs speak to each other most clearly for you, and why might Paul put "no condemnation" at the center?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q11:** If the obedience of faith is unity between different groups, what is one division in your church, family or community where you could practice that obedience in a concrete way?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Over seven weeks we have read Romans as a letter to a blended family. Every group fell short of its own measuring stick; righteousness came from God as a gift to all who trust, as it did to Abraham; grace outpaced sin; nothing could separate us from God's love; God showed mercy to outsiders and grafted them into Israel's tree; and therefore we become people of love. Now Paul shows what that looks like among people who disagree, in a mission shared with women and men alike, and in a letter shaped around one center: no condemnation. The obedience of faith is not earning salvation but living as one family.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one thing from our weeks in Romans you want to keep living?

</div>
