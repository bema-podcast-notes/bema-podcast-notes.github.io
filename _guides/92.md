### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We met John the Baptist in Elijah's clothes, offering a public baptism of repentance, and left an honest question open about whether his fiery expectations matched what the Messiah came to do.

**This week:** Jesus steps into the Jordan himself and then into the wilderness, and the episode uncovers a pattern running all the way back to Genesis 1.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode traces a six-step template that appears in Scripture's great water stories: chaos, water, the Spirit over the water, God speaks, order is expected, and order is tested.[^1] It shows up at creation, the flood, the Exodus and the crossing of the Jordan, and every time the test ended in tragedy. At Jesus's baptism the Spirit hovers like a dove over the water and God speaks again, echoing Genesis 1, and this time the test in the wilderness ends in faithfulness.[^2]

The second half lines up Jesus's three temptations with Israel's three tests in the wilderness, each answered from Deuteronomy.[^3] The notes are careful to say this is not a story of "Jews failed, Jesus succeeded"; Israel stands for everyone who struggles to walk with God, and Jesus shows all of us what faithful trust looks like.[^4] The episode closes with a simple but demanding "formula": know the text, and walk the text.

**Opening Question:** Think of a time when something good finally came together for you, a new job, a fresh start, a hard-won decision, and then almost immediately something tested it. What happened, and how did you handle it?

### The Six-Step Pattern <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-six-step-pattern}

*Matthew tells Jesus's baptism in the language of the first day of creation.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 1:1–3 and Matthew 3:13–17 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes explain that at creation the earth was *tohu va-vohu* ("formless and void," or "wild and waste"), and the Spirit, the *ruach* (spirit, breath or wind), hovered over the waters.[^5] The word for that hovering, *merahefet*, describes fluttering "like a dove," and the notes say this image "hasn't appeared since the opening verses of Genesis."[^6] Put the two passages side by side. What words, images and actions do you notice that they share?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes walk the six steps through creation, the flood, the Exodus and the Jordan crossing, and point out that "Every time the six-step pattern appeared before Jesus, it ended in tragedy": Adam and Eve, Noah's family, Israel in the wilderness and Israel entering Canaan.[^7] In which of these earlier stories can you see the pattern most clearly, and what is Matthew saying about Jesus by telling his baptism this way?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes read Jesus's words "to fulfill all righteousness" as "I need to tell the world that I'm going to show them how to walk the path correctly."[^8] They describe Essene baptism as "A public declaration that 'if you watch me, I'm going to walk the path correctly,'" and they say plainly that "Christian baptism has different theological meaning."[^9] Our traditions teach about baptism in different ways. Without trying to settle those differences, what does this reading of Jesus's own baptism add for you, and where does it raise questions?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes say the pattern helps believers "Recognize that testing after breakthrough is normal, not a sign of failure."[^10] Think back to the opening question. How might it change things to expect a test after a new beginning rather than be surprised by it?

</div>

### Three Tests in the Wilderness <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#three-tests-in-the-wilderness}

*Jesus walks back through Israel's hardest days in the desert and answers every test from Deuteronomy.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 4:1–11 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes line up the three temptations with Israel's tests: bread with the manna of Exodus 16 and Deuteronomy 8:3; the leap from the temple with the water at Massah and Meribah in Exodus 17 and Deuteronomy 6:16; and the kingdoms with Israel's idolatry and Deuteronomy 6:13.[^11] Look at Jesus's three answers. What do you notice about where every one of them comes from, and why might that matter?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes connect the three tests to the three parts of the Shema: heart, soul and might.[^12] They insist that "This parallel is not about 'Jews failed, Jesus succeeded' replacement theology," and that "The Israelites represent all who struggle to walk with God."[^13] The original notes put it this way: "We must see the experience of the Israelites as an extension of our own experience."[^14] Why does that framing matter, both for how we talk about the Jewish people and for how we read Israel's failures as our own?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say that "Satan also knew and quoted Scripture (though he misquoted it)," quoting Psalm 91 in the second temptation.[^15] Read Psalm 91:11–12 aloud and compare it carefully with Matthew 4:6. What do you notice, and what does it tell you about how Scripture can be used against its own intent?

</div>

### Knowing and Walking the Text <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#knowing-and-walking-the-text}

*Knowing the right verse was not enough; Jesus had to trust it while he was hungry.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe the episode's tongue-in-cheek "formula": know the text, walk the text, trust God's story.[^16] They add that "The Spirit drove Jesus into the wilderness to be tested, but didn't provide the answers - Jesus's preparation did."[^17] What practices or habits have actually put Scripture inside you, so that it is there when you need it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes point out that "Jesus didn't just know 'man does not live on bread alone' - He actually trusted God enough to go hungry rather than use His power inappropriately."[^18] They ask, "Where in your life do you know what God says but struggle to trust/walk it?"[^19] Answer that question for yourself as specifically as you can.

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> From the first page of Genesis, God brings order out of chaos the same way: chaos, water, the Spirit hovering, God speaking, an invitation to partner with him, and then a test. Adam and Eve, Noah, the generation of the Exodus and the generation that crossed the Jordan all faced that test and fell. At the Jordan, the Spirit hovers like a dove over Jesus and God speaks again, and in the wilderness Jesus meets Israel's three tests with Israel's own Scripture. He shows every one of us, Jew and Gentile, what it looks like to know the text and walk it.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one passage of Scripture you want to have "in you" before your next test?

</div>
