### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Jacob wrestled at the Jabbok and became Israel, but his transformation was messy and unfinished. God renamed him a second time, perhaps the moment God finally took hold of his life.[^1]

**This week:** We enter the story of Jacob's sons. Joseph is not the spotless hero many of us learned about, Judah's story interrupts his, and a single Hebrew word exposes a family caught in a cycle of deception.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode reads Genesis 37–40 with fresh eyes. Drawing on Rabbi David Fohrman, the hosts argue that the problem with Joseph's coat was not its color but that he received a second one, a public sign that Jacob had chosen him as the heir. Joseph, at seventeen, brings bad reports about his brothers and shares his dreams in ways that provoke them. The brothers throw him into a pit and sell him, and the hosts ask why they are grazing near Shechem at all, since this family was meant to bless the nations.

Then the story stops to tell about Judah and Tamar. The notes trace the Hebrew word *nakar* ("recognize") through Genesis and show how it links these chapters to Jacob's own deceptions. In Egypt, Joseph rises in Potiphar's house and lands in prison, and the hosts ask hard questions about his motives. Many of us grew up with Joseph as a model of virtue, so this week invites us to notice what the text actually says and hold our earlier picture alongside it.

### The Coat and the Dreams <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-coat-and-the-dreams}

*Jacob's favoritism and Joseph's dreams set the brothers against each other.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 37:1–11 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Brent asks why the story does not list Leah's sons with Joseph. The notes observe that Joseph is with the sons of Bilhah and Zilpah, suggesting the mothers' rivalry has passed to the next generation. They also note that Joseph is about 17 and his brothers in their mid-20s, and that the bad report he brings is stronger language than simple tattling.[^3] What do you notice in these verses about Joseph's words and actions, and how his father and brothers respond?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** Following Fohrman, the notes say the issue is not the colorfulness of the coat. Every brother would have received a coat from their father: "The issue is that Joseph got a SECOND coat." By it Israel tells his sons that Rachel's sons are his favorites and that Joseph is the *bechor* (the chosen firstborn) who will receive a double portion.[^4] How does this reading change your understanding of why the brothers hated Joseph as deeply as verse 4 says?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe Joseph as narcissistic, egocentric and unwise toward his brothers, adding that even Israel questions his tact. They point out a reversal: Ishmael once mocked his brother, and now Joseph mocks his own.[^5] If you learned Joseph as a model of virtue, what in today's reading fits that picture and what complicates it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes list family favoritism as a modern parallel, creating long-term dysfunction and rivalry.[^6] Think of a family, team, workplace or church where someone was clearly favored. What did it do to the others, and what did it do to the one who was favored?

</div>

### Recognize This <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#recognize-this}

*Genesis 38 interrupts Joseph's story, and one word ties the two together.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 37:31–33 and Genesis 38:24–26 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes explain that *nakar*, the word for "recognize," is used eight times in Genesis. The first two uses are about Jacob deceiving others, the next two about Jacob being deceived (with Joseph's bloodied coat), and the next two about Judah. When this word appears in Genesis, the notes say, it is used to convict people of their sin, and when Judah heard it, it "would have rung like a gunshot in his ears."[^7] Listen for the word in both passages. What parallels do you notice between the two scenes, including the items each person is asked to recognize?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Brent asks why a story about Joseph suddenly jumps to Judah as an older man. The notes suggest Reuben, the actual firstborn, may have returned to find something he never would have approved, and that Judah's leaving to marry a Canaanite woman may be a sign of a rift in the family.[^8] Why might the storyteller set Judah's story right here, between Joseph going down to Egypt and Joseph arriving in Potiphar's house?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes point out that Tamar's name means palm tree, something true, straight and righteous, and that Judah admits she is more righteous than he is.[^9] When has someone quietly held up something you could not deny about yourself, and what did you do with it?

</div>

### The Lord Was with Joseph <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#the-lord-was-with-joseph}

*In Egypt, Joseph rises and falls, and the text keeps saying the Lord was with him.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 39:1–12 and 39:20–23 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes push back on the idea that Joseph refuses Potiphar's wife simply because he is a good person. They ask whether it is his character, his obsession with his own success, or "because she was the only thing in the house that Potiphar had not given him."[^10] Look closely at Joseph's reasons in verses 8–9. What do you notice about what he emphasizes, and how do you read his motives?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> In prison, Joseph asks the cupbearer to remember him, and the notes paraphrase this as "Remember me when you leave because I want to get out of here!" Marty adds that he can see this being fair, since he cannot imagine anyone behaving differently.[^11] Brent asks why God keeps dwelling with such a "yucky" family, and the notes answer that "G-d has perspective about this family we may not have as we read these stories."[^12] What might God see in this family that is hard for us to see?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes end with questions: "It feels like this story is spinning out of control. Will it find its way back?"[^13] They also say God's favor does not guarantee an easy life.[^14] Think of a season when your own story felt like it was spinning out of control. Looking back, where (if anywhere) can you now see that the Lord was with you, and where is that still hard to see?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The family that was meant to bless the nations is now a stench to their neighbors, divided by mothers' rivalries and a father's favoritism. Joseph, gifted and chosen, is also proud and unwise, and his brothers throw him into a pit. Judah's story interrupts with a word, "recognize," that has followed this family since Jacob deceived his father, and for the first time someone hears it and admits that Tamar is "more righteous than I." Through it all the text insists the Lord was with Joseph. The question the notes leave us with is whether this story can find its way back.[^15]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one family or personal pattern you would love to see God interrupt?

</div>
