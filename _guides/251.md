### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** The pilot of *The Chosen* sent us back to Luke 2, where a limping shepherd and his rough companions were the first to see the Messiah while most people missed him.[^1]

**This week:** John Roberts, whose 18-year-old daughter Olivia died in a car accident three days before she was to leave for college, talks about what grief has taught him about God, Scripture and friendship.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

John describes grief as neither an enemy to defeat nor a friend to get comfortable with, but a companion on a journey that cannot be avoided or rushed.[^3] He speaks honestly about outgrowing a bumper-sticker theology ("God said it. I believe it. And that settles it."), about rereading passages like Jeremiah 29:11 in their setting, and about the questions he first asked in the emergency room: why, and where was God.[^4] He talks about guilt, about the friends who carried his family, and about the wisdom of entering "the house of mourning" in Ecclesiastes 7.[^5]

Most tenderly, he says he finds more meaning in Jesus weeping with Mary and Martha than in Jesus raising Lazarus.[^6] Grief is personal, and some in this room may be carrying fresh or old losses. Every question here is an invitation, never a requirement: you can pass, speak in general terms, or simply listen. No one needs to fix anyone else's grief tonight.

### Opening <span class="guide-time">10 min</span> {#opening}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-opening" markdown="1">

**Opening Question:** Think of a hard season when someone showed up for you, or when you watched someone show up for another person. What did they do (or not do) that helped?

</div>

### Neither Enemy nor Friend <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#neither-enemy-nor-friend}

*John begins by rejecting two common ways of relating to grief.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** John rejects the idea of grief as an enemy to be fought and overcome quickly, an approach he says is especially common among men and leads to shortcuts and avoidance. He also rejects grief as a comfortable friend. Instead it is a presence that "must be acknowledged and engaged," and healing requires "going toward the pain rather than away from it."[^3] What have you been taught, by family, church or culture, about what to do with sadness? How does John's picture compare?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> John and his wife grieved in different ways. She journaled her emotions and prayers and gathered with friends; he withdrew, in a healthy way, to meditate, think and work through his questions. The notes say both "were valid and necessary."[^7] Why might differences like these strain a family or friendship, and what helps people honor each other's ways of grieving?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** John says the chapter on guilt was so hard to write that he shook while typing. His breakthrough came when he realized, "it's not grasping me, I'm grasping it": the grip came from his desire for answers, his anger, and his need to answer "why," which led back to self-blame.[^8] Without needing to share specifics, what do you think it means to be the one holding on to guilt? How might that realization change what a person can do with it?

</div>

### The House of Mourning <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-house-of-mourning}

*Wisdom literature and the Jewish practice of sitting shiva teach the ministry of presence.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Ecclesiastes 7:1–4 and Job 2:11–13 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** What do you notice in these two passages? In Ecclesiastes, compare what the writer says about the house of mourning and the house of feasting. In Job, notice what the friends do during the first seven days, and what they do not do. John admits he might once have thought the writer of Ecclesiastes "had a bad day," but now, having "seen some things," he believes the writer was right.[^5]

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** John draws on Job's friends, who were most helpful in the first week when they simply sat in silence. The key, he says, is not avoiding a grieving person for fear of saying the wrong thing, but showing up and using whatever gifts you have: someone mowed his lawn, others brought meals or helped with logistics.[^9] The notes connect this to sitting *shiva*, the Jewish practice of showing up, "saying less, and worrying about doing the right thing less."[^10] Why is it so hard to sit with someone without trying to fix things or find the right words? What makes simple presence so powerful?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Using biblical names, John describes his "Jonathans" (encouraging friends), "Nathans" (truth-tellers) and "Abigails" (wise counselors).[^11] Who has played one of those roles in your life? Which role do you tend to play for others?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Think of someone you know who is grieving right now, recently or long ago, since grief has no deadline. Given your own gifts (practical help, a meal, a note, a walk, quiet company), what is one way you could show up for them in the next two weeks?

</div>

### Rethinking God in Grief <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#rethinking-god-in-grief}

*John's loss sent him back to Scripture with new questions, and he found a God who meets him there.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read John 11:32–36 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Notice what Mary says to Jesus, what Jesus sees, and how he responds before anything else happens. John says he gets more meaning from Jesus grieving with Mary and Martha than from Jesus raising Lazarus, because it shows "incarnational presence" walking with us in grief.[^6] Why might a God who weeps comfort a grieving person more than a display of power?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Many Christians claim Jeremiah 29:11 as a personal promise. Through his study, John came to see it as a word to a specific people facing 70 years of exile, revealing God's faithfulness "even in the midst of prolonged difficulty," and the notes say that reading was actually more helpful to him than a surface reading "that reality has contradicted."[^12] (Jeremiah 29:4–14 gives the setting.) Why might the verse in its context hold more weight for someone in deep loss?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** John presents his questions (Why did this happen? Was this God's will? Where was God?) as "fair and worthy" for anyone in trauma, and the notes say asking them is "essential and spiritually healthy" even without immediate answers.[^4] Our traditions handle hard questions about God differently. What permission have you been given, or not given, to ask God "why"? What do you think changes when a community makes room for those questions?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q11:** Answering a question from a book his daughter Emily gave him, John wrote: "The most rewarding journey is when one ventures inwardly, a spiritual journey, an in-depth exploration of the soul, a destination unknown where few dare to venture."[^13] What might one honest step on that inward journey look like for you this week, whether or not you are grieving right now?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> This week John Roberts let us into the hardest season of his life. Grief, he told us, is neither enemy nor friend but a companion that must be faced rather than avoided. He found that guilt was something he was grasping, that friends who simply showed up mattered more than perfect words, and that the house of mourning holds a wisdom the house of feasting cannot give. He left behind a theology too small for his loss and found a God who counsels, comforts and weeps alongside us. His invitation is to show up for one another and to take the inward journey few dare to make.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, name one thing from John's story you want to remember the next time you are with someone who is grieving.

</div>
