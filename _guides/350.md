**Last week:** The seventh reflection closed the series with rest, asking whether we trust God enough to set down our gifts and stop, and whether we know Sabbath well enough to pass it on.

**This week:** The format changes from guided reflection to conversation, as Kat Armstrong talks about following images like stones, mountains and valleys through the whole biblical story.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This episode is an interview rather than a reflection on one passage. Kat Armstrong, a Bible teacher and author, talks with Marty and Brent about her six-book *Storyline Bible Studies*, which come in three pairs: *Mountains & Valleys*, *Sticks & Stones* and *Sinners & Saints*. Each pair follows one strand of imagery through the whole Bible. The series grew from a passing comment in Jonathan Pennington's work about mountains in Matthew and from many questions asked by Kat's young son, Caleb.

Marty builds the conversation around three words from his endorsement of the series (fresh, trustworthy and consistent) and adds a fourth at the end: the series equips readers to keep studying without it. Along the way the conversation touches on why many believers feel bored with their faith, deconstruction and "reconstruction," why children's concrete reading is close to the Hebrew way of thinking, and how biblical characters are "a little bit of both." The key conviction is that the Bible is one coherent story, and seeing its unity can help people put their faith back together.

### Once You See It, You Can't Unsee It <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#once-you-see-it-you-cant-unsee-it}

*Kat believes the people, places and things in Scripture are there for a reason, and noticing them can make a familiar story new.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 28:10–22 aloud, then sit together in silence for one minute.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Kat says she used to read Jacob's dream at Bethel only for the angels and the stairway, and never noticed the stone[^1]. Go back through the passage and follow the stone. What happens to it, from the beginning of the story to the end? What did you see this time that you had not noticed before?

*Read 1 Peter 2:4–5 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Stones are Kat's favorite volume. She sees a movement across the story: in the Old Testament people gather, stack, break and rebuild stones; in the New Testament the stone is rolled away from Jesus' tomb and Peter calls believers "living stones"; and throughout, God is "the Rock," whose character "does not crack"[^2]. What does an image gain when it keeps coming back across many books and centuries? How does Bethel's stone change for you when you hear it alongside Peter's living stones?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes record Kat's observation that children meet the Bible mostly as stories, not "like an IKEA manual," and Marty's point that this concrete reading is closer to how the original audience related to the text[^3]. Kat's son once handed her a sticky note of future themes: wine, water, boats, tables and vineyards[^4]. Choose one familiar passage you will read this week. What object or place will you watch for, and who (perhaps a child) could you invite to look with you?

</div>

### Fresh, Trustworthy, Consistent and Equipping <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#fresh-trustworthy-consistent-and-equipping}

*Marty's four words describe what good Bible study does for the people who use it.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Every *Storyline* lesson moves through four steps adapted from Ray Lubeck: context (the passage's setting and the reader's own setting), observation, interpretation, and application. Kat calls interpretation a "slow build," and notes that by the application stage many readers have already worked some of it out themselves[^5]. Which of these four steps does your usual Bible reading tend to skip? What happens to your reading when it does?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Kat wrote the stones study while watching Russia's invasion of Ukraine and seeing refugee children cross into Poland, and the Psalms' picture of God as an unshakable rock spoke directly to that fragile moment[^6]. When has something happening in your own world changed the way a passage read to you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Kat observes that trust in institutions is at an all-time low and that many people tear out "rotten planks" with no clear plan to rebuild. Her website's tagline, "put your faith back together," names the hope that seeing the story's unity rebuilds trust in it[^7]. Marty values resources that are well researched yet can be used across church traditions without "throwing stones"[^8]. Our group spans several traditions. What makes a Bible resource trustworthy to you, and what makes you feel you need to add "asterisks" before using it? If you or someone you love has been pulling out planks, what has actually helped to rebuild?

</div>

### A Little Bit of Both <span class="guide-time">10 min</span> {#a-little-bit-of-both}

*Kat's character studies and her earlier books show the story meeting complicated people in hard places.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> In *The In-Between Place*, Kat pairs Dinah (Genesis 34) with the woman at the well (John 4), linking them by location, Shechem and Sychar. She points out reversals: Dinah is silent while the Samaritan woman has what may be the longest recorded conversation with Jesus; a city is slaughtered in one story and experiences revival in the other[^9]. Skim John 4:4–42 together. What other reversals or echoes do you notice?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** *Sinners & Saints* grew from Caleb asking whether Anakin Skywalker was a good guy or a bad guy. Kat's "saints" are religious insiders who missed what God was doing, and she found she could relate to them; her "sinners" are people written off in their own setting who showed surprising faith[^10]. In this season of your life, which do you recognize in yourself more easily, and how might that change the way you see someone you have written off?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Kat Armstrong's work starts from a conviction that the Bible is one story held together by things we can see and touch: mountains, valleys, trees, stones and complicated people. Once you see the stone at Bethel, you cannot unsee it, and the image keeps gathering meaning until believers become living stones built on the Rock. Good study does more than pass on information; it builds habits of context, observation, interpretation and application, so readers can keep going on their own. Kat imagines readers who no longer need her books, who pull out a concordance and trace a theme like "boats" for themselves[^11].
{: .guide-summary}

*Sit together in silence for one minute before the closing question.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** Going around the room in one sentence each, with no comments until everyone has spoken: what image would you like to trace through the Bible for yourself?

</div>
