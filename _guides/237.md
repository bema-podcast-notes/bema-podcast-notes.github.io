### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We read Acts 21 as the clearest statement that Paul himself lived as a Torah-observant Jew, and we heard sacrifice described as a whole process for drawing near to God rather than a death alone.[^1]

**This week:** Two more chapters of *Understanding the Jewish Roots of Christianity*. Dr. Isaac W. Oliver shows that church and synagogue parted slowly and unevenly, and Rabbi Dr. Eugene Korn traces the painful history of Christian anti-Judaism that followed.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The first half of the episode questions the idea of a single, clean split between Judaism and Christianity. Paul accepted discipline from Jewish authorities, James calls a Christian gathering a "synagogue," Roman tax policy pushed communities apart, and Torah-observant followers of Jesus probably persisted in some places until the seventh century.[^3] Brent compares the process to an S-curve of technology adoption: a slow start, a rapid middle, and a long tail that never quite reaches 100%.[^4]

The second half is hard listening. Korn describes an "adversus judaeos" (against the Jews) tradition that dominated Christian theology for about 1,500 years, from Augustine's teaching about the curse of Cain to cathedral art, forced disputations, massacres, Martin Luther's writings, and the conditions that made the Holocaust possible.[^5] The episode also traces how Jewish thought about Christianity changed over time, and it ends with cautious hope about post-Holocaust dialogue.[^6] This is history that belongs to every branch of the church, so we come to it to understand, not to assign blame to someone else's tradition.

### A Parting, Not a Split <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#a-parting-not-a-split}

*Oliver argues that the separation of church and synagogue happened gradually, region by region, for many reasons at once.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read James 2:1–4 aloud, then 2 Corinthians 11:24.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes point out that the Greek of James 2:2 uses the word "synagogue" for a Christian gathering, though most modern translations render it "assembly," and that Paul's receiving lashes from Jewish authorities five times shows he still submitted to Jewish jurisdiction.[^3] Look at the wording in your own Bible for James 2:2. What does your translation call the gathering, and what picture of the earliest followers of Jesus do these two verses give you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Brent's S-curve has a slow ramp, a rapid acceleration around events like the *fiscus Judaicus* (the Jewish tax), the Bar Kokhba revolt and Constantine, and a long tail of Jewish followers of Jesus into the seventh century.[^4] Where on that curve do you think most Christians today imagine the split happened, and what does the long tail add to the picture?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes list theological, political, economic and social pressures behind the separation. Nerva's reform in 96 CE, for example, meant Jewish apostates were no longer regarded as Jews by Rome, and Christian enthusiasm that asked Gentiles to abandon their ancestral gods was seen as threatening Jewish communities' standing.[^7] Why might it matter to tell the story as a slow, many-sided parting rather than as one dramatic break? What changes about how we think of Judaism and of our own origins?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes record that some Christians in the early centuries attended both church and synagogue (John Chrysostom condemned it), and Brent suggests there is value in learning from other faith perspectives.[^8] Where in your own life do you have the chance to learn from a tradition other than your own, and what helps you do that with both openness and conviction?

</div>

### The Teaching of Contempt <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-teaching-of-contempt}

*Korn shows how theological contempt for Judaism grew, over centuries, into contempt for Jewish people and then into violence.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Korn describes the *Ecclesia et Synagoga* figures carved in European cathedrals: the church triumphant, erect, crowned and clear-sighted, the synagogue downcast and blindfolded, with a broken staff and falling scriptures.[^5] What would a worshiper absorb from walking past those figures every week, even without hearing a single sermon on the subject?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes name a "central conundrum" in this tradition: "If Jews no longer had a divine mission, how to explain Jewish survival?" Augustine's answer was that Jews bear the curse of Cain.[^5] What does it reveal about a theology when it has to explain away the continued life of a people?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The episode makes a sobering claim: while Nazism was not Christian ideology, Christian theology "played a necessary role by convincing Christians that Jews were rejected by God and deserved scorn," after 1,500 years of anti-Judaism.[^9] The notes trace this through crusaders, blood libels and papal decrees, and also through Martin Luther, whose own church in Wittenberg bore a *Judensau* relief.[^10] How do ideas taught in church become permission for how people are treated outside it? Without pointing at any other tradition, what responsibility does this history place on us today in the way we preach, teach and talk casually about Jews and Judaism?

</div>

### Learning From Relationship <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#learning-from-relationship}

*The episode also traces how Jewish thinkers came to see Christianity, and it notes how much relationship changes understanding.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes describe how rabbinic thought moved over the centuries from regarding Jewish followers of Jesus as heretics, to ruling that Christians were not idolaters, to appreciating Christianity as something positive for Gentiles. They connect this to the Jewish teaching that "each covenant is independently valid" and that Gentiles need not convert to Judaism for God's love.[^6] What strikes you about that generosity, given the history we have just discussed?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Maimonides is noted for developing views on Christianity "without actually conversing with Christians," which the notes say shows "how relationship versus mere book knowledge affects interfaith understanding."[^6] Where have you formed opinions about a group of people mainly from books, sermons or headlines rather than from knowing them?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The episode ends with the hope that post-Holocaust dialogue has created "unprecedented opportunities for mutual understanding and appreciation."[^11] What is one concrete step you could take this season toward actual relationship or understanding with Jewish neighbors, writers or communities?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> This week we saw that church and synagogue did not part in a single moment. They drifted apart slowly, pushed by theology, politics, money and fear, and some Jewish followers of Jesus held on for centuries. We also faced what came after: a teaching of contempt, carved in stone and preached from pulpits, that helped prepare the ground for violence and finally for the Holocaust. That history belongs to all of us who bear the name Christian. The episode does not end in despair, though, because it shows that honest relationship can begin to heal what centuries of contempt broke.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one thing from this history you want to remember the next time you hear Jews or Judaism spoken about in church?

</div>
