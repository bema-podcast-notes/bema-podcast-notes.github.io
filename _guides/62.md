### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Ezekiel, whose name means "God strengthens," showed God's glory leaving the ruined temple and going east into exile with the people. He also introduced apocalyptic literature: images and symbols that convey hope to the present.

**This week:** Daniel uses two languages and a carefully built structure to tell God's people how to stay faithful under empire, and at its center stands "one like a son of man."

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This episode reads the book of Daniel through its shape. Daniel is written in two languages, Hebrew and Aramaic, and the shifts between them tell a story of exile and return before any story is read.[^1] The book is also built as a "double chiasm": two mirror-image structures that together form a third, with Daniel 7:13–14 and "one like a son of man" at the center of the whole.[^2]

The hosts suggest that the book's purpose is to encourage perseverance and faithfulness during suffering and empire rather than primarily to predict end-times events.[^3] The episode also proposes that Daniel may have been written much later than the Babylonian exile, possibly in the 2nd century BC, using the exile as its setting.[^4] Christians hold different views on that question, and you do not need to settle it to hear the book's message: every empire falls, pride brings rulers down, and God is present with his people in the fire.

### Names and Languages <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#names-and-languages}

*Before we read a single famous story, Daniel's names and languages are already telling us something.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Daniel 1:1–7 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes point out that the empire renamed Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah as Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego. The Babylonian names praised Babylonian gods, "a form of cultural bullying and forced assimilation."[^5] The notes add: "That we know them primarily by their Babylonian names rather than their Hebrew names reflects how deeply empire can affect identity and memory."[^6] What do you notice in these verses about everything the empire changes in these young men's lives? Why do you think the names most of us learned as children are the Babylonian ones?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes explain the book's languages. Chapter 1 is in Hebrew, the language of God's people; chapters 2–7 are in Aramaic, the common secular language of the ancient Near East; and chapters 8–12 return to Hebrew.[^7] The shift creates "a literary journey from Jewish identity (Hebrew) into the experience of exile and loss of identity (Aramaic), then back to restoration and hope (Hebrew)."[^8] How might a Jewish reader have felt when the Scriptures slipped into the empire's language halfway through the book, and then came back home?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes' application asks us to consider "how dominant cultures attempt to reshape the identities of marginalized groups, and the importance of preserving and honoring original names and identities."[^9] What names or labels has the culture around you tried to give you, and what is one name God has given you that you want to keep answering to?

</div>

### Kings Who Fall <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#kings-who-fall}

*The stories in Daniel 2–7 are arranged like a mirror, and the middle of the mirror is a surprise.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Daniel 4:28–37 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes lay out the first chiasm, which covers the mostly Aramaic chapters:[^10]

- Chapter 2: a four-part statue representing kingdoms
- Chapter 3: refusal to worship, thrown into the furnace
- Chapter 4: the fall of Nebuchadnezzar
- Chapter 5: the fall of Belshazzar
- Chapter 6: refusal to worship, thrown into the lions' den
- Chapter 7: four beasts representing kingdoms

At its center sits Daniel 4:37, which the notes say "places words of worship and acknowledgment of God's justice in the mouth of the prideful, evil King Nebuchadnezzar."[^11] Look again at verse 37. What do you notice about who is speaking and what he says? Why might the author put this confession at the center, in the mouth of the empire's own king?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes read the furnace and the lions' den as mirror stories: both involve refusing to worship as the empire demands, being thrown into mortal danger, God's protection, and the empire acknowledging God's power.[^12] The application, though, is careful: these stories encourage faithfulness while "trusting in God's presence in the midst of suffering rather than necessarily His deliverance from it."[^13] Why does that distinction matter? Where have you had to trust God's presence when deliverance did not come, or did not come quickly?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The episode challenges the traditional view of Daniel as a 6th-century exilic book and suggests a 2nd-century BC date, with the Babylonian exile serving as a backdrop or "stage" for later challenges under other empires. The notes say this would help explain passages like Daniel 9:25–27, which refer to events that do not fit within the Babylonian exile.[^14] Our traditions, and scholars generally, hold different views here, and the notes present this as the episode's suggestion. Whatever date you hold, the book's message stays the same: "No political power, economic system, or cultural empire lasts forever. Placing ultimate hope in any human kingdom leads to disappointment."[^15] What helps you, or troubles you, about hearing Daniel as a word for people under any empire in any century? What human "kingdom" are you most tempted to place your hope in?

</div>

### One Like a Son of Man <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#one-like-a-son-of-man}

*At the very center of the whole book stands a single vision.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Daniel 7:9–14 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes show how the two chiasms combine into one structure across all twelve chapters, with the prophecies of the beasts in chapters 7 and 8 at the center, and Daniel 7:13–14 as the central text.[^16] According to the notes, this figure comes with the clouds of heaven, approaches the Ancient of Days, receives authority, glory, and sovereign power, is worshiped by all nations and peoples, and establishes a kingdom that will never be destroyed.[^17] Compare this figure with the beasts earlier in chapter 7. What do you notice about where each comes from, how each gets power, and how long each lasts?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say this figure "becomes central to Jewish eschatological hope and is heavily referenced by Jesus in the Gospels." The promise is that someone will come who will stand before God, render judgment, and "make everything right in the world."[^18] Knowing Daniel 7, what might Jesus' first listeners have heard when he called himself "the Son of Man"?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes close with an invitation for every generation facing the pressures of empire "to set their faces resolutely toward tomorrow, standing firm in faith" until the day they see one like a son of man coming.[^19] What would it look like for you to set your face toward tomorrow in one specific part of your life this week? Who around you needs to hear that someone is coming to make everything right?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Daniel was written for people living under an empire that renamed them, fed them, educated them, and demanded their worship. Its very languages walk from home into exile and back again. Its stories are arranged so that a proud king confesses God's justice, and faithful people discover God with them in the fire and the lions' den. At the center of it all, beyond every beast and every kingdom that rises and falls, stands one like a son of man whose kingdom will never be destroyed. Every empire is temporary; that kingdom is not.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one way you will refuse to bow to the empire around you this week?

</div>
