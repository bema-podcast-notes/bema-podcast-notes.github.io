### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Genesis 21 and 22 set two parents side by side. Hagar left her son and sat a bowshot away; Abraham walked beside Isaac and answered him with *hineni* ("here I am"), the same word the notes connect to God's own presence with his people.[^1]

**This week:** The story passes to the next generation. Abraham's servant looks for a wife for Isaac by testing for hospitality, and Isaac, repeating some of his father's mistakes, begins to show the nations what God is like.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode reads two chapters about Isaac. In Genesis 24, Abraham's senior servant (the notes call him Eliezer) travels to the household of Nahor and prays for a sign that sounds almost absurd: a young woman who will offer to water all ten of his camels. Rebekah does exactly that, and the notes see in her the same "spiritual DNA" of extravagant hospitality that marked Abraham.

In Genesis 26, Isaac repeats his father's wife-and-sister deception, yet his story seems to run in reverse order to Abraham's, as if he is redeeming it. He stays in the land during famine instead of going to Egypt, and when local herders fill in or claim his wells, he gives them up and digs again. In the end the Philistine king comes asking for peace. The promise that Abraham's family would bless the nations is starting to happen.

### A Prayer About Camels <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#a-prayer-about-camels}

*Abraham's servant goes looking for a wife for Isaac, and the test he chooses tells us what this family values.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 24:1–4 and 24:10–21 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes do some arithmetic on Rebekah's offer. Giving a traveler a drink would have been ordinary, but camels would need at least ten trips each, so watering ten of them meant about 100 trips down into the well and back up for animals that were not hers.[^3] As you listen to the servant's prayer and Rebekah's response, what do you notice about what he asks for and what he leaves out?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes explain that "hand under the thigh" meant swearing on the sign of the covenant, Abraham's circumcision. They also point out that Eliezer stands to inherit nothing, yet "he is as much a part of Avraham's family as anyone else in the family."[^4] What does the servant's role in this story suggest about who belongs to Abraham's family and what makes someone part of it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes put the servant's reasoning this way: "I'm looking for someone who is willing to go down into a cistern 100 times in order to water a camel." Rebekah's whole household then takes in the caravan and feeds the animals, showing they are made of the same "stock" as Abraham.[^5] If someone designed a "camel test" to reveal the character you hope to have, what would it be, and how would you do on it right now?

</div>

### Repeating the Father's Story <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#repeating-the-fathers-story}

*Isaac stumbles where his father stumbled, yet the shape of his story suggests something is being redeemed.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 26:1–11 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes observe that Isaac tells Abimelek his wife is his sister, just as Abraham did twice, and they ask, "How many of us repeat the sins of our parents?" They also notice a difference: Isaac stays in the land rather than going down to Egypt, and he becomes wealthy there.[^6] What do you notice in this passage about how Isaac's situation is like his father's and how it is different?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes point out that Isaac's episodes seem to happen in reverse order to Abraham's: the sister deception (Genesis 20), then a quarrel over water like Abraham's quarrel with Lot (Genesis 13), then an altar at Beersheba like the beginning of Abraham's story (Genesis 12). They suggest Isaac is redeeming his father's mistakes. They also note that "Abimelek" means "my father is king," so this may be a son or grandson of the king Abraham met.[^7] What might it mean for a son to walk back through his father's story and handle parts of it differently?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Each of us carries patterns from those who came before us, some to keep and some we would rather not pass on. Share only what you are comfortable sharing: what is one inherited pattern you are grateful for, and one you are working, like Isaac, to live out differently?

</div>

### Wells and Witness <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#wells-and-witness}

*In the well disputes, the mission to bless the nations becomes visible.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 26:17–33 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes retell the cycle in plain terms: the herders say "We don't like your well so we're going to fill it with dirt," Isaac redigs it, they claim it, he gives it to them and digs another, and it happens again. The notes conclude that Isaac shows the people how hospitable and loving God is.[^8] Walk through the passage together. What does Isaac do each time, and what finally happens with Abimelek at the end of the chapter?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes see God's promise to bless all nations through Abraham happening "right in front of our eyes," even in spite of the people being blessed. Water was a matter of survival, which makes giving up a well costly.[^9] Why might yielding something this valuable become a witness to the Philistines rather than a sign of weakness?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes are honest about why this is hard: "We don't trust the story enough. We don't believe that G-d will bless us maybe because it takes too long and it's painful."[^10] What "well" are you holding onto or fighting over right now? Which of your wells might be worth releasing, which might not, and what helps you tell the difference?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Abraham's family carries a distinctive kind of hospitality, the kind that walks into a cistern a hundred times for a stranger's camels. Isaac inherits that, and he also inherits his father's weaknesses. Yet he stays in the land, and when his wells are taken he keeps moving on and digging again, until the outsiders who opposed him come asking for peace. The notes call this a story of humanity, and also a sign that God's way of blessing the nations works, even when it takes too long and hurts.[^11]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one small act of "camel-watering" hospitality or one "well" you could give up this week?

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

He trades for Esau's birthright, takes his brother's blessing by deception, dreams of a ladder at Bethel, and meets his match in Laban. The episode asks why God would choose such a flawed man, and suggests God may prefer a fire in the belly to apathy.[^12]
