### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** The Psalms gave Israel a song for every season, from joy to lament to rage, and we were warned against the silence of harps hung in the trees.

**This week:** Two more tools for the journey: Proverbs for wisdom in daily life, and Ecclesiastes for the meaning that gets us out of bed in the morning.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode frames the wisdom literature as three tools for surviving the redemption cycle: song (Psalms), wisdom (Proverbs), and meaning (Ecclesiastes). Proverbs are "wise sayings that are generally true," real wisdom with real exceptions, and the book sets up a contest between Lady Wisdom, whom ancient rabbis linked with Torah, and the adulteress of folly. Among its themes, the episode lingers on speech, summed up in Marty's blunt rule: "When in doubt, shut up."

Ecclesiastes looks depressing on the surface, but the episode reads it as an answer to the question "What remains after we're gone?" A good name outlasts fine perfume, and the house of mourning teaches more than the house of feasting. The Jewish practice of Sitting Shiva, simply being present with the grieving, shows what that looks like in practice.

### Wise Sayings That Are Generally True <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#wise-sayings-that-are-generally-true}

*A proverb is real wisdom, not a guarantee.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Proverbs 10:1–5 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The episode defines proverbs as "wise sayings that are generally true." They are "Not comprehensive or universal," "Not for doctrinal statements," and "Situationally valuable."[^1] One of its examples is Proverbs 10:3, "The Lord will not let the righteous go hungry," which is generally true, though "there have been righteous people who experienced hunger."[^2] Go back through the five verses you just heard. Which ones ring true in your experience, and where do exceptions come to mind?

*Read Proverbs 9:1–6 aloud, then read Proverbs 9:13–18 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say Proverbs 1–9 presents wisdom and temptation as "two competing women": Lady Wisdom, representing "Torah, obedience, and the way of life," and the adulteress, representing "foolishness, folly, and the way of death." Some read the Proverbs 31 woman as "the culmination of Lady Wisdom," wisdom "embodied in daily, practical life," rather than a checklist for women.[^3] What do you notice about how each woman calls out, what she offers, and where her house leads? How does reading Proverbs 31 this way change it for you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes warn that proverbs "shouldn't be used to create rigid theological positions."[^4] Most of us have heard a proverb used as a promise, about children turning out well, or about the diligent becoming wealthy. When have you seen a proverb treated as a guarantee, and what did that do to the people involved? Which proverb do you need to hold more loosely, or more tightly, in your own life?

</div>

### Words and Restraint <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#words-and-restraint}

*The theme of speech runs all through Proverbs, and the episode takes it personally.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Proverbs 10:19 aloud, then read Proverbs 17:27 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Marty says this theme struck him in Bible college. As someone who "makes a living with words," he learned that "pride and words do not go well together." His rule: "When in doubt, shut up," because "if you don't know for certain that God needs you to say something, you can always rest securely in the decision to remain silent."[^5] What do you notice in these two proverbs about the connection between words, sin, and temperament? Why might pride and many words travel together?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Among the questions the notes raise is "What does 'when in doubt, shut up' mean in an age of constant commentary?"[^6] Think about your week ahead: a conversation, a meeting, a family table, or a screen. Where would restraint be the wisest word you could offer?

</div>

### A Good Name and the House of Mourning <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#a-good-name-and-the-house-of-mourning}

*Beneath the "meaningless" refrain, Ecclesiastes asks what will last.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes describe the familiar reading of Ecclesiastes as a "bait and switch": everything is meaningless without God at the center. The episode adds a deeper layer, saying the book is really addressing "What remains after we're gone?"[^7] How have you heard Ecclesiastes taught before? What changes if you read it as a book about legacy?

*Read Ecclesiastes 7:1–4 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Listen for the pairs this passage compares. The notes explain that "Fine perfume (costly possessions) gets passed on when you die," while "A good name, character, and impact on others can have eternal ripples," and that "Nobody remembers the parties you threw, but they remember when you showed up in their grief."[^8] Which comparison surprises or troubles you most? Why would the wise heart be found in the house of mourning?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode does not dismiss celebration. "Party matters: We must celebrate to remember where we came from," drawing on Leviticus, but "Presence matters more" when we have to choose.[^9] How do you hold joy and lament together without letting one crowd out the other?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes describe Sitting Shiva, the Jewish practice of presence with the grieving: "Just be there - presence is the gift," and "If you talk, you're not truly Sitting Shiva."[^10] They quote a line that Ecclesiastes predates: "A good character is the best tombstone. Carve your name on hearts, not marble."[^11] Who showed up for you in a time of grief, and what did their presence mean? Who might need you to show up for them now?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Wisdom literature gives us tools for the long road: songs to keep us remembering, wisdom to guide our steps, and meaning to get us out of bed. Proverbs offers wise sayings that are generally true, not guarantees, and it teaches us that restraint in speech is a mark of wisdom. Ecclesiastes, beneath its talk of meaninglessness, asks what will remain after we are gone, and answers that it will not be our possessions or our parties. It will be the good name we carved on other people's hearts, often in the house of mourning, by simply showing up.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one thing you hope will remain of you in someone else's life?

</div>
