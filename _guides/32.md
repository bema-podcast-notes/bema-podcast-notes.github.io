### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Deuteronomy called Israel to remember that they were slaves in Egypt and to let that memory shape their care for the foreigner, the orphan and the widow. Moses' epitaph remembered him not for the land he missed but for knowing God face to face.[^1]

**This week:** Our final week of Session 1. Marty and Brent step back for a "10,000-foot view" of the Torah, gathering Genesis through Deuteronomy into one story.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This is the finale of Session 1. In the capstone episode Marty and Brent offer two frameworks for holding the five books of Moses together. The first is the "Tale of Two Kingdoms": Empire, built on fear, coercion, power and self-preservation, set against Shalom, built on generosity, invitation, trust and self-sacrifice. The second is a partnership story in five phases, one for each book: Genesis as the basis of the partnership, Exodus as the choice, Leviticus as the definition, Numbers as the shaping, and Deuteronomy as the remembering.

Threaded through both is the marriage picture we have followed for months (engagement among the patriarchs, the wedding at Sinai, the Tabernacle as honeymoon suite, the desert as honeymoon) and Israel's calling to be a whole "kingdom of priests." This session gives the closing more time than usual, so we can walk back through the whole Torah, sit in silence for a moment, and send one another into what comes next.

### Two Ways to Hold the Torah <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#two-ways-to-hold-the-torah}

*The capstone gives us two frameworks for reading the five books as one story.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes summarize the Torah as a partnership in five phases:

- **Genesis:** the basis of the partnership (meeting the characters)
- **Exodus:** the choice of partnership (mutual selection)
- **Leviticus:** the definition of the partnership ("DTR," which the notes render "determine the relationship")
- **Numbers:** the shaping of the partnership (the process of becoming)
- **Deuteronomy:** remembering the partnership (owning the story)[^3]

Looking back over our weeks in each book, which one-line summary fits your experience best, and which one surprises you?

*Read Exodus 19:3–6 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe Israel's calling as challenging ordinary kingdom structures: instead of a few priests within a large kingdom, God calls the entire nation to function as priests. The Tabernacle stood in the center of the camp as a visual training ground, and Leviticus served as the priesthood manual.[^4] What do you notice in these verses about what God has already done, what God asks, and what God says Israel will become?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The capstone names the "Tale of Two Kingdoms" as the central theme running through Scripture: Empire, built on fear, coercion, power, wealth, security and self-preservation, and Shalom, built on generosity, invitation, trust and self-sacrifice.[^5] Earlier this session we heard that these two narratives compete both in the world and inside our own hearts.[^6] Where in the five books did you see the two kingdoms most clearly, and where do you notice them competing in your own heart now?

</div>

### Walking Back Through the Torah <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#walking-back-through-the-torah}

*Before we close, we retrace the road we have walked since Week 1.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Here is the session in seven movements, as the notes describe them:

- **Genesis 1–11, the Preface:** the big ideas of who God is, who we are and what the world is like. Creation is fundamentally good (*tov me'od*, "very good"), and we are invited to "Trust the Story."[^7]
- **Genesis 12–50, the patriarchs:** the family of God (Abraham, Isaac, Jacob and Joseph) who, for all their dysfunction, learn to trust God's story.[^8]
- **Exodus and Sinai:** rescue from Egypt and a wedding at the mountain, with the Ten Commandments as the *ketubah*, the wedding contract.[^9]
- **Leviticus and the Tabernacle:** a honeymoon suite and a mobile Genesis, with Leviticus as its owner's manual and the Day of Atonement at its center.[^10]
- **The desert images:** *midbar*, the place of the word, where we become "people of the ears"; the shepherd's voice; the rotem, acacia, ar'ar and tamarisk; wadis, En Gedi and living water.[^11]
- **Numbers:** the honeymoon in the desert, and Moses striking the rock when he should have led with his voice.[^12]
- **Deuteronomy:** remember where you came from, care for the foreigner, orphan and widow, and know God face to face.[^13]

Which movement most reshaped the way you read the Bible, and which one still feels unfinished for you?

*Read Psalm 23:1–4 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Six weeks ago we heard this psalm through desert eyes: "green pastures" as tufts of grass between rocks, "still waters" as muddy pools in a wadi, "paths of righteousness" as trails worn by thousands of years of flocks, and a provision of "just enough."[^14] As you hear it read now, what do you hear that you would not have heard before this session?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The capstone notes say Genesis 1–11 establishes that creation's essential nature is goodness, not fallenness, and that this counterintuitive truth requires active trust in God's story, especially when circumstances suggest otherwise.[^15] Our traditions have taught us to begin the biblical story in different places, and the aim here is to describe, not to settle the theology. How has starting the story with "very good" changed the way you read the rest of the Torah, and the way you look at your own life and the people around you?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">35 min</span> {#closing}

> We began in a garden that God called very good, and we were invited to trust that story. We followed a family that kept failing and kept being drawn back, a people rescued from Pharaoh's Empire and married to God at Sinai, and a Tabernacle where God made a home among them and trained a whole kingdom of priests. In the desert we learned to listen for the shepherd's voice, to receive and give "just enough" shade, to plant trees for our grandchildren, and to become living water for others. We watched even Moses reach for the stick, and we heard Deuteronomy's last plea to remember where we came from and to make room for the vulnerable. The Torah is the story of God choosing, defining, shaping and calling a partner, and Session 2 will follow that partner into the rest of the story.[^16]
{: .guide-summary}

*Sit together in silence for 90 seconds before the next question.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The capstone notes say that without a solid grounding in the Torah we lack the foundation and vocabulary to understand the prophets, the writings and ultimately Jesus himself.[^17] Over these 33 weeks, how has this study changed the way you read the Bible, the way you understand it, and the way you relate to it? Take one of the three, or all of them.

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes suggest ways a kingdom of priests might live today, such as practicing hospitality that mirrors God's welcome, building communities that prioritize relationship over achievement, and caring for the marginalized.[^18] What is one priestly practice you want to carry out of Session 1 and into your everyday life?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** Going around the room, finish this sentence in one sentence each: "As we leave the Torah, I want to keep ____." Let everyone speak before anyone responds.

*To close, one person reads aloud: Go as a kingdom of priests. Trust that God's world is very good. Become people of the ears. Be shade and living water for someone in the desert. Plant a tamarisk tree. And remember where you came from.*[^19]

</div>
