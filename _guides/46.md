### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Proverbs gave us wise sayings that are generally true, and Ecclesiastes taught that meaning is found in a good name carved on hearts, often in the house of mourning.

**This week:** The final wisdom book is the Song of Songs, and with it the fourth tool for the journey: we need relationships. The episode speaks frankly about sexuality, and so will we, with care for one another.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The Song of Songs is openly erotic poetry. It sits in the *Hamesh Megillot* (the "five scrolls") within the *Ketuvim* (Writings), and Jewish tradition kept young boys from reading it until they reached marrying age. Marty argues that the church has mostly taught sexuality as a list of dos and don'ts, when Scripture treats it as part of our whole selves: spiritual, psychological, and physical. He uses three Hebrew words for love to describe what the Song celebrates in marriage: *rayah* (friendship and infatuation), *ahava* (committed love), and *dowd* (erotic love). Like flames, each gives some light, but together they make a bonfire.

The episode also names what Marty calls the adolescence problem, the long modern gap between physical maturity and marriage, and he speaks honestly about his own failures and the shame he carried as a teenager. A few notes for our time together: a group like ours may include people who are married, single, widowed, or divorced, and the episode's concern for wholeness belongs to all of us. Nobody needs to share personal history. Speak at whatever level is comfortable, and keep what others share inside the room.

### A Book That Makes Readers Blush <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#a-book-that-makes-readers-blush}

*Scripture includes a love poem that does not look away from desire.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes place the Song in the *Hamesh Megillot* alongside Ecclesiastes, and they frame each wisdom book as a tool: Psalms ("We need to sing"), Proverbs ("We need wisdom"), Ecclesiastes ("We need purpose"), and Song of Songs ("We need relationships").[^1] They also note that young Jewish boys were "historically prohibited from reading Song of Songs until reaching marital age due to its erotic content."[^2] Why might a tradition both restrict this book and treasure it as Scripture? What does placing it among the "tools for the journey" say about relationships?

*Read Song of Songs 1:1–4 aloud. Then read Song of Songs 4:16–5:1, aloud or silently, as the group prefers.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes say the Song uses "Garden imagery" ("Opening the gate" and "letting her lover into her garden") as "transparent sexual metaphors," and that its body descriptions, though culturally specific, "celebrate the beloved's physical form."[^3] They point to the line "Kiss me with the kisses of your mouth for your dowd is sweeter than wine."[^4] What do you notice about who speaks, which senses are engaged, and how freely desire is expressed? What surprises you about finding this in the Bible?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes close with a strong claim: "Song of Songs stands as biblical proof that celebrating sexuality within appropriate context is not only acceptable but essential for wholeness."[^5] How was this book handled, or avoided, in the churches you have known? What message did that send?

</div>

### Three Flames, One Bonfire <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#three-flames-one-bonfire}

*Friendship, commitment, and desire are meant to burn together.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes define three Hebrew words. *Rayah* is related to the word for neighbor and describes "Poetic infatuation, the electric 'can't stop thinking about you' feeling," which "Naturally waxes and wanes." *Ahava* is "Commitment love," "Independent of feelings or circumstances." *Dowd*, which can also mean "uncle," is "Sexual, erotic love."[^6] They add an important observation: "God commands ahava toward neighbors, not rayah."[^7] What does it suggest that the love God commands is the one that does not depend on feelings?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Drawing on Rob Bell's NOOMA video "Flame," Marty describes each love as a flame; "Together, they create a massive bonfire." When a flame is missing, there is dysfunction: commitment and desire without friendship becomes a "Committed but passionless marriage"; friendship and commitment without desire becomes a "Close friendship without physical intimacy"; friendship and desire without commitment is "Modern hookup culture."[^8] The notes also contrast ancient arranged marriages, which began with *ahava* and hoped to grow into *rayah*, with modern dating, which begins with *rayah*.[^9] What strengths and weaknesses do you see in each starting point?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes observe that modern weddings tend to celebrate *rayah* ("beauty, poetry, emotion, photography") rather than *ahava*, leaving "Couples prepared for a wedding day dominated by rayah, unprepared for marriage requiring ahava."[^10] Think of weddings you have attended or been part of. What would it look like to put commitment at the center of the celebration?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** *Ahava* "Works both romantically and non-romantically (toward neighbors)," and it is the love of "I will be here for you no matter what."[^11] Whether you are married, single, widowed, or divorced, that love is asked of each of us. Where is *ahava* being asked of you this week, especially where the feelings are not there?

</div>

### What the Church Didn't Say <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#what-the-church-didnt-say}

*Marty names what he received as a teenager and what he needed instead.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Marty identifies an "adolescence problem": for centuries marriage came around puberty, but the industrial and educational revolutions widened the gap between sexual maturity and marriage, and the church "Never acknowledged this created a new problem." Instead it offered "Sex is bad, don't do it," which "Created psychological dysfunction by framing sex itself as shameful."[^12] Marty admits he failed in this area as a teenager and "Carried shame for years due to inadequate teaching."[^13] Our traditions have taught about sexuality in different ways, often with good intentions. Speaking only at the level you are comfortable with, what did you receive growing up, and how has it shaped you?

*Read 1 Corinthians 7:8–9 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say Paul "recognized sexual desire as natural, not sinful," and offered "practical, biological-aware counsel."[^14] They also call for honoring single people "without implying they're incomplete or less whole."[^15] What do you notice about Paul's tone in these verses? How might a church speak honestly about desire while fully honoring those who are single?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** What Marty needed at sixteen was honest recognition ("This is actually really hard, and here's why"), a "Kingdom vision" of "Why Jesus' way is better, not just 'right' according to rules," and "Compassion for the struggle rather than only shame for failure."[^16] Think of a young person you love, or of your own younger self. Beyond the rules, what would you most want them to hear?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The Song of Songs is in the Bible on purpose. It celebrates desire, beauty, and the joy of lovers without embarrassment, and it gives us a fourth tool for the journey: we need relationships. Friendship, commitment, and erotic love are meant to burn together, and when one is missing, something goes wrong. Marty's honest confession is that many of us received rules and shame when we needed honesty, compassion, and a vision of why Jesus' way is better. Whatever our story, the call is the same: to treat our whole selves, bodies included, as part of God's good design, and to make our communities safe places to say so.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one thing you want to remember from tonight about God's design for love?

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 47 — The Lord’s Mouthpiece](/guides/47)** (27:35): We leave the wisdom literature and return to the story, entering the prophets. The episode argues that prophets were God's messengers with present-tense messages for their own people, not fortune-tellers decoding the distant future. It organizes the prophets into five historical periods (pre-Assyrian, Assyrian, Babylonian, exilic, and remnant) and suggests that the book of Isaiah carries several voices from different times. This framework will guide us through the rest of Session 2.

**What to bring next week:** Write down, in a sentence or two, what you were taught a "prophet" is. Then read Amos 2:6–8 and ask what it would have meant to the people who first heard it.
