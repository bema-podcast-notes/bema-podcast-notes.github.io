### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Through Heschel's *The Prophets*, we met the prophet as someone who feels what God feels (*pathos*) and stands with the people, and we heard that God's wrath is meant to correct, not destroy.[^1]

**This week:** Reed Dent leads a second introduction: what a prophet actually is and does, read through Walter Brueggemann's short book *The Prophetic Imagination*.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The first half of the episode sets the popular picture of a prophet as a fortune teller against the biblical picture of a spokesperson who stands with God and confronts a whole community in the present. The second half works through Brueggemann's framework: a "dominant culture" built on a religion of triumphalism and a politics of oppression, answered by a prophetic alternative of God's freedom, justice and compassion.[^2]

The heart of the episode is Brueggemann's pairing of criticism that begins in grief with energizing that begins in hope. The hosts argue that each one goes sour without the other, and that both need to be rooted in worship. Some questions invite you to look at your own church background and community; share as much or as little as you like.

### What a Prophet Is, and Is Not <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#what-a-prophet-is-and-is-not}

*The hosts start with a simple model from Exodus: a prophet carries someone else's voice.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Exodus 6:28–7:2 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Moses says he has "faltering lips," so God appoints Aaron to speak for him, and Moses will be "like God to Pharaoh." Brent jokes that Aaron had it easy, since he only spoke for Moses while Moses carried the weight of speaking for God. Marty hears in the phrase a link to Heschel's *pathos*: the prophet has been *with* God and carries that experience to others.[^3] Trace the chain of voices in the passage. Who speaks for whom, and what does each person in the chain need in order to do the job?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Reed grew up in a charismatic church where visiting "prophets" would "speak into your life." He sees some truth in it but thinks the emphasis was misplaced. The biblical prophets, he says, spoke to a community rather than an individual, about the present rather than the future, and about what God *requires of you* rather than what God will *do for you*.[^4] Which of those contrasts is most different from the picture of prophecy you grew up with, in whatever tradition you come from?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Frederick Buechner jokes that "there is no evidence to suggest that anyone ever asked a prophet home for supper more than once." The hosts note that comforting prophets ("peace, peace, when there is no peace," Jeremiah 6:14) get invited back, and that the poster verse Jeremiah 29:11 came from a prophet thrown into a cistern and left for dead.[^5] Think of a time someone told you something true you did not want to hear. How did you treat them afterward, and what would it take for you to invite that kind of voice back?

</div>

### The Dominant Culture and the Prophetic Alternative <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#the-dominant-culture-and-the-prophetic-alternative}

*Brueggemann's model is Pharaoh's Egypt: the prophet speaks to a people who were rescued and called, and whose calling has been tamed.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Exodus 5:6–18 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Brueggemann names two pillars of the dominant culture. A *religion of triumphalism* says "we are here to win, conquer, prosper, and remake the world in our image." A *politics of oppression* treats those who fall behind as dead weight, like Pharaoh's demand for bricks without straw. Brueggemann argues that the first directly *produces* the second.[^6] From the passage, what does Pharaoh assume about the workers, and how does a culture built on "winning" end up treating people that way?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The hosts contrast an American idea of justice that tends to be punitive (balancing the scales) with a prophetic idea of justice that is restorative. Marty describes *mishpat* (justice) and *tzedakah* (righteousness) as right relationship, a straight line rather than a crooked one. The prophets keep returning to the "quartet of the vulnerable": the poor, the widow, the orphan and the sojourner.[^7] Which of those four is hardest to see from where you live and worship, and why?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Brueggemann says a people's calling can be "domesticated" in more than one way: through prosperity, as with the wealthy Israel Amos confronts, or through fear, as when Ahaz turns to Assyria for protection. The hosts suggest economic success, national power, progress or political tribalism as possible modern versions.[^8] This is a question for honest self-examination, not for diagnosing other churches. Where might something like that be quietly taming the calling of your own community, or your own life?

</div>

### Grief, Hope and Doxology <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#grief-hope-and-doxology}

*The prophet works through two linked moves: criticism that begins in grief, and energizing that begins in hope.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Amos 5:24 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Brueggemann writes: "Real criticism begins in the capacity to grieve because grief is the most visceral announcement that things are not right. Only in the empire are we pressed and urged and invited to pretend that things are all right." Amos 5:24 is the hosts' example of energizing, pointing toward where things can go.[^9] In your own words, why would grief have to come *before* criticism? What changes in criticism that does not start there?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Criticism alone produces what Reed calls "ironic hipster" cynicism, and Marty notes this is a common weakness in deconstruction conversations. Energizing alone produces sentimentality; Reed's brother-in-law struggled in churches that offered only happy songs and no grief.[^10] When you drift, which way do you tend to drift, toward cynicism or toward sentimentality? What usually pulls you there?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The hosts say numbing yourself to grief also numbs your capacity for hope and joy, because you cannot choose where the numbness stops. They add Brueggemann's line that "prophecy without doxology becomes ideology": critique and hope cut off from worship collapse into partisan positions on the left or right.[^11] Sharing here is optional. Where might you have numbed a grief in order to cope, and what would one small act of lament, rooted in worship, look like for you this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The biblical prophets were not mystics reading personal destinies. They were spokespeople who shared God's heart and stood up in public to remind a rescued people of their calling, which is why they were rarely invited back for supper. Empires run on triumphalism and oppression; God's people are called to a different world shaped by God's freedom, justice and compassion. The prophet criticizes through grief and energizes through hope, and both are needed. Beneath it all is doxology, and the message moves through indictment and a call to repentance toward God's own act of restoration, when God will "swallow up death forever" (Isaiah 25:8).[^12]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: name one place in your life where you need either more grief or more hope.

</div>
