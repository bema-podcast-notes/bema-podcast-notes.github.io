### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Porshe Chiles helped us notice whose stories go untold, from Hagar, who named "the God who sees me," to the Ethiopian official asking whether anything stood in the way of his belonging.[^1]

**This week:** We return to Genesis and to midrash. Isaac goes up Mount Moriah with Abraham, but the text never brings him back down, and the episode explores what that silence might mean.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode starts with Genesis 22:19, which mentions Abraham and his servants returning to Beersheba but says nothing about Isaac.[^2] Two streams of midrash read that gap differently: one emphasizes Isaac's obedience and even a death and resurrection, the other his trauma and separation from his father.[^3] The second reading follows Isaac to Beer Lahai Roi, the well where Hagar met God, and notices that Abraham has to travel to mourn Sarah.[^4]

Abraham still sends for a wife for his absent son, and Isaac meets Rebekah in a field and is "comforted."[^5] The midrash connects Isaac's later blindness to *ayin ra'ah*, "a bad eye," a perspective of scarcity shaped by trauma.[^6] Marty shares that this reading helps him understand religious trauma, harm that can happen even when parents are sincerely trying to follow God.[^7]

*A note for our group:* this episode touches religious trauma and family pain. Share only what you choose to share, and let others do the same.

### Where Is Isaac? <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#where-is-isaac}

*The episode treats Isaac's disappearance from the text as a deliberate signal inviting deeper reading.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 22:1–19 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Listen for every time Isaac is named, and for the phrase "the two of them went on together." The notes call Isaac's absence in verse 19 "not mere literary oversight but a deliberate textual signal."[^2] Who goes up the mountain, and who comes down? What did you notice?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The first midrashic stream says Isaac died and was raised three days later, and the notes suggest Hebrews 11:19 may reference it: Abraham "reasoned that God could even raise the dead, and so in the manner of speaking, he did receive Isaac back from death."[^3] The second stream says Isaac "was so traumatized by the experience that he fled from his father and did not return home."[^3] What does each reading help you see that the other might miss?

*Read Genesis 23:1–2, Genesis 24:62 and Genesis 16:13–14 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes observe that Abraham "went to mourn for Sarah," suggesting he was not with her when she died, and that Isaac "had come from Beer Lahai Roi," the well Hagar named "the well of the Living One who sees me."[^4] Why might it matter that Isaac ends up living at the place associated with another person who experienced family trauma and exile? What does a place like that offer someone who is hurting?

</div>

### Trust the Story <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#trust-the-story}

*Abraham keeps acting as a father to a son who is not home, and an unexpected meeting in a field becomes part of Isaac's healing.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes call it remarkable that Abraham sends for a wife for Isaac: he "acts as a father for a son who is not even living at home," with "no guarantee Isaac will ever return."[^5] They describe this as faith "not as getting everything right, but as continuing to participate in God's story even when outcomes are uncertain and past actions have caused harm."[^5] How does that description of faith compare with the one you grew up with?

*Read Genesis 24:62–67 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Notice where Isaac is, what he is doing and how the passage ends. The notes say the comfort Isaac receives "is not just for Sarah's death but for all the accumulated trauma and loss," and that Rebekah "becomes an instrument of healing and restoration."[^8] What details in these verses suggest healing, and what do you make of the timing of this meeting?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode's recurring phrase is "trust the story," which is "explicitly not presented as a formula guaranteeing that 'all the Isaacs come back home.'"[^9] Why do you think the hosts insist it is not a formula? What is the difference between trusting the story and expecting a guaranteed outcome?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes picture both Abraham and Isaac acting "faithfully without certainty."[^10] Where in your life (a relationship or a situation) are you being asked to keep participating without knowing how it will turn out? What would one faithful step look like this week? You can answer in general terms.

</div>

### Bad Eyes and Good Eyes <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#bad-eyes-and-good-eyes}

*The midrash connects Isaac's blindness to the way trauma shapes how we see.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 27:1 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The midrash tells of angels weeping as Abraham prepared the sacrifice, their tears falling into Isaac's eyes and giving him *ayin ra'ah*, a bad eye.[^6] In contrast, a "good eye" (*ayin tova*) meant "viewing the world through abundance, generosity, and seeing the best in people."[^6] The notes suggest this helps explain Isaac's favoritism toward Esau and his naming Jacob "deceiver." How does that connect with what we saw in the Jacob episodes a few weeks ago?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty "vulnerably shares that this midrash helps him understand religious trauma," harm "often inflicted by well-meaning parents or spiritual leaders trying to faithfully follow God."[^7] The episode "offers no simple judgment of whether Abraham was 'right' or 'wrong.'"[^7] Why might it be important to be able to say both that someone was sincere and that harm was done? Share personal experience only if you want to.

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes say "trauma doesn't just affect us emotionally - it shapes how we see and interpret the world around us."[^6] Where do you notice a "bad eye" (scarcity or suspicion) shaping how you see someone right now? What is one practice that might help you look at them with a good eye this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Isaac went up the mountain with his father, and the text never brings him down. The midrash follows him to the well where Hagar met the God who sees, and it watches Abraham keep acting as a father with no guarantee his son will return. In a field, Isaac meets Rebekah and is comforted, yet his "bad eye" goes with him into the next generation. The story offers no formula, only an invitation to keep trusting that God is at work in messy, wounded families like ours.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, where do you most need to "trust the story" right now?

</div>
