### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Nahum was the counterbalance to Jonah. God's patience is the "1000" and *diyn* (judgment with finality) is the "3," but the 3 is real, and for oppressed Israel it was a message of hope that God hears the cry of the oppressed.[^1]

**This week:** Zephaniah speaks to Judah in the days of King Josiah as Babylon rises. His central image is *t'shuvah*, a return to the design we were made for.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Zephaniah is the seventh prophet in the series and the first to warn of the new threat from Babylon. Judah had been rescued once before, under Hezekiah's reforms, but slid back into destructive patterns under Manasseh and Amon.[^3] The book moves in three steps: the problem (Judah, Jerusalem and especially the priests), judgment on all the surrounding nations with Judah caught in the middle, and finally hope, with a humble remnant and God rejoicing over his people with singing.[^4]

The episode's key word is *t'shuvah*, from the root *shuv*, "to turn." It means something like a U-turn back to an original path, not merely feeling sorry. Zephaniah also shows that worshiping the wrong god and mistreating people cannot be pulled apart: the priests of Jerusalem were taking part in Molech worship, sacrificing children in the Valley of Hinnom.[^5] Our traditions use different language for repentance, so the questions invite each of us to describe what we were taught rather than to settle who is right.

### A U-Turn Home <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#a-u-turn-home}

*Zephaniah's call is less about groveling and more about coming home.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Zephaniah 2:1–3 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes describe this as the prophet's call to seek righteousness and humility before the day of the Lord arrives.[^6] What exactly are the people asked to seek, who is being addressed, and what tone do you hear in these verses: threat, plea, or something else?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes explain that *t'shuvah* comes from *shuv*, "to turn," and more accurately conveys making a U-turn or returning to an original path. It assumes humanity has an original, good design from which we have strayed.[^7] Each of our traditions has its own way of talking about repentance. How was repentance described where you grew up in faith, and how does the idea of returning to your original design compare with that?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say that rather than seeing the "true self" as fundamentally broken or sinful, *t'shuvah* suggests our truest identity is found in operating according to God's design. When we fail or sin, we are being "less human" and giving the world a less accurate picture of who we truly are.[^8] Our traditions describe human nature in different ways, and this is a picture to examine rather than a verdict. When you fail, what do you usually tell yourself about who you really are, and how does this picture compare?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes list what *t'shuvah* returns us to: our design as image-bearers of God, a path of mercy, compassion and love, the calling to be a kingdom of priests, and the wholeness and *shalom* (peace, wholeness) of Genesis 1. They say this "reframes repentance not as groveling in unworthiness, but as reclaiming our true identity and purpose."[^9] Which of those four destinations feels farthest from where you are right now, and what would one step back toward it look like this week?

</div>

### Wrong Gods, Real Victims <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#wrong-gods-real-victims}

*Zephaniah will not let worship and justice be filed in separate drawers.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Zephaniah 1:4–6 and Zephaniah 3:1–4 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes point out that Zephaniah singles out Judah's leaders: priests who profane the sanctuary and do violence to the law, prophets described as unprincipled and treacherous, and officials compared to roaring lions and evening wolves.[^10] What do you notice about who is named in these verses, and why might the prophet aim so directly at the people entrusted with guiding everyone else?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode introduces the Valley of Ben Hinnom outside Jerusalem, where children were burned as offerings to Molech. The valley later became the city's garbage dump, a place of perpetual fire, and its Greek name *gehenna* is often translated "hell" in the New Testament.[^11] How does knowing the history of a real valley change the way you hear that word?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes call it a false dichotomy to choose between idolatry and injustice as the real problem, and they ask: "Is the tragedy that people have their doctrine wrong, or that they are sacrificing children in fire?" The wrong god-narrative (fear and self-preservation rather than self-sacrifice) leads to the mistreatment of the vulnerable. The episode goes on to argue that things are immoral "because of their impact on people," not because they break arbitrary rules.[^12] Our traditions may weigh right belief and right practice differently. Where do you see belief and treatment of people connected in your own church's story, and where have they drifted apart? The notes end with a question to carry: "Does our theology lead us to exclude or include the marginalized?"

</div>

### Neighbors Fall, and a Song at the End <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#neighbors-fall-and-a-song-at-the-end}

*Judah watched everyone around them fall and still assumed it could not happen to them.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Zephaniah 3:6–7 aloud, then Zephaniah 3:14–20.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes say God expected that when Judah saw Philistia, Moab, Ammon, Cush and even Assyria fall, they would surely repent. Instead Judah "was still eager to act corruptly." The notes suggest their history of miraculous deliverances may have given them a false sense of invincibility: "Maybe others fall, but not us."[^13] What do you notice in the shift between verse 7 and verse 14, and what does it tell you about where Zephaniah wants the story to end?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode points to Hezekiah's reforms as proof that *t'shuvah* is possible and that God responds to genuine repentance, yet those reforms faded under Manasseh and Amon. The notes observe that "reforms can be temporary if not sustained by ongoing commitment."[^14] What helps a change of direction last, in a person or in a congregation?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes call Zephaniah 3:17 "one of the most tender images in prophetic literature": God takes great delight in his people and rejoices over them with singing. They say the warnings and purification are "the difficult but necessary path to restoration," and that discipline serves love.[^15] Where in your life are you tempted to think "others fall, but not us," and how might hearing God's song over you make it easier to turn around rather than harder?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Zephaniah speaks to a people who had been rescued before and had drifted back. He names the problem without flinching: priests serving a god who demanded children, leaders acting like wolves, a nation sure that judgment was for other people. But his call is *t'shuvah*, a U-turn back to the design we were made for as image-bearers. And the last word belongs to a God who rejoices over his returning people with singing.[^16]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, name one place where you want to make a U-turn back toward your original design before we meet again.

</div>
