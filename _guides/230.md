### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Dallas Jenkins described being called back from metrics to simply looking at Jesus, and admitted he needs to improve at practicing Sabbath and rest.[^1]

**This week:** Pastor and author Mandy Smith explores what keeps us from resting, what Sabbath teaches about control, and how a childlike faith can move beyond the baggage of Western culture.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Mandy Smith, author of *Unfettered: Imagining a Childlike Faith beyond the Baggage of Western Culture*, names four barriers to rest (intellectual, theological, power and existential concerns) and describes Sabbath as releasing control and trusting that God is carrying the world. She notes that the seventh day in Genesis has no closing "evening and morning," an invitation to rest that never closes.[^3]

She also distinguishes four ways of being: childlike, adult-like, childish and "adult-ish," the over-concerned, respectable voice that blocks wonder with shame and fear. Brent tells of a dragonfly that interrupted a retreat day, and Mandy tells of hawks riding the updrafts. Finally, she asks why Western presentations of the gospel often do not feel like good news, and what other cultures' metaphors might offer.[^4]

### Resting from Our Desire to Be God <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#resting-from-our-desire-to-be-god}

*Mandy reframes rest as letting go of control rather than simply taking time off.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 1:31–2:3 aloud. If you read all of Genesis 1 this week, keep the earlier days in mind.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes point out that the seventh day "uniquely lacks the refrain 'evening and morning'," suggesting an ongoing invitation to rest, and they connect this to Hebrews, where the invitation to enter God's rest still stands.[^5] Compare the seventh day with the six before it. What is different about how it is described and how it ends? What might it mean that this day is left open?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** Mandy names four barriers to rest: intellectual concerns (needing to figure everything out first), theological concerns (feeling we must constantly work or prove ourselves), power concerns (fearing everything will fall apart if we stop), and existential concerns (worth tied to productivity).[^3] Which of these do you see most often in the people and culture around you? If you are comfortable sharing, which one is most familiar in your own life?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say Western culture tells us that stopping means failure and that rest is weakness, symbolized by cough drop wrappers that say "suck it up" instead of encouraging rest when sick.[^3] Where else do you hear that message, at work, in church or in your own head?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Mandy describes Sabbath as "resting from our own desire to be God," learning through the discomfort of giving God control for a day. She mentions the end-of-Sabbath ritual of pouring wine until the cup overflows, a picture of wanting the sweetness of Sabbath to flow into all of life.[^6] What would it look like for you to give God control of one day, or even part of one, in the coming week? What would be hardest to set down?

</div>

### Childlike, Not Childish <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#childlike-not-childish}

*Mandy names the voice that keeps us from receiving God's presence, and invites us to notice the Kingdom already flourishing around us.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Mandy distinguishes four aspects of self: childlike (unafraid of powerlessness), adult-like (unafraid of power), childish (insecure in powerlessness) and adult-ish (insecure in power, over-controlling, concerned with appearances). The goal is to hold both our place as "crowning glory of creation" and "nothing but dust."[^7] Why do you think Mandy aims for both childlike and adult-like, rather than choosing one? What goes wrong when a person or a church has only one?

*Read Matthew 6:25–34 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say that when Jesus says to "consider the lilies and the birds," he is "not offering a Hallmark sentiment" but pointing to the Kingdom of God's abundance already present. Mandy describes hawks gliding on updrafts, "flourishing in the Kingdom of God," as a reminder that "the same peace and awareness is available to me as well if I just open myself up to it."[^8] What exactly does Jesus ask his listeners to do with the birds and the lilies? Report the verbs you notice.

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** On a retreat day by a creek, Brent was interrupted by a dragonfly. It felt "stupid" to his adult-ish self, yet it became an encounter with "God's holiness, the holiness of your own existence, and of all creation," and nothing needed to "come of it."[^9] When has your own adult-ish voice talked you out of a moment of wonder, or shamed you for one? What would it take this week to let yourself be interrupted?

</div>

### Good News That Feels Like Good News <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#good-news-that-feels-like-good-news}

*Mandy asks whether the way the gospel is often presented in the West actually sounds like good news.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes contrast an American trying to drive a bed home on a two-door car, arms out the windows holding it down, with a more communal Australian instinct to consider how one's actions affect others.[^10] What small, everyday habits reveal how individualistic or communal your own culture is?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes say Western presentations of the gospel tend to be individualistic, guilt-based and courtroom-shaped, and often do not feel like good news "because it starts with convincing people they are horrible sinners rather than connecting with actual felt needs." Other cultures lean toward communal, shame-and-belonging, family metaphors like the prodigal son. The notes insist neither approach should be discarded.[^11] Think diagnostically about the tradition you know best. What picture of the gospel did you learn first, what does it do well, and whom might it fail to reach?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes conclude, "The Gospel must be genuinely good news, not just a system we must convince people they need."[^11] Think of one person you know. What felt need or longing in their life might be the place where good news would actually sound good?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Mandy Smith invites us to see rest not as time off but as letting go of the wish to be God. The seventh day has no evening and morning; the invitation to rest is still open. The adult-ish voice in us prefers control and respectability, but childlike faith is free to be interrupted by a dragonfly, a hawk or a cup that overflows. And the good news, if it is truly good, meets people in their real needs and not only in our systems. This week, the invitation is to stop, receive, and notice.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one thing you will let go of, or one interruption you will welcome, this week?

</div>
