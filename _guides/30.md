### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** In the wadis we learned to follow the guide's voice around the next bend. At En Gedi we learned that God is our living water and wants living water to flow through us to others, and the wadi floods taught us that wisdom is about where we build.[^1]

**This week:** Back to the story in Numbers. Moses strikes the rock and is told he will not lead the people into the land, and the desert's lesson about the shepherd's voice helps explain why.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Why was Moses kept out of the Promised Land? The familiar answers (he hit the rock twice, he did not follow the instructions exactly, he took the credit) are, in Marty and Brent's view, inadequate when examined closely. The episode reads Numbers 20 against its deliberate twin, the water from the rock in Exodus 17, and against the BEMA framework of two competing narratives: Empire, which rules by force, fear and the "stick," and Shalom, which leads by invitation, trust and the "voice."

Moses spent forty years in Pharaoh's household and forty years shepherding in the desert before he led Israel. In Numbers 20, the episode suggests, he reverts to Empire's methods. Two Hebrew words carry the argument: *nakah*, "to strike to kill," and *paniym*, "before" or "in front of," which describes where God and the people stand in relation to the rock. The death of Miriam at the start of the chapter, with no public mourning recorded, may help explain Moses' state of mind, and the story of Balaam's donkey, which soon follows, drives home the contrast between stick and voice.

### Two Rocks, Two Stories <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#two-rocks-two-stories}

*Numbers 20 is written to be read alongside Exodus 17.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Exodus 17:1–7 and Numbers 20:1–13 aloud, with a different reader for each.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes say the Numbers 20 story is deliberately linked to Exodus 17 through specific Hebrew phrases and narrative parallels.[^3] Compare the two stories. What does God tell Moses to do each time? Where does God stand, where do the people stand, and what does Moses say and do? List as many similarities and differences as you can find.

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes name three traditional explanations for Moses' punishment: striking the rock twice, not following instructions, and taking credit. The episode calls them inadequate when examined closely.[^4] Which explanation were you taught, if any, and what did it leave you satisfied or unsatisfied with? (Our traditions may have taught this story differently; the point is to describe, not to defend.)

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** In Exodus 17, God told Moses to *nakah* ("strike to kill") the rock while God stood *paniym*, "in front of" him, between Moses and the rock. God was taking the blow for the people.[^5] (We met this scene in Week 22.[^6]) In Numbers 20, the notes suggest, Moses places the people in front of the rock, reversing the lesson, and strikes twice; they even raise the possibility that he directed violence toward the people rather than taking it upon himself.[^7] The notes also read God's verdict as Moses failing to show God as *kadosh* ("holy"), meaning fundamentally different from other gods: marked by love, mercy and restoration rather than violence.[^8] What do you make of this reading? If it is right, what exactly did the people see about God when Moses struck the rock?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes draw a principle from the story: leaders are called to demonstrate God's character through their methods, not just to accomplish right outcomes through wrong means.[^9] When have you seen a good result reached by a harmful method, in your own life or around you? What did that method say about the God we claim to represent?

</div>

### Stick Power and Voice Power <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#stick-power-and-voice-power}

*Moses learned two ways of leading, and under pressure the older one came back.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes divide Moses' life into three forty-year phases: raised in Pharaoh's household, learning Empire's methods while keeping God's heart for the oppressed; shaped as a shepherd in the desert, learning God's ways; and leading Israel, with ongoing lessons in using God's authority rather than Pharaoh's methods.[^10] Three weeks ago we heard that a shepherd leads with the voice and never with the stick.[^11] Where do you see each phase of Moses' life showing up in Numbers 20?

*Read Numbers 20:1 and 20:29 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes point to Miriam's death at the start of the chapter, with no public mourning recorded, in contrast with the mourning for Aaron at its end. They suggest grief may have shaped Moses' emotional state, and that this may be a "test of the soul," with Moses essentially saying, "these people don't deserve water unless they properly mourn my sister."[^12] What do you notice about how the two deaths are reported? How does the possibility of unprocessed grief change the way you feel about Moses here?

*Read Numbers 22:21–31 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Soon after this story comes Balaam, a prophet beating his donkey with a stick until God opens the donkey's mouth. The notes read it as God using voice when humans will not, and connect it with Jesus saying that if the people kept quiet, the stones would cry out.[^13] Why might Numbers place this story so close to Moses striking the rock? What is the storyteller trying to make us see?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes say anyone in authority, whether employer, teacher or parent, faces the choice between Empire and Shalom methods, and that the way we use authority tells one narrative or the other.[^14] Where do you hold some authority, at home, at work, at church or among friends? What does reaching for the stick look like for you when you are tired or grieving, and what would leading with your voice look like there this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#closing}

> Moses learned leadership in two schools: forty years in Pharaoh's house and forty years following sheep in the desert. At Massah, God stood before the rock and took the blow for the people; at Meribah, under the weight of grief and frustration, Moses struck in anger and showed the people a god who uses the stick. The episode suggests this is why he could not lead them into the land: the land could not be established through the narrative of force and fear. How we lead matters as much as where we lead people.[^15]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: where is one place this week where you want to put down the stick and use your voice?

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 31 — Remember Where You Came From](/guides/31)** (43:08): We reach Deuteronomy, Moses' final teaching before Israel enters the land. Its words for "remember" (*zakar*) and "do not forget" (*shakach*) appear again and again, and the repeated phrase "Remember that you were slaves in Egypt" becomes the reason for caring for the foreigner, the orphan and the widow. The episode ends with Moses' epitaph, which speaks of his intimacy with God rather than his failure.[^16]
