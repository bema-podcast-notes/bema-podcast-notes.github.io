### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** The flood story ends with God hanging up his bow and promising to "Sabbath from destroying," a God who knows when to stop destroying just as he knew when to stop creating.

**This week:** Right after the flood, Noah plants a vineyard, and the story ends with a curse on the wrong person. The question is whether Noah can learn the lesson God has just modeled.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This short, strange passage is full of problems: the sons are listed in inconsistent order, Noah's nakedness in his own tent becomes a crisis, and Noah curses his grandson Canaan when it was Ham who acted. The hosts notice that the story mirrors the garden (a cultivated place, fruit, nakedness, covering, and a curse), and they introduce midrash, an ancient Jewish form of commentary that leads readers on a journey of discovery rather than handing them a conclusion.

One midrashic reading, which the hosts follow, understands "seeing his father's nakedness" as a serious violation that robbed Noah of a future son, and it reads Noah's curse as revenge: robbing Ham of a son in return. This material is mature, and we will discuss it plainly but without detail. The heart of the episode is not the shock but the choice: will Noah "know when to say enough," or will he pass his injury on to the next generation?

### Problems in the Vineyard <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#problems-in-the-vineyard}

*The story is full of problems, and the problems are invitations.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 9:18–29 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes open with a list of problems. The sons "are not listed consistently." Noah already knows how to make wine. "What is the big deal with Noah being naked in his own tent?" How does Noah figure out who did what? And why curse his grandson Canaan, when "Ham did something wrong, not Canaan"?[^1] If you brought a list of problems this week, share one. What other questions does the passage raise for you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The hosts point out that the creation story seems to be continuing: "a garden with fruit should be next." Noah plants a vineyard and tastes its fruit, then there is "weirdness about nakedness," then a covering of the nakedness, then a curse.[^2] Lay this story beside Genesis 2–3, which we read two weeks ago. Which parallels do you find most striking, and what do they suggest about whether the flood "fixed" humanity?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The principle of first mention says that when Scripture introduces a word or image for the first time, "that initial context often frames how that element should be understood throughout the rest of the biblical narrative." This is the Bible's first vineyard.[^3] What difference might it make to remember Noah's vineyard when vineyards appear later in Scripture?

</div>

### Reading with the Rabbis <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#reading-with-the-rabbis}

*Midrash takes you around the block to get next door.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes describe midrash as "an ancient Jewish form of commentary." Westerners "will exegete the passage, be deductive and straight forward," while Easterners "will lead you on a process of discovery," telling "a story within the story."[^4] Another summary calls this "taking you around the block to go next door," a method that "often appears ludicrous or arbitrary to Western minds."[^5] How were you taught to study a passage? What might be gained by a method designed to make you discover rather than simply be told?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** In Hebrew, "seeing nakedness" can mean more than looking; the notes say it could indicate molestation or castration, and the midrash concludes it was castration.[^6] The clue comes from the garden: there were four rivers, and "Lineage is also imagined with a river." Noah had three sons and was told to be fruitful and multiply, so "Noah is expected to have a fourth son."[^7] Without dwelling on the details, how does this reading change the way you understand Noah's anger and his curse?

*Read Genesis 2:10–14 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> As you listen to the four rivers, notice what the text bothers to tell us about them. Why might an ancient reader have pictured a family line as a river? What does the image suggest about what Noah lost?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Marty, following Rabbi Fohrman, says Noah "is bent on revenge. Ham has robbed Noah of something, a fourth son, and now Noah is bent on robbing Ham of something from him."[^8] Another summary puts Noah's logic in one line: "you robbed me of future sons, so I'll rob you of yours."[^9] When have you felt the pull of that kind of reasoning, the sense that a wrong done to you should be repaid in kind?

</div>

### The Curse and the Cycle <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#the-curse-and-the-cycle}

*Noah steps into a role reserved for God and passes the injury to the innocent.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes observe that this word for curse is used only by God and one person, Noah: "This curse is reserved for G-d alone." Noah "is stepping into the role of G-d," throwing the curse "NOT on the one who deserves it but on the one who doesn't deserve it."[^10] By contrast, in the garden God "Cursed the ground and serpent, not the humans directly."[^11] What does the contrast between God's response and Noah's response reveal about the difference between justice and vengeance?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes cite Richard Rohr: "any nation that loses the capacity for self-criticism will become idolatrous." Hebrew Scripture is unusual in showing even its heroes, like Noah and David, failing badly.[^12] Every tradition in this room has heroes and founding stories. What does it look like for a church or tradition to keep its capacity for self-criticism without losing its love for its own story?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes name this as "one of the places we are first introduced to forgiveness," and describe forgiveness as "the ultimate act of trust in God's sovereignty and the key to breaking cycles of vengeance."[^13] The plea of these stories is the same each week: "Don't lash out in our insecurity and our shame."[^14] Think of a wrong you carry that you might be tempted to pass on to someone who did not cause it. What would it look like to say "enough" and let it stop with you? You may answer this one privately.

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The flood did not change the human heart, and right away the garden story happens again: a vineyard, fruit, nakedness, a covering, and a curse. This time it is Noah who curses, using a word reserved for God, and he aims it at a grandson who did nothing wrong. The notes sum up what Genesis keeps teaching us: how to be like God, when to stop creating, when to stop destroying, and how to trust the story. Noah's invitation was to trust God with justice and let the world continue down a redemptive path, and that invitation is ours too.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, where is God inviting you to "know when to say enough" this week?

</div>
