### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Haggai called the remnant to give careful thought to their ways and build God's house. God promised that the humble second temple would carry greater glory than Solomon's, because glory is about God's presence, and Haggai warned that drift is easy and holiness takes work.[^1]

**This week:** Zechariah, Haggai's partner prophet, encourages the same struggling community for decades. The episode gives us a working definition of apocalyptic literature and reads the king on a donkey in its first setting.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode suggests that Zechariah and Haggai were probably toddlers when the first returnees arrived in Jerusalem, grew up in the "mess and struggle" of rebuilding, and may have trained together as prophets.[^3] Where Haggai's ministry lasted months, Zechariah's lasted decades, because the remnant needed encouragement through a long season under Persian rule.[^4]

The heart of the episode is a definition: apocalyptic literature is a genre that uses symbols and images to convey hope to its present-day audience, rather than a blueprint for the end times.[^5] With that lens, the episode reads Zechariah 9:9–10 as a call to persevere faithfully within empire, traces the theme of humble kingship through Scripture, and notes that almost all of Zechariah's images reappear in Revelation.[^6] This is a short episode, so we will spend more of our time reading the texts together.

### Hope in Symbols <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#hope-in-symbols}

*Zechariah speaks to a weary community in pictures, and the episode asks what those pictures were for.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Zechariah 1:7–17 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** This is the first of Zechariah's night visions, the man among the myrtle trees.[^7] Read it slowly and list the images: who appears, what they report, and what God says at the end. What do you notice about the mood of the vision, and what would these words have sounded like to people rebuilding a ruined city under a foreign empire?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The episode defines apocalyptic literature as a genre that "utilizes symbols and images to convey hope to its present-day audience." The notes add that its primary function is not to provide blueprints or timelines for the end times, while God may still choose to fulfill the imagery in ways that align with it.[^8] They describe a typical shape:[^9]

- Opening visions that encourage faithfulness and endurance in present trials
- Symbols of God, God's people, their trials and God's character
- Closing proclamations that a new kingdom, reality and ruler is coming
- A call to walk faithfully through present circumstances

How does this shape fit what you just read in chapter 1, and what changes when you read a vision first as encouragement for its original listeners?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Our churches have taught different ways of reading books like Daniel, Zechariah and Revelation, and many of us grew up with charts, timelines or strong opinions about the end times. Without deciding who was right, what did the way you first learned to read these books do for you: did it produce hope, fear, curiosity or something else, and how might this definition shape the hope you draw from them now?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q4:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say the episode's goal is not to explain everything but to give enough context and tools that listeners can read the prophets themselves, so that the material can "turn the lights on" for readers.[^10] Which prophetic book from this session has started to make sense to you in a way it did not before, and what tool helped most?

</div>

### From Zechariah to Revelation <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#from-zechariah-to-revelation}

*Revelation draws on images its first readers already knew from the prophets.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Zechariah 6:1–8 aloud, then Revelation 6:1–8.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes list parallels between Zechariah and Revelation, including four horses drawing chariots and the four horsemen, a man among myrtle trees and a man among lampstands, a measuring line and a measuring rod, the high priest's clean garments and the multitude in white robes, and a woman in a basket and a woman on a beast.[^11] Compare the two passages you just read. What is shared, what is different, and what does it suggest that John reached for Zechariah's pictures?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes suggest that much of the hand-wringing over Revelation's difficulty could be resolved by becoming more familiar with the Hebrew Scriptures John quoted, and that reading Scripture in isolation from its broader biblical context leads to confusion.[^12] How might your own reading habits change if you read the New Testament with the prophets open beside it?

</div>

### A King on a Donkey <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#a-king-on-a-donkey}

*A promise first given to a remnant under Persian rule becomes the picture Jesus chooses on his way into Jerusalem.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Zechariah 9:9–10 aloud, then Matthew 21:1–9.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Look at what the king rides, what he does to the chariots and the war horse, and what he brings to the nations. Then look at what the crowd in Matthew does and says. What do you notice when you hear these two passages side by side?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes say Zechariah originally encouraged the remnant that if they could "walk obediently within empire," without their own king or full independence, God would eventually bring their promised king through divine providence, not military conquest.[^13] They trace this humble kingship through David, the youngest of eight sons; Micah's Bethlehem, "least among the clans of Judah"; Isaiah's Suffering Servant; and this king on a donkey.[^14] By riding a donkey, Jesus was "declaring Himself king while simultaneously redefining what kingship means."[^15] How does knowing the first audience deepen, rather than diminish, the way you hear Palm Sunday?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes put the question to modern readers: are we looking for "warhorses" or "donkeys"? They ask whether we expect God's work to come through impressive displays of power or through humble, often overlooked means.[^16] Where do you find yourself looking for a warhorse right now, in your church, your community or your own life, and what might a donkey look like there?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Zechariah's ministry lasted decades because rebuilding was "a long season" requiring perseverance, and the notes say that sometimes faithfulness means sustained obedience over many years.[^17] What long season are you walking through, and what would it mean to walk obediently within it?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Zechariah grew up in the rubble of the return and spent decades giving his people pictures of hope. His visions were not first a timetable but encouragement for weary people under Persian rule, and nearly all of them echo later in Revelation. His king on a donkey taught the remnant that rescue would come through humility and God's providence rather than war horses, and Jesus rode into Jerusalem embodying that very promise. The notes close by saying the Judaism that emerged after the exile was fundamentally transformed, setting "the stage for Jesus in a really big way."[^18]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, name one place where you want to look for a donkey instead of a warhorse this week.

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 69 — Malachi — Q&A](/guides/69)** (17:59): The last prophetic voice of the remnant period. Malachi uses a question-and-answer format in which God makes an accusation and the people answer with defensive questions, exposing a community disappointed by the return and slipping into apathy. The episode asks whether we "imagine new tomorrows" or lie to ourselves about a better past, and it ends with a faithful few writing a scroll of remembrance.[^19]

**What to bring next week:** Think of a season you call "the good old days." Write down two things that were truly good about it and one thing you tend to leave out when you remember it.
