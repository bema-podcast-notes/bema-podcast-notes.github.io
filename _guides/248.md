### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Josh Bossé showed us a book where God is never named, and how Esther's wisdom and her people's community became the way deliverance came.[^1]

**This week:** Elle Grover Fricks gives a Hebrew lesson on joy, then asks what centuries of commentary have added to the brief story of Lot's wife.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The first half is a word study. Elle counts the concept of joy 921 times in Scripture, far more than "do not fear," and walks through six Hebrew words, several of them strikingly physical: leaping like a horse, spinning in circles, jumping, shouting.[^3] Some of those words describe God himself rejoicing over his people, which challenges a picture of God (and of worship) as distant and emotionless.[^4]

The second half uses reception history, the study of how communities have read a text over time, to look at Lot's wife in Genesis 19.[^5] Elle shows how the Talmud and Origen layered motives onto a woman the text never explains, and how those readings linked women with carnality and weakness.[^6] She then offers an alternative reading built on the Torah's use of salt for purity, holiness and covenant, in which Lot's wife looks back in grief as a holy witness.[^7] Elle frames this as a reading to weigh, not the only faithful one.

### Six Ways to Say Joy <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#six-ways-to-say-joy}

*Hebrew does not have one word for joy; it has a whole vocabulary of movement.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Elle walks through six words (the notes' spellings):[^3]

- *sameach*, "cheerful, rejoicing," often shared in community (as in *Chag Sameach*, "Happy Holidays");
- *chedvah*, a loanword from Akkadian once tied to the god Enlil, used in Nehemiah 8:10 for "the joy of the Lord";
- *shush*, to leap around for joy like a horse;
- *gil*, to spin for joy, as in a circle dance;
- *alaz*, to jump for joy, as at a concert or wedding;
- *ronin*, a high-pitched shout for joy, sometimes a battle cry.

Which of these surprised you most, and which one is closest to how you actually express joy? Which is furthest from it?

*Read Zephaniah 3:17 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** Elle says Zephaniah 3:17 uses *gil* to describe God spinning for joy over his people, and that Deuteronomy 28:63 pictures God "shushing" over them to do them good.[^4] Read the verse again slowly with that picture in mind. What do you notice about who is rejoicing, over whom, and how? How does it sit beside the picture of God you grew up with?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Elle notes that *chedvah* came from a Mesopotamian festival context, and Nehemiah 8:10 "subversively" uses it of the joy of the Lord, putting YHWH where Enlil had been.[^3] Why might a biblical writer borrow a word from another culture's religion? What does that suggest about how Scripture talks to its surrounding world?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes list assumptions this word study challenges: that worship should be solemn, quiet and restrained, and that emotions are weak or irrational, warning against "cultural preferences disguised as God's character."[^8] Our traditions worship in quite different ways, and each has its own reasons. Treat this diagnostically: what does your tradition's style of worship do well, and which of these Hebrew pictures of joy does it leave little room for? Elle suggests experimenting privately if public expression feels uncomfortable;[^9] what is one small experiment with joy you could try this week?

</div>

### Lot's Wife and the Weight of Commentary <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#lots-wife-and-the-weight-of-commentary}

*Elle asks us to separate what the text says from what we have been told it says.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 19:12–26 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Read slowly and make two lists together. What does the text actually tell us about Lot's wife (her name, her words, her reasons)? What does it tell us about Lot (his hesitation, how he leaves the city)? The notes point out that she is never named and no reason is given for her look back.[^10] What do you notice about where the text puts its attention?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Elle traces two influential readings. The Talmud (Tractate Sanhedrin 108) says she was punished for bad hospitality, even alerting the neighbors to the guests. Origen shrank her to a "little statue of salt," said she was "terrified by the excessive crackling of flames," and made her an allegory of "the flesh" against Lot's "rational understanding in the manly soul."[^6] The notes say such readings "portrayed women as inherently more prone to sin" and have abetted the exclusion of women.[^11] Why do you think readers across centuries filled in her silence this way? What did those readings reveal about the readers more than about her?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Elle names a principle for sparse texts: when Scripture gives little detail, readers must choose whether to assume good or bad motives, and Jesus' teaching about the good eye and the bad eye applies to how we read.[^12] What would it look like to read an ambiguous biblical character with a good eye? What risks might come with that, too?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes observe that what we think is "biblical" may actually be layers of commentary, and that interpretive traditions can cause real harm to real people.[^13] Think of a story or verse you learned in a particular way growing up. How could you check what the text itself says, and what difference has that kind of checking made for you before?

</div>

### A Holy Witness? <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#a-holy-witness}

*Elle offers an alternative reading, held lightly, built from the Torah's own use of words.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** Elle notes that the word translated "pillar," *netziv*, means "garrison" everywhere else, and that salt (*melach*, with a picture of being pulverized into crystals) is linked in the Torah to purity, holiness and covenant: the holy incense and the salt of the sacrifices.[^7] She connects the scene to Abraham's bargaining for the city in Genesis 18 and suggests that Lot's wife may have looked back in grief over what Sodom could have been, so that God "memorializes her compassion rather than punishing her disobedience."[^14] How does this reading strike you? What in the text supports it, and what questions does it leave open?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Common teaching says believers are "salt of the earth" because salt preserves and flavors; the notes suggest adding the Torah's emphasis on purity, covenant and transformation.[^15] How does that change what it might mean for you to be salt?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q11:** Lot's wife has been remembered for centuries as a warning. Who in your own life have you been quick to read with a bad eye, filling in motives you never actually saw? What would a good-eye reading of that person look like this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> This week Elle showed us a God who leaps and spins for joy over his people, and a vocabulary of joy that is loud, physical and shared. Then she turned to a woman with no name and no recorded words, and showed how much commentary has been piled onto her silence, often at the expense of women. The text keeps its attention on Lot's hesitation and choices. Whether or not Elle's reading of a grieving, holy witness persuades us, the episode asks us to read with humility, to notice what the text does and does not say, and to choose a good eye where the text leaves room.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, name either one way you want to practice joy or one person you want to read with a good eye this week.

</div>
