### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Israel asked for a king to make them like the nations and got Saul, an impressive donkey herder from Benjamin. Then God chose David, the overlooked youngest son, a shepherd who would hallow God's name instead of his own.

**This week:** The shepherd boy meets the giant. The episode looks past the familiar underdog story to what the details of 1 Samuel 17 say about Saul, about David, and about us.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The story of David and Goliath is set in the Valley of Elah, one of four valleys leading up to Jerusalem, which made the standoff a critical defensive battle.[^1] For forty days, Goliath came out and Saul did nothing, and the notes read that as a test Saul failed.[^2] The episode notices that Saul came from Benjamin, the tribe famous for its expert slingers, and reads Goliath's repeated sixes and scale armor as a portrait of the serpent from Genesis 3, whose head David crushes.[^3]

The heart of the episode is David's motive, *kiddush hashem* ("sanctification of the name"), and its challenge to "throw your stone": God can use a willing person with a modest skill more than an expert who stays in the tent.[^4] The episode closes with seven observations about David's character and an honest paradox: the "days of David" are remembered as Israel's glory days, even though David's record includes serious failures.[^5]

### Forty Days in the Valley of Elah <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#forty-days-in-the-valley-of-elah}

*The giant is only half the story; the other half is a king who will not move.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read 1 Samuel 17:1–16 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** If you did the preparation, share the numbers you found. The notes describe the setting: the Philistines camped on one ridge and Israel on another, defending a strategic chokepoint to their homeland.[^6] They explain champion warfare (*ish habonim*, "champion man"), in which armies settled a conflict by single combat to avoid a bloodbath.[^7] Goliath came out morning and evening for forty days, timing the notes connect with the daily sacrifice times.[^8] What do you notice about the setting, the challenge, and how Saul and Israel respond in verse 11?

*Read Judges 20:15–16 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes say the Benjamites' left-handed slingers were "elite special forces warriors, the ancient equivalent of Navy SEALs," and that Saul "came from the very tribe renowned for their expertise with the sling."[^9] Forty in the Bible marks a time of testing, and "Saul and Israel had been tested for 40 days and found wanting."[^10] Why do you think a king from the tribe of slingers stayed in his tent for forty days?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes connect a detail from 1 Samuel 13: Saul counts 600 men, while Judges mentioned 700 Benjamite slingers. They read seven as completeness and six as "sinful humanity and incompleteness," so Saul with 600 is "acting in his own strength rather than God's."[^11] What do you make of this kind of number symbolism, and what does it add to (or take away from) your reading?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes describe "Believers who know what they should do but remain paralyzed by fear" and "Leaders who have the position but won't lead when it matters."[^12] Without naming more than you want to, where in your life have you been standing on the ridge for forty days, knowing something needs to be done and not moving?

</div>

### Throw Your Stone <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#throw-your-stone}

*David is not the best slinger in Israel, but he is the one who goes down the hill.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read 1 Samuel 17:32–37 and 1 Samuel 17:45–50 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Listen for David's reasons. The notes point out that he keeps asking who this Philistine is "that he should defy the armies of the living God," and that he fights "so that the whole world will know that there is a God in Israel."[^13] They define *kiddush hashem* as "acting in ways that bring honor and holiness to God's reputation."[^14] What reasons does David give in these verses, and what reasons is he not giving?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes hear a subtle edge in David's words to Saul about the lion and the bear: "I've been doing the work of a shepherd, Saul - what have you been doing?" Saul was supposed to be Israel's shepherd, but he had been "cowering in fear while David has been faithfully protecting his flock."[^15] How does reading David's speech this way change the scene between the boy and the king?

*Read Genesis 3:15 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes observe a pattern of sixes around Goliath (six cubits and a span, a 600-shekel spearhead, a brother with six fingers) along with scale armor, which together portray him as "serpent-like." David crushes his head, "first with the stone, then by cutting it off," which the notes call a pre-Jesus echo of Genesis 3:15.[^16] How does this connection shift the story from a lesson about courage to something larger?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes put the challenge simply: "God can use a stone in the air more than a stone that stays in the riverbed or pocket." Everyone has a stone, "a talent, passion, training, or vocation," and it matters more to be willing to use it than to be the best at it.[^17] What is the stone in your pocket right now, and what would it look like to throw it this month?

</div>

### A Man After God's Own Heart <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#a-man-after-gods-own-heart}

*The episode closes by asking what made David different, and admits he was far from perfect.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read 1 Samuel 30:21–25 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** This is one place where David shares the spoils of battle. The notes say David "shared spoils of victory even with those who didn't contribute to the battle, simply because they belonged to the community," because "He understood God's economy works differently than human merit systems."[^18] What do you notice about the objection in verse 22 and David's answer in verses 23–24?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes list seven observations about David's character: intimate spiritual practice, unconcern with personal fame, a thirst for restorative justice (*mishpat*), seeing the marginalized, generous distribution, counterintuitive leadership, and later forgetfulness.[^19] Which of these surprised you, and which do you most want for yourself?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q11:** The notes name a paradox. David is revered "more than Abraham or Moses in many ways, yet his moral character is often less exemplary," and his record includes "many questionable decisions beyond just the Bathsheba incident."[^20] The notes suggest that "David's heart orientation toward God and willingness to repent mattered more than moral perfection."[^21] What do you think it means to be a person after God's own heart, and what would it mean for you to be described that way, flaws and all?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> For forty days, a king from the tribe of expert slingers stood frozen on a ridge while a giant mocked the armies of the living God. Then a shepherd boy who had been protecting his father's sheep walked down the hill with a sling and a few stones, wanting only that the whole world would know there is a God in Israel. The episode sees in Goliath the serpent of Genesis 3 and in David a head-crushing offspring of the woman. Yet the challenge it leaves is close to home: God uses willing people more than perfect ones. David was not flawless, but his heart was turned toward God's name rather than his own.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is the stone you are going to throw?

</div>
