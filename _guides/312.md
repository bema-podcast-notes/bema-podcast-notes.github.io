### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We traced how John's Gospel went from the gospel of highest honor, to a text Enlightenment critics dismissed as late and Greek, to what Jewish scholar Israel Abrahams in 1924 called "the most Jewish of the four gospels."[^1]

**This week:** Reed Dent opens a new BEMA "anthology" on the parables of Jesus by asking why Jesus put his deepest teaching into short, earthy stories instead of clear statements.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Reed starts with a tension. Our church and Western culture expect that the more important a subject is, the more clearly and directly it should be stated, yet Jesus "doesn't seem to care about any of that list."[^3] Matthew ties Jesus' parables to Psalm 78, a psalm that retells Israel's whole story, which signals that these small stories carry the largest subjects there are.[^4] The word "parable" itself means "something thrown alongside": something we cannot grasp directly, set beside something familiar.[^5]

The hosts then ask why a story can change a person when a direct statement cannot. Many parables answer someone's question and reach a blind spot the person did not know they had, the way Nathan's story reached David.[^6] Reed closes with his own parable about a man who dissects the swallow he loves, a warning against over-explaining, and four tips for reading parables: Engage, Wonder, Identify, and Discover.[^7]

### Opening <span class="guide-time">10 min</span> {#opening}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-opening" markdown="1">

**Opening Question:** Reed opens by asking whether anyone has ever been asked a really big question, such as "How do I forgive someone who keeps hurting me?"[^8] Think of a time you asked a big question like that, or someone asked you one. What kind of answer actually helped?

### Hidden Since Creation, Told in Stories <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#hidden-since-creation-told-in-stories}

*Matthew links Jesus' way of teaching to Israel's whole story, which raises the stakes of these small stories.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 13:10–13 and 13:34–35 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Reed sums up our cultural expectation: when a topic matters, you must be clear, accurate, thorough, and well informed. Marty adds that in church settings vagueness can feel "almost sinful."[^3] Reed is careful to say accurate information is not bad; Jesus just does not treat it as the main tool.[^3] What do you notice in these verses about how Jesus describes his own way of teaching? What surprises you?

*Read Psalm 78:1–8 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** Matthew says the parables fulfill the words "I will open my mouth in parables; I will utter things hidden since the creation of the world." He calls it "the prophet," but the quotation comes from Psalm 78:2, a psalm that covers the Exodus, water from the rock, rebellion, God's anger, exile, and being shepherded under David.[^4] The notes call this *remez* ("hint"), where quoting part of a passage calls the whole passage to mind.[^4] What does the opening of this psalm suggest about the size of the subjects Jesus' short stories carry?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> In the parables God appears as a farmer, a winemaker, a baker, a cranky judge, and a king; people appear as day laborers, party guests, and runaway kids; the kingdom appears as yeast, a mustard seed, and buried treasure. Eugene Peterson notes that almost nothing in them is overtly religious: they are set in "the blood, the soil, the sweat."[^5] Why might Jesus choose ordinary, earthy images for the largest subjects there are?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Marty describes two ways to handle a truth too big to grasp. The Western move is toward the abstract (definitions, propositions, systematic theology); the Hebraic move is toward the concrete (a story, an image, a lived situation). He argues that wisdom "has to be experienced."[^9] Each of our traditions has given us gifts from both directions. Which direction has shaped your own faith most, and what has that given you or left out?

</div>

### Why a Story Gets Past Our Defenses <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#why-a-story-gets-past-our-defenses}

*Many parables answer someone's question, and the story reaches a blind spot a direct statement could not.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read 2 Samuel 12:1–7 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Marty points out that Nathan does not open by accusing David about Bathsheba and Uriah. He tells a story about a rich man who takes a poor man's only lamb, which lowers David's defenses and lets him judge the situation before he realizes it is about him.[^10] What do you notice about the moment David's own verdict turns back on him? What did the story accomplish that an opening accusation could not?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes list three questions that drew a parable from Jesus: Peter asking how many times he must forgive (the Unforgiving Servant), Peter asking what the disciples will get for leaving everything (the Workers in the Vineyard), and a man asking Jesus to make his brother split an inheritance (the Rich Fool). Jesus could have said directly, "Peter, you're unforgiving," but that would be "accusatory and powerless."[^6] Choose one of these. What do you think the person was really asking, and why might a story land where a rebuke would not?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Robert Farrar Capon contrasts right-handed power, which works like "a hammer on a nail" and forces from outside, with left-handed power, which works like the aroma of bread baking and draws people in.[^11] Where have you seen left-handed power at work, in a parent, a teacher, a friend, or a church? What did it change that pressure could not?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Reed offers a two-sided summary: "The things about ourselves that are hardest to hear, we cannot hear directly," and "The things about God that are most crucial to understand, we cannot understand directly."[^12] Marty adds that saving face does not mean dodging accountability; confession, repentance, and repair still follow.[^12] Think of something about yourself you could only hear "slant." Without needing to share details, what made it possible for you to finally hear it?

</div>

### Living in the House, Not Dissecting the Bird <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#living-in-the-house-not-dissecting-the-bird}

*Reed closes with a warning about over-explaining parables and four practical tips for living inside them.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** Kenneth Bailey writes, "Parables do more than explain meaning; they create meaning."[^13] Reed contrasts a conveyor belt of facts with a house that has windows on every side, where you are invited to move in and look out at evangelism, discipleship, justice, or the church.[^13] He also tells of a man who catches the swallow he loves and dissects it so his friends can take notes.[^7] Which habits in the way we study the Bible look most like the conveyor belt or the dissection? What would living in the house look like instead?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Reed's four tips are Engage (track connections and cultural details), Wonder (wander through the background), Identify (take turns being each character), and Discover (hope that God will "worm his way down into us and change something in the soul").[^7] Which of the four comes most naturally to you, and which would you most like to practice during the coming weeks of this series?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Jesus put the largest subjects there are, the whole story Psalm 78 tells, into stories about farmers, bakers, barns, and runaway kids. He did it because the truths we most need are the ones we cannot take in head-on: hard truths about ourselves set off our defenses, and the deepest truths about God shrink into definitions. A parable comes at us slant, lets us see ourselves before we realize it is about us, and leaves the power with the hearer. Over the next weeks the invitation is to live in these stories rather than dissect them.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: finish the line "A story reaches me when..."

</div>
