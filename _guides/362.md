### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Session 7 closed with Marty back from a 100-day sabbatical, saying rest does not have to produce something profound and that different views among the hosts are healthy ("of course they see it differently"). It ended with a preview of the coming study of Isaiah.[^1]

**This week:** We begin a long series on Isaiah. Before reading the book itself, the hosts set up a way to read the prophets, with Abraham Joshua Heschel's *The Prophets* as a guide.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This episode is not a verse-by-verse study. Marty, Brent and Reed Dent explain why Isaiah still matters after studying Jesus, name two common mistakes in reading the prophets, and introduce Heschel's central idea: *pathos*, the prophet's sharing in God's own feelings about the world while standing with the people.[^2]

Along the way they cover the crisis King Ahaz faced in Isaiah 7, Heschel's phrase "few are guilty, all are responsible," God's wrath as something meant to correct rather than destroy, and the way the prophets join personal moral responsibility to social responsibility. Some of this touches on politics and church tradition. The goal is to notice our own habits, not to settle who is right.

### Why Isaiah, and How Not to Read It <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#why-isaiah-and-how-not-to-read-it}

*The hosts begin by asking why anyone should slow down for Isaiah, and how readers usually go wrong with the prophets.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Isaiah is the book Jesus quotes most from the Prophets, just as he quotes Deuteronomy most from the Torah and Psalms most from the Writings. The hosts say coming to Jesus does not make Isaiah unnecessary; Isaiah helps us understand Jesus.[^3] What lines or images from Isaiah do you already carry with you, and where did you first meet them (a sermon, a song, a Christmas program, your own reading)?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty says most books on the prophets fall into one of two traps. One reads the prophets mainly as forecasts of the future. The other dissects the text as literature and leaves it "lifeless." Marty points to Dr. Ephraim Radner's call to "take Scripture seriously" as the middle path.[^4] Which of the two pulls have you felt most in the churches or classes you have been part of? What did that approach help you see, and what might it have kept you from seeing?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Brent asks the question many listeners have: if Jesus fulfilled Isaiah, why go back? The hosts answer that the Bible is an ongoing conversation you keep wrestling with.[^5] What would it look like for you to come to Isaiah this season as a conversation partner rather than a book you already know the answers to?

</div>

### The Prophet Who Feels Fiercely <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-prophet-who-feels-fiercely}

*Heschel's word for the heart of prophecy is* pathos*: the prophet feels what God feels and speaks from inside that experience.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 5:1–7 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Heschel describes the first movement of God's chastisement as a "failure of freedom," pictured in the vineyard of Isaiah 5 that God planted, cleared and walled.[^6] As you listen to the passage, notice the verbs: what does the owner do for the vineyard, and what feelings come through in his voice by the end? Report what you hear before discussing what it means.

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Reed contrasts two pictures of how God responds to sin. One is a "holiness whack-a-mole," where God waits with a hammer to smash each sin as it pops up. The other is a spurned husband or a father whose children will not listen, saying both "I love you so much" and "you are driving me crazy."[^7] The hosts add that every honest parent knows the difference between a parent who *gets* angry and an *angry parent*.[^8] Which picture of God did you absorb growing up, and how does the second picture change the way you hear the vineyard song?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Heschel's phrase "few are guilty, all are responsible" sums up the prophetic call. The prophet counts himself among the guilty and speaks "as one of you," not like a street preacher pointing fingers. His anger comes from compassion and solidarity, not from feeling morally superior.[^9] Where is it easiest for you to stand outside a problem and point at it? What would it change to speak about that problem as someone inside it?

</div>

### Ahaz, History and Two Pairs That Belong Together <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#ahaz-history-and-two-pairs-that-belong-together}

*In Isaiah's day, the question was who really controls history. The hosts also name two pairs our culture tends to split apart.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 7:1–9 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Pekah of Israel and Rezin of Aram had allied against Judah, with Assyria looming behind them. Ahaz assumed powerful kings were shaping history; Isaiah insists that "the world was in the hands of God," so that "what seemed a terror to Ahaz was a trifle to Isaiah."[^10] From the passage, what exactly is Ahaz afraid of, and what does Isaiah ask him to do with that fear?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> At Reed's church, two back-to-back sermons on Amos (a time of prosperity) and Isaiah (a time of threat) led to an observation: scarcity and abundance both lead people to set God aside. In abundance we forget who provided; in scarcity we reach for "Assyria, please help us."[^11] Which of the two is the bigger danger for you right now, and how can you tell?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The prophets hold together personal moral responsibility and social responsibility, which today are usually split between conservative and progressive politics. They also treat idolatry and injustice as cause and effect: what you worship shapes how you treat others. Marty asks how you could pursue social responsibility without personal morality, and how personal morality could be real if it produced no social good.[^12] Without naming anyone else's politics, where do your own loyalties tend to lean on one side of these pairs? What is one concrete step that would help you hold both this month?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The prophets were not mainly fortune-tellers, and they were not only literary artists. In Heschel's picture they felt what God feels (grief, longing, anger and disappointment) and spoke from inside that experience while standing with the people. That picture turns God from a cold judge with a hammer into a grieving parent or a wounded spouse, whose anger is real but meant to correct. "No word is God's last word": even judgment is followed by a future and hope. Whether we live in fear or in comfort, Isaiah asks us what it asked Ahaz: will we trust that history is in God's hands?[^13]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what do you hope to *feel*, not only learn, as we read Isaiah together?

</div>
