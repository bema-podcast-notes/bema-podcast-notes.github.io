### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** The church at Ephesus tested false apostles and refused the Nicolaitan bargain of a pure heart and compromised hands, yet it had forsaken the love it had at first. The episode offered three possibilities for that love: love for Christ, love for one another, and love for the inclusion of Jews and Gentiles.

**This week:** The second letter goes to Smyrna, a wealthy, death-fascinated city loyal to Rome, where believers lived in poverty and faced prison. Every line of the letter answers something in the city's own life.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode recalls BEMA's definition of apocalyptic literature, "the use of pictures and images to convey a message of hope to the present day," and introduces a simple method for the seven letters: study the city's context, connect the text to that context, and understand what the letter meant to its first readers.[^1] Smyrna was named after myrrh, used in preparing the dead; it had an unusual fascination with death and resurrection, used crowns to mark social class, and was more emphatic about emperor worship than most cities in Asia.[^2]

The notes explain that a Jewish community in Smyrna, unusually, rejected the inclusion of Gentile converts and told Rome that Gentile believers were not true Jews, removing them from the exemption that let Jews avoid Caesar worship.[^3] This is the background for the phrase "synagogue of Satan," which the episode insists is not a blanket statement about Judaism.[^4] The letter ends with a promise of a crown of life and protection from the second death, a promise the notes connect to Polycarp, Smyrna's bishop, martyred decades later.[^5] Because the episode is short, this guide spends extra time in the text itself.

### A City of Myrrh and Crowns <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#a-city-of-myrrh-and-crowns}

*The episode builds a method for reading all seven letters: context first, then connect the text to it.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Revelation 2:8–11 aloud, slowly, twice.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes list three steps Marty uses for each letter: "Context is everything," connect the text to context, and understand the original message.[^6] They describe Smyrna as a wealthy port city named after myrrh, a major export "used in preparing dead bodies," with an "Unusual infatuation with death and the concept of resurrection," a center of medicine and of pagan resurrection cults, and a city known for using crowns (wreaths, bronze, silver, gold) to mark social classes.[^7] Read the letter again with that list in mind. Which words or phrases jump out now that would have sounded different to someone living in Smyrna?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes explain that Smyrna formed an alliance with Rome in the third century BC, created the cult of Roma, the goddess personifying Rome, and was "More emphatic about emperor worship than most other cities in Asia."[^8] Market transactions often involved oaths or sacrifices to the emperor, and guild membership required emperor worship, so refusing meant economic exclusion.[^9] Jesus says, "I know your afflictions and your poverty, yet you are rich." How do you hear that line differently knowing that these believers' poverty was the price of refusing to call Caesar a god?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes lay out the letter as six movements of encouragement: recognition ("I know your afflictions"), reversal ("You are rich"), justice, warning, promise and hope.[^10] Which of those six would you most need to hear if you were a believer in Smyrna, and why?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The letter opens by naming Jesus as the one "who died and came to life again," which the notes read as speaking directly to a city fascinated with death and resurrection myths.[^11] Where in your life, or in our culture, do you see a fascination with death or with starting over, and what does it mean to you that Jesus is introduced this way?

</div>

### Slander From the Synagogue <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#slander-from-the-synagogue}

*This phrase has a painful history of misuse, and the episode works hard to read it in its own context.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Revelation 2:9 aloud, then Romans 11:11–24.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes report that, unusually, Smyrna's Jewish community "rejected the inclusion of Gentile converts" and wrote to Rome declaring Gentile believers were not true Jews. That removed the Gentile believers from the exemption that allowed Jews to avoid Caesar worship, and the result was severe persecution and economic hardship.[^12] Looking at Revelation 2:9, what exactly does Jesus say these opponents are doing, and to whom?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes are emphatic that "synagogue of Satan" is "NOT a blanket condemnation of Judaism." It specifically addresses Jews in Smyrna who rejected Gentile inclusion, echoing Paul's teaching in Romans 9–11 that the essence of being Jewish is being a light to the Gentiles. The notes compare it to Jesus calling Peter "Satan" when Peter opposed God's purposes.[^13] They warn that without context the phrase "has been (mis)used as anti-Semitic ammunition."[^14] Read Romans 11:17–24 again. How does Paul's picture of the olive tree help us hold this phrase responsibly, and what would it look like for Christians today to refuse to misuse it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes suggest the letter "Reverses the accusation": those excluding Gentiles are the ones failing to be what Israel was called to be.[^15] Where have you seen a community of faith define itself by who it keeps out rather than by who it welcomes, and what did that cost?

</div>

### Faithful to the Point of Death <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#faithful-to-the-point-of-death}

*The letter does not promise that the suffering will stop. It promises what lies on the other side of it.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Revelation 2:10–11 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes read the "10 days" of persecution as likely symbolic, completeness (seven plus three), "a complete period of suffering within community," and observe that apocalyptic literature uses numbers "to tell a story."[^16] They read the crown of life against Smyrna's habit of marking status with crowns, and the "second death" as a new idea for a city infatuated with death, shifting focus "from physical death to spiritual/eternal death."[^17] How do the crown and the second death answer the specific fears and values of this city?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes tell the story of Polycarp, bishop of Smyrna and a disciple of John, who refused to deny Christ, told his accusers he had served Christ eighty-six years without being wronged, and was martyred around 156 AD. They call him an example of the "faithful unto death" encouragement of Revelation 2:10.[^18] What does a story like Polycarp's do for a community that is suffering, and what does it do for you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes say the letter offers "hope to sustain believers through present suffering, not escapist fantasies about the distant future."[^19] Where are you, or someone you love, in a season of hardship that is not going away soon, and what would it mean to hear "I know" from Jesus in that place?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Smyrna smelled of myrrh, the spice of burial, and loved stories of death and rising again. It crowned its citizens by rank and outdid its neighbors in devotion to Rome. Believers who refused to call Caesar a god lost their livelihoods, and when some in the local synagogue declared them outsiders, they lost their protection too. Jesus does not promise them an easy road. He tells them he knows, that they are rich, that he is the one who truly died and came to life, and that the faithful will wear a crown no city can give and never be touched by the second death.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one "crown" our culture offers that you are tempted to chase, and what would it mean to set it down?

</div>
