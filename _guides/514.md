### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Elle argued that God's original promise in Exodus 23 was liberation, that *ḥerem* means "devoted to God" rather than "destroyed," and that much of the darkness in Joshua comes from Israel adding its own methods to what God said.

**This week:** We meet the first of the series' overlooked women: Rahab, a Canaanite outsider who tells Israel's rescue story back to them and asks to be included in it.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Elle Grover Fricks leads Brent Billings and Reed Dent through her own translation of Joshua 2, the first of several stories about "un-underlined" women in Joshua and Judges. Rahab is a Canaanite sex worker who hides two Israelite spies, deceives the king of Jericho, and negotiates rescue for her family[^1]. The group notices how much of the Exodus story echoes through the passage, what the names and key Hebrew words mean, and how comic and cinematic the storytelling is.

Elle then asks what Rahab's story adds to the popular idea of "biblical womanhood," and names four parts of Rahab's faith, ending with the courage to join a new people. Some of this touches on questions our traditions answer differently, especially women's roles and Rahab's lie. The aim is not to settle those questions tonight but to look honestly at the woman the text actually shows us.

### Names and Echoes <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#names-and-echoes}

*From the very first verse, the narrator signals that this is a rescue story, and an outsider is the one who tells it.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Joshua 2:1–17 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Reed noticed that the word "melt" sent him back to the Song of the Sea (Exodus 15:15), and from there more echoes of Exodus appeared[^2]. Look back over the passage. Which details remind you of the Exodus story, and which ones did the group notice that you missed?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Elle's translation renders the meaning of each name: Yehoshua (Joshua) means "Yah delivers," Shittim means "acacia grove," the wood of the tabernacle, and Rachav (Rahab) means "spacious." She offers three readings of Rahab's name: a crude pun on her profession, a nod to her hospitality toward outsiders, or a callback to God's promise of a "spacious" land, the opposite of *Mitzrayim* (Egypt), which suggests narrowness and oppression[^3]. Which reading do you find most compelling, and what does it add to the story?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes put the point this way: a Canaanite woman is the one telling Israel's rescue story back to them. The outsider knows the insiders' story and uses it to ask for the same kind of rescue[^4]. What do you make of the fact that an outsider understands Israel's God this well? When has someone outside your own circle reminded you of something true about your faith that you had stopped noticing?

</div>

### Rahab Takes Charge <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#rahab-takes-charge}

*The narrator paints Rahab as shrewd, bold and in command, and the spies' response surprises.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Elle points out the comedy: Joshua orders the spies to "walk, see the land," and they go straight to a prostitute's house. Rahab then uses the Hebrew imperative again and again, which is unusual in the Bible: she tells men to "walk," to "be hidden," and everyone does exactly what she says. The king even comes to her and *asks* her to send the men out[^5]. Look again at verses 1–7 and 15–16. Who gives the orders, who follows them, and what surprises you about how the narrator portrays her?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Elle reads *zonah* plainly as "prostitute," seeing no basis for the softer "innkeeper" reading. Reed adds that Rahab is not a *reformed* prostitute: she is still in that life when God uses her, which is not an endorsement of sex work but does challenge the idea that a person must "clean up" before serving God. Elle also notes that Rahab was not at Sinai, and her sense of integrity does not treat lying as wrong[^6]. How did you first learn Rahab's story in your tradition? What does this plainer reading add, and what does it unsettle for you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Elle is careful not to be prescriptive: narrative describes rather than commands. But because Hebrews 11 holds Rahab up as a model of faith, her qualities belong somewhere within what "biblical" womanhood can look like. Rahab is financially independent, unmarried, provides for her family, is hospitable at great risk, and is confident, competent and persuasive. Elle honors stay-at-home motherhood as a real gift; her concern is with mandating one pattern, which harms single women, women without children, empty-nesters and widows[^7]. Our traditions hold a range of convictions on women's roles, and this question is not asking you to change yours. Which of Rahab's qualities do you see welcomed in your church community, and which have a harder time finding room? Who around you might be encouraged by the wider picture her story offers?

</div>

### The Courage to Join a New People <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#the-courage-to-join-a-new-people}

*Rahab does not only want to escape; she asks to belong.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The spies answer Rahab with honor: "Our souls under yours unto death." They promise to do *chesed* (active, concrete compassion) and *emet* (truth firm enough to support a life). The cord Rahab lowers is a *tikvah*, a word that means both "cord" and "hope"[^8]. Elle names four parts of Rahab's faith: she is willing to believe what she heard, she gives intellectual assent to who God is, she trusts God's hospitality to make room for her (unlike Lot, who only asks to escape), and she has the courage to join a new people[^9]. Which of the four comes most naturally to you, and which is hardest?

*Read Jonah 4:1–3 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Reed connects Rahab to Jonah, where the pagan sailors and the Ninevites appeal to a God who might show mercy while the prophet himself wants destruction. The principle: the "wrong people" are often the ones who see something true about God. Elle suggests that in polarized times we ask our opponents, "What element of God might you be keying into that I've neglected?"[^10] Think of someone you strongly disagree with. How might you answer that question about them?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** Rahab probably lived the rest of her life known as "the Canaanite" and "the woman from Jericho." Her house was in the wall, so her entry into God's people came as her old world literally collapsed[^11]. Elle names real obstacles to joining a faith community today: church hurt, busy schedules, sports, screens, and fear that a community won't match one's views exactly[^12]. Where are you holding back from belonging, in this group, a church or a friendship, and what is one small step toward it? Share only what you are comfortable sharing.

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Rahab is far more than "the prostitute who hid the spies." She is shrewd, independent, hospitable at great risk, unafraid to give orders, and ready to deceive a king to protect strangers. In her mouth, Israel's own rescue story becomes an argument for compassion toward an outsider. The names, the wordplay and the Exodus echoes all say the same thing: God's story has always made spacious room for people no one expected. Rahab did not clean up her life first; she believed, trusted there would be a place for her, and found the courage to join a new family while her old world fell.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what does Rahab's story give you courage to do this week?

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 515 — Women in Joshua & Judges: Daughters of Zelophehad](/guides/515)** (53:08): Five sisters whose father died without sons bring their case publicly before Moses, Eleazar and the whole assembly, so their family's land will not pass to their uncles. God's reply is immediate, "The daughters of Zelophehad speak rightly," and God rewrites the inheritance law; years later the sisters return to Joshua to make sure the promise is kept. It continues the series' look at women who speak boldly, and adds a lesson for leaders on hearing a cry for justice.

**What to bring next week:** Read Numbers 27:1–11 and Joshua 17:3–4. Note what objection the sisters answer before anyone raises it, and how God's answer compares with what they asked for.
