### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Marty and Brent named the Crusades as Empire thinking rather than Shalom and urged us to own this history rather than disown it, because "a dark history disowned by the descendants of it will be bound to repeat itself."[^1]

**This week:** We follow a bankrupt Christendom from the end of the Crusades to the edge of the Protestant Reformation, and meet the voices who challenged the system from inside it.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The Crusades left the church financially and spiritually drained. In its need to rebuild, the church began to attach payments to the sacramental system, and the sale of indulgences became one of the clearest abuses of the era. Against this background the episode introduces John Wycliffe, called the "Morning Star of the Reformation," and Jan Hus, often called a "true father of the Reformation." It then traces the forces that prepared the ground for Luther, Zwingli and Calvin: medieval nationalism, the Western Schism, the printing press and the spread of Scripture in the languages people actually spoke.[^3]

The hosts paint with broad strokes and acknowledge that they tell this story from a Western, American, Evangelical perspective. They also insist on nuance: the sacraments were first understood as conduits of grace rather than magic, and Brent keeps repeating that "there's always a remnant" of faithful people, even in the darkest periods. This guide asks us to look at the period diagnostically, asking how good things became tools of control, rather than to condemn any tradition.[^4]

### A Bankrupt Church and the Sale of Grace <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#a-bankrupt-church-and-the-sale-of-grace}

*The church's need for money after the Crusades bent a system meant to carry grace into a system that extracted payment.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The medieval church recognized seven sacraments: baptism, Eucharist, confession (penance), confirmation, marriage, holy orders and anointing of the sick. The notes say these were originally understood as "conduits" through which believers could experience God's grace in daily life, not as mechanisms of salvation itself and not as magical rituals.[^5] Which of these practices does your tradition keep (by whatever name it uses), and how were you taught to describe what happens in them?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** After the Crusades, Christendom needed to rebuild but lacked the money. Parishioners coming to confession were increasingly required to make financial contributions, and indulgences promised forgiveness or reduced time in purgatory in exchange for payment. This worked in a largely illiterate society where priests held access to a Latin Bible that ordinary people could not read.[^6] Trace the chain from the empty treasury to the sale of indulgences. At what point did a real spiritual need become a lever, and what made ordinary people so easy to move with it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The hosts draw a line from this period to our own. Just as medieval priests used theology to control illiterate people, modern leaders can use specialized or "insider" knowledge to manipulate a congregation, and the fear-based logic of indulgences can show up today in teaching that leverages guilt and fear for financial gain.[^7] What practices or habits in a church protect people from this kind of leverage, and which of them do you see (or wish you saw) in your own congregation?

</div>

### Wycliffe, Hus and the Remnant <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#wycliffe-hus-and-the-remnant}

*Two pre-Reformation reformers challenged the system in very different ways, and the faithful were never entirely absent.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Read this list of John Wycliffe's contributions aloud, slowly. According to the notes, Wycliffe (c. 1320s–1384):[^8]

- attacked the imperial privilege and excessive wealth of the church hierarchy;
- advocated dissolving the sharp distinction between clergy and laypeople;
- argued that Scripture should not be held captive in Latin but translated into vernacular languages;
- challenged papal authority and the doctrine of transubstantiation;
- inspired the Wycliffe Bible, the first complete translation of Scripture into English.

What do you notice about these five items? Which are about access, which are about power, and which one do you think would have cost him the most?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Jan Hus (c. 1372–1415) built on Wycliffe's work, challenged indulgences and led what became the Bohemian Revolt. He was executed as a heretic at the Council of Constance in 1415, and his followers fought the Hussite Wars against the papacy. The notes describe his approach as "zealot" methods: using imperial and military means to fight the empire itself. Marty is unsure about endorsing those methods but treats Hus, like the zealots of Jesus's day, as one legitimate response to systemic oppression, even if not the only or best one. The episode recalls the range of responses in Jesus's world: Pharisees, Sadducees, Essenes and Zealots.[^9] When you see an institution you love doing real harm, which instinct do you tend to follow (reform from inside, withdraw, or resist openly), and what do you think shaped that instinct in you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Throughout the episode Brent keeps saying, "There's always a remnant." The notes compare this to the Essenes in Session 3, who removed themselves from corrupted institutions while preserving true faith, and say the remnant idea keeps us from claiming that all medieval clergy were corrupt or that no genuine faith existed.[^10] Who has been a "remnant" voice in your own story, someone who stayed faithful inside an imperfect church or system? What did they actually do that you still remember?

</div>

### Nations, Presses and the Priesthood of All Believers <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#nations-presses-and-the-priesthood-of-all-believers}

*Politics and technology prepared the ground for the Reformation, and a new idea gave ordinary believers responsibility for the faith.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> In the 1300s and 1400s, people began identifying first with their nation, becoming "French Catholic" rather than simply "Catholic." The Western Schism (1378–1417), with rival men claiming to be pope, further eroded confidence in Rome. When the Reformation came, Germans tended to follow Luther, the French Reformed followed Calvin, and the Swiss followed Zwingli. The notes observe that denominational affiliation today often reflects cultural, regional or family identity as much as theological conviction.[^11] Without judging anyone's tradition, how much of your own church identity came from family, region or culture, and how much from conviction you reached yourself?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Gutenberg's printing press (around 1440) made books cheaper and helped spread Scripture in vernacular languages, from Wycliffe's English Bible to Erasmus's Greek New Testament (1516), Luther's German Bible and Tyndale's English translation. Luther's "priesthood of all believers" made orthodoxy (right belief) and orthopraxy (right practice) the responsibility of the whole community rather than an elite clerical class. The hosts compare the press to the internet, and name the tension between open access and the continuing need for scholarly expertise and wisdom.[^12] In a group like ours, where everyone can open a Bible and search online, what does it look like to carry that shared responsibility well, and when do we still need to lean on people with deeper training?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The years from 1300 to 1550 show how good things can be bent. Sacraments meant to carry grace became a way to raise money from frightened people who could not read the Bible for themselves. Yet there was always a remnant: Wycliffe putting Scripture into English, Hus refusing to be silent, and countless unnamed believers who stayed faithful. Nationalism, a schism in the papacy and a printing press opened the door to the Reformation, and with it came the idea that the whole community, not only the clergy, is responsible for right belief and right practice. That gift came with a cost the coming weeks will show: a church that began to split along national and theological lines.[^13]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, name one way you will take up your share of the "priesthood of all believers" this week.

</div>
