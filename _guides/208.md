**This week:** The first Midrash episode of Session 6 answers BEMA's most frequently asked question and, along the way, teaches how to read Midrash well.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Back in Episode 8, BEMA taught the Midrashic tradition that Iscah (Genesis 11:29) and Sarai are the same person. Listeners noticed a problem: if Abraham was Terah's firstborn and Sarai was ten years younger than Abraham, then her father Haran would have been only eight to ten years old when she was born.[^3] Marty and Brent explain that the Midrash holds two traditions here, one in which Haran fathers Iscah miraculously at eight and a more popular one in which Abraham was not the firstborn at all.[^2]

That second tradition connects to a pattern across Genesis: Shem, Abraham, Isaac, Jacob and Joseph are all chosen though none is the firstborn.[^4] The episode also explains what Midrash is (multiple streams of rabbinic commentary, not a single book), what *semikhah* means, and why the best question to ask of any Midrash is "why?"[^5]

### The Most Asked Question <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-most-asked-question}

*A small detail in a genealogy turns out to raise BEMA's most common question.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 11:26–32 aloud, then Genesis 17:17.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Using the family tree you sketched (or sketching it together now), who is listed, and in what order? What do you notice about what the text tells us of Haran compared with Abram? Now add Genesis 17:17. Walk through the age problem the notes describe and explain it in your own words.[^3]

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes point out that Noah's sons are listed "Shem, Ham, and Japheth," but the Table of Nations in Genesis 10 presents them in a different order, signaling that the first listing "reflects importance rather than birth order."[^6] What does it change to know that a biblical list can rank by importance rather than by age? Where do we still list people that way today?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Marty admits he did not catch the age problem at first, and says, "I'm constantly in learning mode as well. I'm constantly learning new things. I'm constantly evolving."[^7] How does a teacher's admission like that affect your trust in them? How do you tend to respond when a question exposes something you had not noticed in your own reading?

</div>

### Not the Firstborn <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#not-the-firstborn}

*The answer Marty prefers fits a pattern that runs through the whole book of Genesis.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes trace the pattern: Shem over his older brother, Abraham over Haran, Isaac over Ishmael, Jacob over Esau, and Joseph over his ten older brothers.[^4] In a world where primogeniture (firstborn inheritance rights) "was the dominant social structure," what would this pattern have said to the people first hearing these stories? Why do you think Genesis keeps repeating it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes draw this lesson: "God's calling and election are not based on human hierarchies, social position, or birth order."[^4] In churches and communities today, who tends to be treated as the "firstborn," the people expected to lead? Who tends to be overlooked? What would it look like for our congregations to take this pattern seriously?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Think of a time you were the one nobody expected to be chosen, or the one everyone expected to be chosen. What does the Genesis pattern say to that experience? (Share only if you want to.)

</div>

### Asking Why <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#asking-why}

*Midrash is less about settling what happened and more about learning what the tradition wants us to see.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Maimonides said, "Any man who believes everything in the Midrash is a fool. Any man who believes nothing in the Midrash is a heretic." The notes say the key question is "Why is the Midrash trying to show me this?"[^8] Try that question on the two traditions. If Haran fathered Iscah at eight, what might that tradition want to emphasize? If Abraham was not the firstborn, what does that one emphasize? What do you learn from letting both stand?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty compares this to the difference between documented church history (like Eusebius) and church tradition (like competing claims about where apostles are buried).[^9] Every tradition in our room carries stories and practices of different weight. How do you tell the difference in your own tradition between what is documented and what is tradition? How might asking "why does this tradition exist?" help you hold it with more respect and more honesty?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> A question about one man's age opened up two lessons. First, Genesis keeps choosing the one who was not supposed to be chosen: Shem, Abraham, Isaac, Jacob and Joseph. The covenant invitation does not follow birth order or status. Second, Midrash is a conversation among generations of scholars who kept multiple traditions alive and asked what each one was trying to teach. Maimonides warns against believing everything and against believing nothing, and the question "why?" helps us find the path between those two.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: who is one "not-firstborn" person in your life, or in our church, whose voice you want to listen for more closely?

</div>
