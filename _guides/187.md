### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** The woman clothed with the sun was carried on eagle's wings as Israel was carried out of Egypt, and the beasts took shape in Domitian's gymnasium and the marketplace stamp. John called his readers to patient endurance and faithfulness, because they knew how the story ends.

**This week:** The two sides line up: the Lamb and his followers against the beast and his. Then seven bowls are poured out, and John blesses those who die faithful.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode frames Revelation 14–16 as a contest that looks weighted toward the beast, yet the angels celebrate early because the outcome is certain.[^1] The seven bowls deliberately echo the plagues of Exodus, and the notes insist that for biblically literate readers this imagery would evoke not "Oh no!" but "Oh yes!": God's wrath here is restorative justice, a purifying fire that puts things right.[^2] Armageddon, *Har Megiddo*, "mountain of Megiddo," points back to the historic battlefield at Megiddo rather than forward to an encoded future event.[^3]

The episode also sits with the cost. Marty argues that Domitian's persecution was among the worst the church faced under Rome, and John's blessing on "the dead who die in the Lord" acknowledges that faithfulness could mean death.[^4] Reading Revelation in the ruins of Ephesus, the hosts reflect that those believers' deeds really did follow them.[^5]

### Two Teams on the Field <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#two-teams-on-the-field}

*Chapter 14 sets the Lamb and his followers directly opposite the beast and his.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Revelation 14:1–5 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes describe two teams: the seven-headed beast with followers marked on their foreheads and hands, and the slain Lamb on Mount Zion with 144,000 followers bearing God's name on their foreheads.[^6] Compare these verses with Revelation 13:16–17, which we read last week. What parallels and contrasts do you notice between the two groups?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes suggest the harps may recall Psalm 137, where captives in Babylon hung their harps in the trees, so that "now there is reason to sing again."[^7] Read Psalm 137:1–4. The notes also say the matchup looks weighted toward the intimidating beast, yet the angels "celebrate early because the end of the story is inevitable."[^8] Why might it matter that the singing starts before the contest is over?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes connect the sound of rushing waters to the Asclepion in Pergamum, where healing waters flowed in honor of the god Asclepius, setting the healing voice of a false god against the true voice from heaven.[^9] When have you been able to sing, pray or give thanks before you knew how a situation would turn out, and what made that possible?

</div>

### Bowls That Remember Egypt <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#bowls-that-remember-egypt}

*The bowls of wrath are terrifying on the surface, and the episode asks us to hear them as John's first readers would have.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Revelation 15:1–4 and 16:1–12 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes match the bowls to Exodus: festering sores to boils, the sea and the rivers to the Nile turned to blood, darkness on the beast's throne to the plague of darkness, and frogs to the Exodus frogs.[^10] Go through the bowls one by one and name the Exodus plague each one recalls. Notice also that the song in 15:3 is "the song of God's servant Moses and of the Lamb." What story is John telling his readers they are in?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Two backward-pointing details stand out in the notes. Armageddon is *Har Megiddo*, a fortress city on the great highway near the Jezreel Valley "where battle after battle occurred," a place students visit on BEMA trips to Israel.[^11] And when John recalls Isaiah 8 at the fifth bowl, the reader who knows Isaiah would catch the hint: "God's deliverance is at hand."[^12] How does it change the feel of these chapters to see them pointing back to known places and texts rather than forward to a hidden timetable?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes say God's wrath here is "not indiscriminate destruction but restorative justice": "a purifying fire that leaves what ought to remain," completed and finite, and followed by songs of deliverance.[^13] They add that biblically literate readers would respond not with "Oh no!" but with "Oh yes!"[^14] Our traditions have taught about God's wrath in different ways, and many of us carry more than one picture. How does this reading compare with what you were taught? And if God's wrath means putting things right, what injustice in our world do you most long to see put right?

</div>

### Deeds That Follow <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#deeds-that-follow}

*In the middle of the conflict, John pauses for a blessing that would have landed hard in a persecuted church.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Revelation 14:12–13 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes describe this "stark acknowledgment of martyrdom" as faithful witness rather than defeat.[^15] Marty argues that Domitian's persecution was "one of the worst in all of church history in the Roman Empire," and that the up-and-down nature of persecution under different Caesars meant believers never knew when the next wave would come.[^16] What do you think "their deeds will follow them" offered to people who were burying friends?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Sitting in the shaded ruins of Ephesus, the hosts reflected that the faithfulness of those believers rippled through two thousand years. The notes then ask, "Do we believe our deeds today matter and will ripple through eternity?"[^17] Which ordinary act of faithfulness in your life right now do you hope will ripple further than you can see?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> John lines up the two teams, and the beast looks like the favorite. But the angels are already singing, because the end of the story is not in doubt. The bowls pour out the plagues of Egypt again, and readers who knew the Exodus would hear in them not terror but the promise that God was about to deliver his people and put the world right. And in the middle of it all is a blessing for those who die faithful: they will rest, and their deeds will follow them, as they have followed those believers in Ephesus all the way to us.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one faithful deed you want to do this week, even if no one ever sees it?

</div>
