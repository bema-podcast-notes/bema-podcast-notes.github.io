### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Grace always outpaces sin ("how much more"), our identity as image-bearers runs deeper than our fallenness, and baptism pictures burying a fear-driven life and rising to a life of trust.

**This week:** Paul uses slavery and marriage to describe what has changed, names the struggle every believer still feels, and lands on the assurance that there is no condemnation and nothing can separate us from God's love.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode explores Romans 6–8 through Paul's everyday metaphors. Slavery was familiar to his readers, since the notes estimate that about one in three people in the Roman Empire lived under some form of it, and marriage law gave Paul a picture of being released by a death.[^1] The famous line "wages of sin is death" (6:23) is heard as the end of an argument about wages and gifts that began with Abraham in Romans 4.[^2]

The episode then turns to Romans 7. It reads the Greek word *sarx* ("flesh") as our animal appetites rather than a "sinful nature," insists that the law is holy and good, and describes sin as an intruder rather than our true identity.[^3] It closes with Romans 8: no condemnation, a creation that groans for its own restoration, and a love that not even Caesar's swords could break.[^4]

### Two Masters, Two Marriages <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#two-masters-two-marriages}

*Paul uses the slavery and marriage of his world to picture the change that has taken place.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Romans 6:15–23 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** What two masters does Paul describe, and what does each one give in the end? Look closely at verse 23: which side gets "wages," and which side gets a "gift"? The notes connect this verse to Paul's argument about "wages vs. gifts that began in Romans 4 with Abraham."[^5] How does hearing verse 23 as the end of that argument change the way it sounds?

*Read Romans 7:1–12 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes explain Paul's marriage picture: believers "have died to their old covenant relationship based on working for justification and are now free to enter a new covenant relationship with Christ based on grace and faith."[^6] They also stress that this does not mean abandoning Torah observance, and that "the law is not the problem - it's holy, righteous, and good."[^7] Why do you think Paul is so careful, in verses 7 and 12, to defend the law at exactly this point?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Paul's readers knew what it was to belong to a master. What are the "masters" people serve today without quite noticing, and what would it look like for you to live as someone who has been set free from one of them?

</div>

### The Struggle Within <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-struggle-within}

*Paul describes the experience of wanting to do good and finding something else at work in him.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Romans 7:14–25 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Go back through the passage and notice the pronouns. When Paul says "I," what does that "I" want? What does he say is doing the things he hates (look at verses 17 and 20)? What does verse 25 suggest about where the struggle ends?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes say *sarx* "is often mistranslated as 'sinful nature' but actually refers to our animal appetites or beast-like nature," connecting back to Genesis, where humans are called to master their animal impulses and choose when to say "enough."[^8] They also call sin "an intruder within us, not our true identity."[^9] What difference does it make to describe the struggle as image-bearers against appetites, rather than a sinful self against God?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe the law as "a pedagogue (teacher) that reveals what sin is and points toward righteousness," adding that "The issue isn't the law itself but how sin uses the law to produce rebellion within us."[^10] Where have you seen a good rule stir up the very thing it forbids?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The episode argues that Romans 7 "isn't evidence of total depravity but proof that our true self (made in God's image) conflicts with the sin that dwells within us," so that "The struggle validates our goodness rather than negates it."[^11] Our traditions have taught different things about human nature, and some of us were taught that this passage proves how deep sin goes. Whatever you were taught, how might this reading change the way you face your own struggle this week?

</div>

### No Condemnation <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#no-condemnation}

*Paul's argument arrives at the assurance that nothing can separate us from God's love.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Romans 8:1–2 and Romans 8:31–39 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** List everything Paul names in verses 35–39 that cannot separate us from God's love. The notes point out that Roman believers faced literal persecution and that Paul's assurance meant nothing, "including Caesar's condemnation or Roman swords," could separate them from God's love.[^12] Which items on Paul's list would have felt most real to them, and which feel most real to you?

*Read Romans 8:18–23 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say "Creation itself groans for redemption, emphasizing restoration of this world, not escape from it."[^13] What does Paul say creation is waiting for? How would believing that this world will be restored, rather than abandoned, shape the way you live in it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes quote Freud: "how bold one gets when one is sure of being loved."[^14] They say no condemnation frees believers to live "without the crushing weight of guilt and shame that comes from trying to earn righteousness."[^15] If you were fully sure you were loved and not condemned, what is one thing you would do more boldly this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Paul's readers knew slavery and marriage law, and he used both to say that a death has set us free from an old master and an old way of working for justification. The law is holy and good; the problem is sin, an intruder that uses even good things against us. The struggle of Romans 7 is real, but the episode reads it as the cry of an image-bearer wrestling with appetites, a struggle that shows our goodness rather than cancelling it. And the argument ends in Romans 8: no condemnation, a creation waiting to be restored, and a love nothing can break. The struggle is real, but it is not the end of the story.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one thing you would do more boldly if you were sure of being loved?

</div>
