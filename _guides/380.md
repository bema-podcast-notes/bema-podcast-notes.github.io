### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Reed opened the Psalms series by reading them as poetry that gives honest words for every season of life, using Brueggemann's orientation, disorientation and new orientation.[^1]

**This week:** Brent, Reed and Josh walk slowly through Psalm 13, a short lament of disorientation that turns, almost suddenly, toward trust.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Psalm 13 skips any polite opening and begins with "How long?" The hosts look closely at its Hebrew. The word for "forget," *shakach*, suggests being misplaced rather than deliberately rejected, and the idiom of God's "hidden face" appears across Scripture with several different meanings. They follow the psalm as it escalates from questions to demands, then turns in verse 5 to trust in God's *hesed* (steadfast love) and a vow to sing.[^3]

Much of the conversation is pastoral. Josh, who works in a pastoral role, describes what helps and what hurts when someone feels abandoned by God, and Reed passes on the advice that "grief is a dance," so the grieving person should lead. The main point is that Scripture makes honest room for God's absence, and that trust and anguish can be held together.[^4] Some questions touch personal pain; share only what you choose.

**Opening Question:** The episode compares God's silence to an unanswered text: "Did you forget to check your phone, or are you ignoring me? Did I do something?"[^5] Think of a time you waited on a reply or a call that did not come. What stories did you tell yourself while you waited?

### Forgotten, or Mislaid? <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#forgotten-or-mislaid}

*Psalm 13 begins without preamble and asks whether God has forgotten.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Psalm 13 aloud, slowly. If you prepared, share where you marked the change in tone.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes say the psalm goes "straight for the jugular." "How long" appears four times in verses 1–2, so the repetition itself becomes a protest, and the specific situation is never named.[^6] What do you notice about how the psalm opens and how it is built? Why might leaving the situation unnamed make it easier for many people to pray?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** Reed contrasts two Hebrew words for "forget." *Nashah* means willful neglect or deliberate abandonment. *Shakach*, the word in Psalm 13, means misplacing or losing track of something; the Hebrew title of *Home Alone* uses it, because Kevin was overlooked rather than rejected. The hosts are careful not to call anything accidental on God's part. The point is the psalmist's perception: "I haven't been thrown out. I've been mislaid." Brent suggests this lets the psalmist give God *ayin tovah* (a "good eye," the benefit of the doubt).[^7] How does feeling mislaid differ from feeling rejected? How might that difference make trust possible by the end?

*Divide these four verses among readers and read them aloud: Genesis 4:14; Ezekiel 39:23; Isaiah 54:8; Job 13:24.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The hosts trace the "hidden face" idiom through these texts. Cain says he will be hidden from God's face because of his own guilt. In Ezekiel the hidden face describes exile caused by unfaithfulness. Isaiah 54 says it lasts only "for a moment," so "How long?" has an answer even if no one can say when. Job asks "Why do you hide your face?" right after insisting he has done nothing wrong.[^8] What reason or result does each passage give for the hidden face? What does it suggest that Scripture holds all of these side by side?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q4:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Josh notes that the sufferer often asks, "Did I do something?" He also points out that exile happens on two levels, national and individual, and that some people caught in a national exile they did not cause would never personally see the return.[^9] When you, or someone close to you, have felt God's absence, which explanation has been most tempting to reach for? What happens to a person when guilt is assumed but cannot be found? Share only what you are comfortable sharing.

</div>

### From Demand to Dance <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#from-demand-to-dance}

*The psalm escalates before it turns, and the turn does not cancel the anguish.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** In verses 3–4 the tone shifts from questions to demands: "Look on me and answer." The enemy grows from singular to plural, piling one weight on another. The psalmist asks for "a response, not necessarily a solution": "If I hear your voice, I'll have the courage to uncurl from this fetal position."[^10] What is the difference between asking God for a response and asking for a solution? What would it mean for you to pray that bluntly?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The hosts offer two readings of the turn in verse 5. Brent hears a pot boiling over and then a deep breath: once everything is out, the psalmist can say, "But you've been good to me." Josh hears the psalmist playing the worst case to its end and then snapping back to memory: "I remember Egypt, I remember the desert." The same Hebrew word for "rejoice" moves from the enemies in verse 4 to the psalmist in verse 5, and its root may involve circling or dancing. The word for "good to me," *gamal*, is linked with weaning a child, which Josh hears as, "I don't doubt you, I'm just so alone right now."[^11] Which reading of the turn rings truer to your own experience, and why? Josh insists that trust and anguish "coexist from start to finish." Where have you seen both at once?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Josh traces *hesed* to its first appearance, Lot fleeing Sodom. Lot's story looks like a fall, but traced far enough it leads to Ruth and then David. Reed reads the psalm through Jesus: salvation is not escaping the fall but being raised beyond it. Josh concludes, "If resurrection is on our side, then it is actually okay if everything goes wrong."[^12] Where in your life would it make a difference to believe that God's love reaches past a fall instead of preventing it?

</div>

### Joining Someone in the Dance <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#joining-someone-in-the-dance}

*How we sit with people in a Psalm 13 season matters as much as what we believe about it.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Before visiting a friend who had lost a child, Reed was told by Chris Nation, a pastor of grief and loss: grief is a dance, so let the grieving person lead. Quoting "we are more than conquerors" too early is like stepping on their feet. Josh adds that dancers move in circles but rarely end where they started, and Reed contrasts a dance with a three-legged race aimed at a finish line.[^13] Think of a time someone stepped on your feet in a hard season, or led you well. What did they do?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes argue that laments like Psalm 13 are "as fully Scripture as Romans 8." Frederick Buechner said we must admit God's visible absence before we can speak credibly of God's invisible presence. A faith that speaks only in optimistic terms, "the optimist club," will not be taken seriously by people living through real darkness.[^14] Our traditions differ in how often lament is voiced in worship. In the churches you have known, where has there been room to pray "How long?" out loud, and where has there not? What might change if there were more room?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Josh's helpful responses are concrete: ask what the suffering actually is, offer a parent's presence ("What's wrong, buddy?"), sometimes simply hug or say "You don't deserve what's happening to you," and help the person notice where God may already be present.[^15] Who in your life might be in a Psalm 13 season right now? Which one of these responses could you offer them this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Psalm 13 shows that faith does not mean pretending God always feels close. The psalmist feels mislaid, faces God's hidden face, demands an answer, and then turns to trust in God's steadfast love and a vow to sing. That turn does not erase the anguish; the two sit side by side. We have permission to pray our own "How long?" without dressing it up, and when others are in that place, our task is not a quick fix but a willingness to join the dance and let them lead.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, name a person or a situation you want to bring to God with your own "How long?" or your own *but you have been good to me*.

</div>
