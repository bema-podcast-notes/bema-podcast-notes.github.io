### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** The daughters of Zelophehad asked plainly and publicly for their father's inheritance. God answered "rightly" and rewrote the whole law, and Joshua later kept the promise without delay.[^1]

**This week:** Akhsah (the notes spell her name Aksa), offered in marriage as a prize, rides to her father Caleb (Kalev) and asks him for springs of water. He gives her both the upper and the lower springs.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Akhsah's story appears twice, in Joshua 15 and Judges 1, and Brent and Elle wonder whether recording it twice is itself a "double portion" that signals its importance.[^3] Elle avoids two easy readings, "this is pure oppression" and "#girlboss," and reads Akhsah as a woman with limited but real agency inside a flawed system. She looks at what the names mean, the sharp Hebrew verb for Akhsah's "urging" of her husband, and the contrast with Rebekah (Rivka), who is asked for her consent and blessed before she leaves home.[^4]

Most of the episode is given to Elle's "Biblical Womanhood Corner," six observations drawn from Akhsah's story, and to Caleb as a model of generous, non-defensive leadership. The notes stress that the lessons apply to everyone: men also struggle to name their needs, and leaders of any gender can learn from Caleb.[^5]

### Reading Without the Two Buttons <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#reading-without-the-two-buttons}

*A short, uncomfortable story invites a careful reading that resists easy answers.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Joshua 15:15–19 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes observe that Akhsah's husband Othniel (Otniel) never acts, that she goes to her father herself and speaks in commands, and that the text records no rebuke of her.[^6] What did you notice about who speaks, who acts, and who stays silent in these five verses? Point to the words that show it.

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Elle translates the names: Kalev means "dog," Kiryat Sefer means "city of scrolls," Otniel means roughly "my God is violence," and Aksa means "ankle bangle." Names about ornament first appear with Adah and Zillah, the wives of Lamech (Genesis 4), the first man to use the language of "taking" a wife. Akhsah's name points to a culture that saw women as decoration for someone else's benefit rather than as image bearers.[^7] What changes in the story when the woman named for an ornament turns out to be the one who speaks?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Elle describes two reflexive readings of women's stories in Scripture: the oppression reading ("This is bad, and maybe the Bible is bad") and the #girlboss reading ("Don't worry, she's actually super empowered"). She proposes a third: read with curiosity and accept the "messy middle," where women have limited but real agency inside flawed systems.[^8] She even offers a sympathetic reading of Caleb's contest, yet the notes admit, "The discomfort remains."[^9] Which button do you tend to reach for when a biblical story troubles you? Where in your own life are you living in a "messy middle," with some agency inside a system you did not design?

</div>

### Give Me a Blessing <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#give-me-a-blessing}

*Akhsah's request gains depth when it is set beside Rebekah's story and read in the Hebrew.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 24:57–60 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Rebekah is asked whether she will go, and after she agrees her household blesses her. Akhsah is given away with neither step. Elle hears Akhsah's "Give me a blessing!" as: "We skipped steps. You never asked what I wanted, and you sent me off without a blessing."[^10] Having just read Genesis 24, what do you notice about the two scenes side by side? How does the comparison change the way Akhsah's words sound?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Translations soften the verb for Akhsah's "urging" of Othniel as "persuaded" or "urged," but Elle links it to the Hebrew word for a thorn, suggesting pricking or inciting. Church cultures often hold such strategies against women ("nagging," "complaining"). Elle's point is that these strategies often grow out of systems where women's direct words go unheard, and the text does not condemn Akhsah.[^11] When have you seen someone labeled as nagging or complaining when they were really trying to be heard? What made their direct words hard to hear?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Akhsah doesn't ask for a "spiritually chic" blessing like holiness or patience. She asks for water, which she needs to survive on land in the Negev.[^12] Elle notes that many people are trained out of naming their desires, and she critiques the song lyric "I'm not here for blessings / You don't owe me anything," pointing out that Jacob wrestled all night for a blessing.[^13] Her practical example: tell your spouse "I need two days of rest" instead of silently hoping God provides an "oasis."[^14] What need have you been treating as too ordinary or too unspiritual to name? Who could you name it to this week?

</div>

### Learning From Caleb <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#learning-from-caleb}

*Caleb's quick, generous answer offers a picture of leadership for anyone who holds power.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Judges 1:11–15 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Caleb gives Akhsah both the upper and the lower springs, with no lecture about her tone and no question about where her husband is.[^15] Elle contrasts this with how organizations often stall: "We hear you… there's a board meeting next month… maybe a quarter of a spring… be grateful."[^16] In her closing blessing she asks us to be like Caleb: repent of defensiveness, accept directives from unexpected people, carry the hard conversations ourselves, and give double portions even when others resent it.[^17] Which of these moves seems hardest for people with power, and why?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Elle's second observation is that Akhsah offers her wisdom and vision to her husband instead of following a "say it once, gently, then let it go" model. Elle points out that Proverbs personifies Wisdom as a woman, and Brent admits he would own a $15,000 camera lens without Maggie's "wise opposition."[^18] Different traditions in this room have taught couples different ways of making big decisions together. How was weighing a spouse's counsel modeled or taught in the communities that shaped you, and what does Akhsah's example add to that picture?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes suggest a question for anyone in workplace or church leadership: when someone brings a clear, urgent need, is "process" truly required, or just more comfortable?[^19] Think of a place this week where you are the Caleb, holding something (time, money, permission, attention) that someone else needs. What would a quick, generous, non-defensive yes look like there?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Akhsah is named for a piece of jewelry, offered as a war prize, and married off without being asked or blessed. Still, she does not stay silent. When her husband does nothing, she rides to her father and tells him plainly what she needs: water for her desert land. She does not attack him or flatter him, and Caleb does not get defensive, demand gratitude, or hide behind process; he gives her twice what she asked for. The challenge runs both ways: name our needs honestly when we are the ones asking, and give generously and quickly when we are the ones with power.[^20]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, finish one of these: "This week I want to be more like Akhsah by…" or "This week I want to be more like Caleb by…"

</div>
