### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Marty described the pillar of community as accountability, being only one piece of God's image, and learning from what others see, and he warned with Bonhoeffer against loving our dream of community more than the real people in front of us.[^1]

**This week:** Reed Dent gives his own view of the same pillar. Following Jesus gets harder, not easier, the further you go, so nobody should try it alone.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Reed has worked at Campus Christian Fellowship (CCF) for about 18 years and now directs it. In this short episode he compares healthy Christian community to Japanese woodworking joints, where hand-cut pieces lock together into one strong whole without glue or screws. His model has three interlocking parts, summed up as "Sharing life together and exploring questions of faith while taking faithfulness seriously": friendship, conversation and practice.[^3]

He closes with the Lord's Table, which he says "re-members" the community into one body, with thoughts on envy and each person's place in the body of Christ, and with a warning that a community must keep the ability to criticize itself. One story in this episode involves carrying other people's sorrow. As always, share only what you want to share; listening is a full way to take part.[^4]

### Joinery, Not Glue <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#joinery-not-glue}

*Reed's central image contrasts what holds a group together from the outside with what holds it together from within.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Reed describes two ways to join wood. In "slapdash" joinery, you push two blocks together and drive a screw through or clamp them with glue: something outside the people holds them together, like a program, an obligation or an event. In Japanese joinery, hand-cut pieces lock together like a 3D puzzle, each piece stays itself while becoming part of the whole, and if you take one piece out the joint fails.[^5] Thinking about groups you have belonged to, what tended to hold them together: outside fasteners, or the people themselves? What difference did you notice?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Reed admits, "If it's just me, I tend to go awry pretty easily," and Marty adds that rebuilding a whole value system and ethic can't be done inside your own head.[^6] Reed also calls community a craft and a learned skill: you will fumble and have to try again, and leaving after one week, or after the first offense, cuts that learning short.[^7] What makes staying through the awkward parts of community hard for you, and what has helped when you did stay?

</div>

### Friendship, Conversation, Practice <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#friendship-conversation-practice}

*Reed's three elements depend on each other like the pieces of a joint.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Reed builds on a paraphrase of John Paul II: "The appropriate context for theological inquiry is love and mutual friendship." Drawing on Frederick Buechner's idea that "all doctrine begins as an experience," he argues that knowing someone's life is the best way to understand their faith, so games, movies and pickup basketball are "not superfluous."[^8] Marty points to social media comment threads as the opposite: debate that leaves everyone unsatisfied.[^9] How have you seen friendship change the way a theological conversation went, for better or worse?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Reed sets two guardrails for conversation: no rejection, condemnation or fracturing when people disagree, and no echo chamber. He tells of a student who asked him late one night, with a tremble in his voice, whether he could still be a Christian if he didn't believe in six literal days of creation, because he had never felt safe asking at home.[^10] The point of the story is not the answer to the creation question, and people in this room may hold different views on it. The point is whether the question could be asked at all. Reed also adds: "Sometimes the truth is hard because it's not what you've been taught, but sometimes it's hard because it *is* what you've been taught."[^11] What does it take for a group to be "rigorous without being rigid," safe enough for that student's question while still free to push back?

*Read Luke 6:46 and 1 John 2:6 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Reed warns that people can get so caught up in wondering that they let go of what they are actually convinced of. He contrasts a "country club" church, where people "pat each other on the back," with Kierkegaard's point that Jesus asks for disciples, not admirers. You can ask every question you have about the Trinity, Scripture, predestination or the Fall, he says, and still you must walk as Jesus walked.[^12] What do you notice these two verses asking for? How do honest questions and real obedience fit together?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Which of Reed's three elements (friendship, conversation, practice) is strongest in the groups you belong to, including this one, and which is thinnest? What is one small step toward strengthening the thinnest?

</div>

### Re-Membering and the Real You <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#re-membering-and-the-real-you}

*Practice includes remembering God together, and community needs each person to show up as themselves.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** At CCF, Reed sees the Sunday sermon as "a set-up" for the Eucharist (the Lord's Supper or communion, depending on your tradition). He plays on the word "re-member": to put the body back together, so the community gathers, is fed, and goes back out as one body. The notes point out that the word historically comes from the Latin for "to call to mind," but the wordplay still teaches something real. Brent calls the table "the equalizer": even if the sermon didn't land, you can still come to the table with your family.[^13] Reed's wife, a VA counselor who works with veterans carrying combat trauma, says: "It's all about Eucharist for me... I take all of that sorrow and I soak it into the bread and I wring it out. And then I can be fed."[^14] Our traditions observe the Lord's Supper in different ways and at different rhythms. What does the picture of the table as a place to bring sorrow, and to share it as a community, add to how you understand it? Share only as much as you are comfortable sharing.

*Read 1 Samuel 18:6–16 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Reed recently preached on Saul's envy of David. He observes that envy usually aims at the people closest to us: he doesn't envy celebrities, but his colleague Derek, whose goodness he sees up close. His conclusion: "The body of Christ doesn't need a bunch of fake Dereks. It just needs a real you."[^15] What do you notice in the passage about what sets off Saul's envy and how it grows? Where do you feel the pull to be a copy of someone close to you, and what might offering "a real you" look like? Answer the second part only if you choose to.

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Reed quotes Richard Rohr: "Any people group that loses the capacity for self-criticism will become idolatrous." Communities die, he says, when as a group they stop asking, "Are we doing what is right? Are we following in the way?" Brent notes that the line that makes you say "oof" is usually the true one.[^16] What would help a group like ours keep asking that question regularly without turning it into condemnation of one another?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Reed takes the pillar of community down to everyday life. Following Jesus is costly and complicated, and nobody is built to do it alone; faith reduced to private belief turns flimsy, while faith lived among others becomes strong. A good community isn't held together by outside fasteners. Each person, shaped by their own story, locks into the others through friendship that makes room for honest questions, conversation that is curious but rigorous, and practice that holds us to the way of Jesus while we remember God together at the table. Stay through the awkward parts, be the real you, and keep asking whether you are really walking as Jesus walked.[^17]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, name one ordinary thing you will do with someone this week to help "lock" your community together.

</div>

She builds on the Hebrew root *'ud* ("again"), which sits behind the biblical language of witness, to describe God's people as witnesses who tell the stories of God's work over and over, like a bride sharing "letters from the groom" while she waits for his return. She also names three barriers that keep people out of community: hurt done by the church, intellectual superiority, and lone-wolf self-sufficiency.[^18]
