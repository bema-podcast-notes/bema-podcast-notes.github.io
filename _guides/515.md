### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Rahab, a Canaanite outsider, told Israel's own rescue story back to the spies and asked not only to escape but to be included in God's people. Her faith had four parts: willingness to believe, intellectual assent, trust in God's hospitality, and the courage to join a new people.[^1]

**This week:** Five sisters whose father died without sons bring their case before Moses and the whole assembly. God answers "rightly," rewrites the inheritance law for everyone, and years later Joshua keeps the promise without delay.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This episode continues Elle Grover Fricks's series on women whose short stories are scattered through Joshua and Judges. The daughters of Zelophehad (Mahlah, Noa, Hoglah, Milcah and Tirzah) are named twice, once in Numbers and again in Joshua, which the notes call an honor from both God and the narrator in a Bible where many women go unnamed.[^3] They come prepared, they ask plainly, and God's answer opens with the verdict before going further than they asked.[^4]

The hosts also look at what the family's names mean, a rabbinic story about how the case climbed the chain of leadership, and the sisters' follow-up with Joshua. Elle closes with her "biblical womanhood corner," a list of what these women actually do in the story. She is clear that she isn't shaming any community or leader; she wants to separate what is biblical from what is cultural.[^5] Come ready to notice, compare and think out loud.

### A Case Before the Whole Assembly <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#a-case-before-the-whole-assembly}

*The sisters bring a public case, and God answers it quickly and thoroughly.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Numbers 27:1–11 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes point out that the sisters speak in front of Moses, Eleazar the priest, the leaders, and the whole assembly, and that God's answer begins with the verdict: "The daughters of Zelophehad speak rightly."[^6] As you listened, what did you notice about how the sisters made their case and how God responded? Name one specific detail (who was present, what they said, or what changed).

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Elle translates each name in the family line. Read in order, they move from Hepher ("dug / humiliated") and Zelophehad ("first rupture" or "shadow of fear") through the daughters: Mahlah ("diseased"), Noa ("wanderer"), Hoglah ("partridge"), Milcah ("queen") and Tirzah ("she will delight"). Brent pointed out that things seem to "get better" with each daughter.[^7] What does it add to the story to hear these names as a movement from trauma toward delight?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The sisters expected someone to say their father died under God's judgment with Korah (Numbers 16), and they answered it before anyone raised it. They also admitted he "died in his own mistakes" without letting that erase his legacy.[^8] God then went further than their request and changed the whole order of inheritance. As Brent put it, "We were just asking for this one contingency and you give us like ten contingencies."[^9] What does this kind of answer (quick, thorough, a change to the system rather than a one-time exception) suggest about God's character? Where in your family, workplace or church have you seen a problem handled as an exception when it really needed the fuller fix?

</div>

### Up the Chain and Back Again <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#up-the-chain-and-back-again}

*A rabbinic story imagines the case climbing to God, and Joshua 17 shows the sisters following up.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Joshua 17:3–4 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Years later, Moses has died and Israel is in the land. The sisters go to Eleazar, Joshua and the leaders and remind them what God commanded. Joshua gives them the land immediately, "according to the mouth of the LORD." Brent noted this is "not like a modern class action lawsuit": there is no committee and no "these things take time."[^10] Compare this scene with Numbers 27. What has changed, and what has stayed the same?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty shared a midrash in which Rabbi Simeon ben Lakish imagines the women going first to the chiefs of tens, who said, "This is above our jurisdiction," and then up through every level until Eleazar sent them to Moses. Moses said, "I too have a superior," and took the case to God. Marty reads it as humility running from bottom to top, which leaves room for God to affirm what Torah is designed to do. Elle reads it as a story of the women's persistence, like the persistent widow.[^11] (Eleazar's name, Elle notes, means "God is our ally," from *ezer*, the strong, rescuing help of an ally rather than a "sweet little helper."[^12]) Which reading speaks to you more, and what does each one ask of the people in the story?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Elle gives three takeaways: be like the daughters (bold, persistent, unafraid of how you're perceived), be like Moses (willing to look "wrong" in order to listen to outsiders and do what's right), and be like Joshua (quick to follow through).[^13] She compared Joshua 17 to being promised something by a boss who then leaves, and having to raise it with the new person.[^14] Think of a role you hold right now in a family, job, church or friendship. Which of the three is hardest for you to live out in that role, and why?

</div>

### The Biblical Womanhood Corner <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#the-biblical-womanhood-corner}

*Elle lists what the sisters actually do, and the group asks what is biblical and what is cultural.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Here are some of the traits Elle draws from this story: communal, bold and persistent, focused on policy change rather than personal attacks, willing to address injustice publicly, unafraid to name grief and loss, strategic, family-minded, not told to "go get married," asking directly, and following up.[^15] The notes add that many of these traits are not specifically "feminine"; they are what a faithful follower of God looks like, and here they show up in women.[^16] Which of these traits fit easily with what you were taught growing up about faithful women, and which would take more thought or conversation to fit? What might explain the difference?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty asked how the sisters' public boldness fits with Tamar (Genesis 38), who confronts Judah privately. Elle's answer: deal with private sins privately and public problems publicly. Judah's sin was personal, but the inheritance rule affected every woman in Israel. Brent added that going public carried real risk of embarrassment if the ruling went against them.[^17] How do you decide whether a concern belongs in a private conversation or a public one?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The sisters didn't say, "What do you think would be right, Moses?" They said, "Give us a holding."[^18] Elle admitted she often asks for things indirectly ("Have you thought about…?") to avoid a negative reaction.[^19] The sisters also spoke about their father's death without worrying about being called too emotional.[^20] Think of something you need (or a loss you carry) that you have been hinting at rather than naming. What would it sound like to say it plainly, and to whom? Share only as much as you want to.

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Five sisters whose names move from "diseased" to "she will delight" stood before the whole community and asked plainly for what was right. They prepared, they owned their father's flaws, they attacked no one, and they did not soften their request to manage anyone's feelings. Moses was humble enough to take the case higher, God answered "rightly" at once and rewrote the whole system, and Joshua kept the promise without hesitation. Justice in this story is neither slow nor grudging, and it is not a one-time exception.[^21]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, which do you want to practice this week: the boldness of the daughters, the humility of Moses, or the follow-through of Joshua?

</div>

Like the daughters of Zelophehad, she asks for land and what it needs, with some notable differences. Elle reads her as a woman with real but limited agency inside a flawed system.[^22]
