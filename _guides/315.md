### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Jesus refused to be a "divider" between two brothers, and God's question to the barn builder, "Whose will it be?", turned the focus from possessions to people.[^1]

**This week:** Reed leads the discussion of Luke 15, where Jesus answers religious leaders who are "grumbling" because he welcomes tax collectors and sinners and eats with them.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The hosts begin by giving the Pharisees a fuller, more human picture, then read the story the way a Middle Eastern villager would have heard it.[^2] The son's request for his inheritance is a shocking insult. His rehearsed speech echoes Pharaoh's empty "repentance." The father runs out to protect his son from the village before the son has said a word.[^2]

The story then breaks its own pattern. The older brother stands outside the party, the father goes out to plead with him, and the story never says whether he goes in. The main point, as the notes put it, is that God takes the first step: his kindness leads people to repentance, and the people who see themselves as righteous may be the last lost thing the Father goes out to find.[^2]

### Opening <span class="guide-time">10 min</span> {#opening}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-opening" markdown="1">

**Opening Question:** Brent mentions that his grandmother happily gave him a set of drawers he admired before she died, the kind of warm gift that makes it hard for us to hear how offensive the son's request in Luke 15 would have sounded.[^3] What is something that was handed down to you, or that you hope to hand down, and what makes it meaningful?

### Grumblers and Lost Things <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#grumblers-and-lost-things}

*Jesus tells "this parable" to grumbling religious listeners, and the shape of its three stories matters.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Luke 15:1–10 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes lay out the pattern of the chapter: one sheep out of 100, one coin out of 10, and then one son out of 2, with the first two stories ending in "Rejoice with me!"[^4] What do you notice about who is listening in verses 1–2, and about how the first two stories end? Keep that pattern in mind for later.

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The hosts insist the Pharisees were not all the same. There were Shammai and Hillel Pharisees, and there was Nicodemus; Eugene Peterson even wonders whether some Pharisees were real followers of Jesus.[^5] Reed argues that American evangelicals are probably most like the Pharisees.[^5] Why might it matter to read the Pharisees as real, varied people rather than stock villains?

*Read Exodus 16:2–3 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Luke's word for "grumbling" (*diagongyzō*) matches the Greek translation of Exodus 16, where Israel grumbles in the wilderness out of fear, distrust, and a longing to go back to what they knew.[^6] The notes suggest the Pharisees are on unfamiliar ground and want security: their sincere wish to stay set apart makes them ask whether eating with sinners can be right.[^6] Reed describes the same unease when worshipping alongside people whose morals, doctrine, or politics seem to threaten what we feel called to protect.[^7] When have you felt that kind of unease about whom God seemed to be welcoming? Share only what you are comfortable sharing.

</div>

### The Son Who Left and the Father Who Ran <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-son-who-left-and-the-father-who-ran}

*Read through village eyes, the son's request, his speech, and his father's running all carry more weight than modern readers expect.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Luke 15:11–24 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Kenneth Bailey spent 15 years asking people across the Middle East about this request. "Has anyone ever made a request like this?" "Never." "What would happen?" "His father would beat him, of course." "Why?" "This request means he wants his father to die."[^8] Marty adds that while the father is alive, the property *is* the father, and the word for what he divides is *bios*, his very "living."[^8] Knowing how villagers heard the request, what do you notice about the father's response in verse 12? How does it compare with what you would expect?

*Read Exodus 10:16–17 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The son's rehearsed words, "I have sinned against heaven and against you," echo Pharaoh's after the plague of locusts, and Pharaoh was using confession to get relief.[^9] "Hired servants" were contract workers who lived independently in the village, so the son's plan would let him earn the money back while staying estranged as a son.[^9] Compare the two speeches. What does the echo suggest about where the son's heart is as he walks home?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The father is "filled with compassion" (*splanchnizomai*, moved deep in the gut), and he runs, something men in that culture did not do.[^10] The notes suggest he runs to reach his son before the village does, since Bailey describes a *kezazah* ceremony in which a village cut off someone who had lost the family's wealth among Gentiles.[^10] He embraces his son before any confession, and the notes conclude that repentance does not have to come before forgiveness: "It's the kindness of God that leads us to repentance" (Romans 2:4).[^11] Our traditions have taught the order of repentance and forgiveness in different ways. What does it change for you that the father acts first? Where have you received, or held back, grace that came before any apology?

</div>

### The Son Outside the Party <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#the-son-outside-the-party}

*The third story breaks the pattern, and the last lost son is the one who never left home.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Luke 15:25–32 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The older son refuses to go in, and the father goes out again to plead with him.[^12] The older son says "this son of yours," refusing to call him a brother; the father answers gently, "this brother of yours."[^12] What do you notice in the older son's words and in the father's reply? What is the father inviting him to see?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Tim Keller puts it this way: the younger son is cut off from the father by rebellion, the older son by his own righteousness.[^12] The pattern narrows to one son out of one, and no celebration is mentioned; Brent notes that in the Good Samaritan the Pharisees expected to appear and didn't, while here they discover they were in the story all along.[^12] Why do you think Jesus leaves the ending unwritten?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** Marty distinguishes the "self-righteously religious" from the "religiously self-righteous," people who sincerely want to follow Jesus but whose religious habits keep getting in the way, and he says he relates to Nicodemus for this reason.[^13] The notes add that when we call someone "that person" or "their kind," the father's reply reminds us that if God has received them, they are our brother or sister.[^14] Where might your own faith habits keep you standing outside the party? What would it take to walk in?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The parable we call the prodigal son is really about a father who will not stop searching. The younger son treats his father as dead and comes home with a speech borrowed from Pharaoh, yet the father runs, risks his dignity, and shields his son with an embrace before a word of repentance. Grace comes first, and change follows. Then the father goes out again, this time to the faithful, resentful son outside the walls. Nobody keeps the older brother out of the party except himself, and Jesus leaves the ending for each of us to write.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: if you finished the story, what would the older brother do next?

</div>
