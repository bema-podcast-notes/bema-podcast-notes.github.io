### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Copernicus moved the earth out of the center, the church adjusted "not gracefully," and Marty argued that since this era the church has been "trailing behind trying to catch up."[^1]

**This week:** Enlightenment thinkers reshape science and politics, Protestant freedom brings both creativity and splintering, John Wesley launches a movement of practical holiness, and the French Revolution opens the "post-Christian era."[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This short episode covers 150 years. Isaac Newton, the modern father of mechanics, and John Locke, the father of liberalism (in the sense of a philosophy of liberty and equality, not the modern partisan label), continue the secular growth curve that began with Copernicus. The Edict of Nantes (1598) had granted Protestants civil liberty and equality, which freed people to study, publish and teach, but also cost Protestantism its unifying "catholic element," in the lowercase sense of universal, so that Christian thought split again and again.[^3]

Against this background John Wesley argued vigorously against Calvinist premises and preached personal repentance, transformation and the pursuit of holiness; the movement became the Methodist Church. Voltaire's satire attacked established religion and championed the separation of church and state. With the French Revolution, Marty says, Western culture stopped defaulting to Christianity, and many parts of the church remain in denial about the loss of influence that followed. Some of us come from the Methodist tradition and others from traditions that read predestination differently, so this guide asks us to describe and learn rather than to debate.[^4]

### Freedom and Fragmentation <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#freedom-and-fragmentation}

*Protestant freedom unleashed new thinking and also broke apart the church's common structure.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes describe two results of the Edict of Nantes. Positively, people could think for themselves, study independently, publish their findings and teach their own beliefs and systems, and the Reformation actively encouraged individual interpretation. Negatively, Protestantism lost its central unifying system, its "catholic element," and as Europe fragmented and migration increased, Christian thought experienced exponential division over the next two hundred years. The notes add that theological diversity is not inherently negative.[^5] Looking at your own experience, what has the freedom to read and interpret for yourself given you? What has the wider church lost by having so many separate streams?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Locke's political philosophy, the notes say, created a tension in American democracy between liberalism (bent toward complete freedom) and conservatism (bent toward maintaining constant ethics), a balance that lets democracy work when it functions properly. Newton's laws, meanwhile, moved Western thought toward "modernity."[^6] Where do you see that same tension between freedom and fixed ethics play out inside churches, and what does a healthy balance look like?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Our group gathers people from Church of Christ, Baptist, Christian Church and Methodist backgrounds, which is itself a picture of those two centuries of splitting. Thinking of the "catholic element," meaning what is universal and shared, what do you think Christians across traditions still hold in common? What would it take to recover something of that unity without pretending our differences are not real?

</div>

### Wesley's Method <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#wesleys-method}

*Wesley's legacy is less a system of ideas than a way of forming people.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Read this list aloud, slowly. According to the notes, Wesley preached passionately about:[^7]

- personal repentance and confession;
- the power of personal will to pursue transformation;
- active pursuit of God's holiness;
- practical applications of theology.

His "method," from which Methodism takes its name, included small-group accountability, personal spiritual disciplines and service to the poor.[^8] What do you notice about the words in the list and in his method? How much of it is about believing the right things, and how much is about practicing a way of life?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe Wesley's theology as a vigorous argument against Calvinist premises, particularly predestination and human agency. They also say his commitment to discipleship, community and "pragmatic orthopraxy" (right practice) offers insights often overlooked in contemporary evaluations, and they point to Robby Gallaty's *Rediscovering Discipleship* as a study of his methods.[^9] Our traditions have answered the questions of predestination and free will differently. Rather than settle that debate, describe how your tradition's answer has shaped the way it forms and disciples people.

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Wesley's movement grew through small groups that held one another accountable, practiced spiritual disciplines together and served the poor.[^10] Of those three practices, which is strongest in your life right now and which is weakest? What is one concrete way this group could borrow from Wesley's method in the weeks ahead?

</div>

### When Culture Stops Blinking Christian <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#when-culture-stops-blinking-christian}

*The French Revolution marks the start of a world where Christianity is no longer the cultural default.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Voltaire used wit and satire rather than theological argument to attack established religion. He advocated freedom of religion, freedom of speech and the separation of church and state, and the notes say he accurately identified the dangers of religion mixing with state power, the negative side of "Christendom" that this session has traced since Constantine.[^11] Why can an outside critic sometimes see a church's problems more clearly than insiders can? What would it look like to listen to critics as diagnosticians rather than enemies?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Marty explains the "post-Christian era" with an image: before the French Revolution, if you unplugged Western culture and plugged it back in, it would "blink Christian" as its default setting. Afterward, it no longer did, though the shift reached America more slowly. The result was declining political and cultural influence, a reality the notes say many parts of the church, particularly fundamentalist evangelical movements, remain in denial about.[^12] Where do you see evidence that our culture no longer blinks Christian, and how do you notice yourself reacting? If the church no longer holds the default, what does faithful witness look like on your street this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Between 1650 and 1800 the world that Christendom built began to come apart. Newton and Locke reshaped science and politics, and Protestant freedom let believers think and publish for themselves while the church split into ever more streams. In the middle of it, John Wesley called people to repentance, holiness and a practical method of discipleship lived out in small groups and service to the poor. Voltaire named the dangers of religion fused with state power, and the French Revolution marked the moment when Western culture stopped blinking Christian. Marty's challenge is to stop denying that loss and to learn how to follow Jesus faithfully in a culture that no longer assumes we are right.[^13]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, name one practice from Wesley's method you want to try this week.

</div>
