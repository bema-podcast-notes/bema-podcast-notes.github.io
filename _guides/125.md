### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Jesus entered Jerusalem on a donkey from the east while Pilate rode in from the west, then cleared the Court of the Gentiles and cursed a fig tree that stood for corrupt leadership, leaving a riderless donkey as an invitation to choose a kingdom.

**This week:** In the temple courts, Jesus tells three parables directed at the chief priests. Each one exposes leaders who claim God's work but will not do it, and each asks whether we might be missing what God is doing.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode begins with a distinction it calls critical. For three years Jesus worked within the world of the Pharisees as an internal reformer, patiently and repeatedly. In Jerusalem he spends one week confronting the Sadducees, the priestly aristocracy controlling the temple, and that week leads to his death. The notes insist that the Sadducees represent "not Judaism itself" but a specific corrupt power structure, and that seeing this "prevents the tragic misreading that 'the Jews killed Jesus.'"

The three parables follow. In the two sons, the son who says no but obeys is preferred to the one who says yes and does nothing. In the tenants, Isaiah's vineyard is now producing fruit, but the tenants hoard it. In the wedding banquet, the invited guests refuse to come, the hall fills with unlikely people, and a guest in the wrong clothes points to a prophecy in Zephaniah. Brent and Marty close with stories about parties that did not go as planned and a question about whether we would recognize God's party if we saw it.

<div class="guide-q guide-q-opening" markdown="1">

**Opening Question:** Think of a time you threw a party, planned a meal, or organized an event that did not go the way you expected. Who ended up coming, and what did you learn?

### One Week and Two Sons <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#one-week-and-two-sons}

*Jesus' tone changes when he turns from patient reform to confronting corrupt power.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes describe two very different approaches. With the Pharisees, Jesus worked "as a kind of internal reformer," never giving up on that community. With the Sadducees, he spent one week in "direct confrontation of a corrupt power structure," a group the notes call a "religious mafia" of seven families who monopolized the high priesthood.[^1] The notes also say plainly that "The Pharisees did not kill Jesus."[^2] How does this distinction change the way you have heard the story of Jesus' death told? Why might it matter for how Christians speak about Jewish people?

*Read Matthew 21:28–32 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The priests answer Jesus' question correctly without realizing he is describing them as the second son. The episode suggests a historical hint at Zadok and Abiathar, the two priestly lines in David and Solomon's time; the Sadducees claimed descent from Zadok. "God would rather have the 'wrong' son who actually does the work than the 'right' son who merely claims the position."[^3] What do you notice about the two sons' words compared with their actions? Why do you think Jesus brings in tax collectors and prostitutes at the end?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes summarize the parable this way: it "challenges whether we claim God's work but don't do it."[^4] Where are you most tempted to say yes to God and then not follow through?

</div>

### The Tenants and the Fruit <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#the-tenants-and-the-fruit}

*Isaiah's vineyard returns, with one devastating change.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 5:1–7 aloud, then read Matthew 21:33–46 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes say the priests would have recognized Isaiah's watchtower, winepress, and wall, but at first assumed the tenants were the Romans. Then comes the twist: "the priests themselves are the corrupt tenants." Unlike Isaiah 5, where God found no good fruit, "in this parable the vineyard IS producing fruit," and the tenants are hoarding it.[^5] Compare the two vineyards. What is different about the problem in each story? What does it say that the people were bearing fruit even while their leaders were failing them?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The episode suggests Jesus may also be referencing Isaiah 61, where foreigners tend Israel's fields while Israel serves as priests to the nations. The notes say the priests had the mission "completely backwards": "They see foreigners (Romans) as the problem when actually foreigners are supposed to be part of God's solution."[^6] Read Isaiah 61:5–6. How does this vision of Israel as a priestly people change the way you hear the tenants' sin?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes apply this to religious communities in any era: "Are we hoarding God's blessings for ourselves or our in-group?"[^7] Where might your church, your family, or you personally be holding onto something that was meant to be shared?

</div>

### The Banquet and the Strange Clothes <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#the-banquet-and-the-strange-clothes}

*God is already throwing a party, and the invited guests are missing it.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 22:1–10 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes read the burning of the city as a prophecy of Rome's destruction of Jerusalem in 70 CE, and the filled banquet hall as the *mamzers* (outsiders) Matthew keeps highlighting.[^8] Brent shared that when their Easter dinner guests canceled, he and his wife invited students from a university fraternity; only one came, but they had a meaningful dinner. Marty told students asking how to make an impact on campus that "God's already doing stuff on your campus." The notes add: "The Kingdom isn't something we create; it's something we join."[^9] What do you notice about how the king responds when the first guests refuse? Where might God already be throwing a party near you?

*Read Matthew 22:11–14 aloud, then read Zephaniah 1:7–9 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The guest without wedding clothes is a "weird" detail that the episode reads as a *remez* (hint) to Zephaniah 1, which speaks against those "clad in foreign clothes" or "strange clothes," people who want to be part of God's work but on their own terms.[^10] Marty mentions a tradition, which he says he has not fully verified, that a king provided clothing for his guests.[^11] What do you notice about Zephaniah's language next to the parable? What might "strange clothes" look like in a religious community today?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** Marty asks: "Am I insistent that God throw the kind of party I would throw? Am I blinded by the fact that God's definition of a party isn't my definition? Am I too distracted by my own agenda to even see the party God's throwing?"[^12] Choose one of these three questions. How does it apply to your life or to a community you belong to?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> After three years of patient work among the Pharisees, Jesus spends one week confronting the priestly leadership of the temple, knowing it will cost him his life. His three parables expose leaders who say yes and do nothing, who hoard fruit the people are bearing, and who refuse an invitation to the king's own celebration. Meanwhile the hall fills with unlikely guests. The episode warns that this is not a story about "the Jews," but about religious power in any tradition, including ours. And it asks whether we are missing the party God is already throwing.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: where will you watch for God's party this week?

</div>
