### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We met Jacob as part of a family drama rather than a simple morality tale. At Bethel, God promised "I am with you" and "I will not leave you" without ever saying Jacob had done the right thing.[^1]

**This week:** Jacob arrives at Laban's well with empty hands, lives through years of consequences, and is finally embraced by the brother he deceived. The episode asks why God delays justice, and what kind of justice is worth waiting for.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode opens with a question: why does God delay justice when Jacob is clearly in the wrong? Marty and Brent frame it with two Hebrew words, *diyn* ("judgment or punishment") and *mishpat* ("restorative justice, putting things back in their proper place").[^3] They follow Jacob from his tearful arrival at the well, through Rashi's two readings of those tears, to the reunion of Genesis 33, which they present as the foundation for Jesus's Parable of the Prodigal Son.[^4]

The episode closes with Rachel's death on the road to Bethlehem and Jacob's late-life words to Joseph, "to my sorrow," which can be read as "because of me."[^5] Through Jacob's final chapters the text keeps switching between the names Jacob and Israel, and the hosts read that as a lifelong wrestling between who we have been and who God is calling us to become.[^6]

### Empty Hands at the Well <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#empty-hands-at-the-well}

*Jacob's long-awaited arrival at Laban's household turns out to be a moment of loss as much as joy.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 29:1–14 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Listen for what Jacob does when he meets Rachel. Rashi offers two readings of verse 11, "Then Jacob kissed Rachel and began to weep aloud": a "Prophetic Premonition" that Rachel would not be buried with him, and "Empty Hands," because he arrived with nothing to offer her, "unlike Eliezer who came to court Rebekah with bracelets, rings, and jewels."[^4] What details in this passage support either reading? Which one feels more true to the scene to you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes record a midrashic tale explaining Jacob's poverty: Esau's son Eliphaz pursued Jacob to kill him but spared him and took all his possessions instead, and Jacob reasoned, "A poor person is as good as death."[^7] The rabbis also notice that the phrase "weep aloud" is used both for Esau when he loses the blessing and for Jacob at the well.[^8] What does it add to see both brothers crying the same cry of loss?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes say that God's presence and promise "don't eliminate the natural consequences of our choices," and they list what Jacob lived through: years of deception under Laban, favoritism among his own children, the loss of Rachel and constant instability.[^9] How do you hold together *God is with me* and *I am still living with the results of what I chose*? Share an example only if you would like to.

</div>

### Diyn, Mishpat and a Running Brother <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#diyn-mishpat-and-a-running-brother}

*The episode argues that the deepest justice in Jacob's story is not punishment but a forgiveness that takes years to grow.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The hosts suggest that both Esau and Jacob have some claim to the firstborn blessing, so simple judgment "would not truly restore what was broken," and that God's delay is "not passive approval but strategic patience aimed at deeper restoration."[^3] In your own words, what is the difference between *diyn* and *mishpat*? Which one do you instinctively want when you have been wronged?

*Read Genesis 33:1–11 aloud, then Luke 15:20.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Compare the two scenes. In Genesis, Esau "ran to meet Jacob and embraced him"; in Luke, the father runs to the returning son.[^4] What words, actions or postures do the two passages share? What does Jesus change when the forgiving brother becomes the forgiving father?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> In the hosts' reading, the elder brother of the parable stands for listeners who should have been like Esau, "running to reconcile with their wayward brothers," but instead "stood with arms crossed in judgment."[^4] This is a call aimed inside the family of faith, not at outsiders. Without naming anyone else's church, where do you see the "arms crossed" posture show up in religious communities like ours, and what makes it so easy to fall into?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes say true *mishpat* came through "the patient work that allows Esau to grow into someone capable of forgiveness."[^4] The hosts are careful to say this does not apply universally, "especially in cases of abuse or severe trauma."[^10] With that limit in mind, where in your relationships (family, friendships, church) might the slower path toward reconciliation be worth considering? You can answer in general terms.

</div>

### Jacob and Israel to the End <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#jacob-and-israel-to-the-end}

*Near the end of his life, Jacob looks back on the road he chose and names its cost.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 35:16–20 and Genesis 48:1–7 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** As you listen, notice when the text uses the name "Jacob" and when it uses "Israel." The notes point out that "Israel rallied his strength and sat up on the bed" while "Jacob said to Joseph," and they describe this duality as "the lifelong struggle between who we have been (Jacob the deceiver) and who God is calling us to become (Israel, one who wrestles with God)."[^6] What did you notice, and what do you make of the fact that the old names keep returning even at the end?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The phrase "to my sorrow" in Genesis 48:7 "can be more literally translated as 'because of me' or 'it's my fault.'"[^5] The notes give two readings: Jacob blaming his rash oath about Laban's stolen gods, or Jacob recognizing that much of his family's suffering came from "the hard road of deception he chose."[^5] Which reading seems more likely to you, and what would it cost a person to say the second one out loud?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** If your own life had two names, one for who you have been and one for who God is calling you to become, what would they be? What does it look like this week to answer to the second name, even while the first one still shows up?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Jacob reached the well with empty hands, and the consequences of his deception followed him for years. God did not remove those consequences, and God did not leave. The justice the story finally offers is not Jacob's punishment but Esau's embrace, a forgiveness that took a long road to grow. Jesus takes that embrace and puts it in the father's arms. And Jacob, at the end, can name the cost of his choices while the text calls him both Jacob and Israel.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, where do you most need patience right now: patience to forgive, or patience while you are being made into someone new?

</div>
