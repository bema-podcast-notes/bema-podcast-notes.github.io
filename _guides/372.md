### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Isaiah 34–35 showed that God's anger comes from injustice and aims at restoration, and Marty summed up Isaiah 1–39 as "a father disciplining his sons."[^1]

**This week:** At the narrative bridge of Isaiah 36–39, Marty and Brent talk with Elle Grover Fricks about how biblical texts came together and how to read Isaiah as a whole.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This episode is less an exposition of Isaiah 36–39 than a friendly argument about how to read texts like it. Elle, who studied with Baruch Schwartz at Hebrew University of Jerusalem, explains the Documentary Hypothesis and its newer form, what they claim, and what they have been useful for. She then gives six objections to how the theory is used in practice. Marty is more open to the idea of multiple sources and voices. Both agree that thinking critically about where texts came from is worthwhile, and that the theory becomes harmful when it is used to explain away the literary artistry of the text.[^3]

The group tonight likely holds a range of views on these questions, and that is welcome. The aim is not to settle who wrote what, but to notice what each approach helps us see, what it can hide, and why it matters for reading. The conversation closes with two pictures of how Isaiah holds together: Elle's movement from diagnosis to healing, and Marty's four sections or voices.[^4]

### Words That Sound Threatening <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#words-that-sound-threatening}

*Some scholarly words mean something different from what they seem to say.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes explain that "redaction" in biblical studies means putting texts together, "the opposite of the FBI picture of blacking out lines with a Sharpie." "Source criticism" means thinking critically *about* sources, not criticizing Scripture. Marty recalls that in his Bible college training, "criticism" was treated as a warning sign for people who "don't believe in the inspiration of the Bible."[^5] What words or approaches were you taught to be wary of when studying the Bible? What was that caution trying to protect?

*Read Deuteronomy 34:5–12 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes observe that "even a plain reading shows Moses could not have written the account of his own death at the end of Deuteronomy. The early rabbis noticed this." Elle jokes that this is "where we got the idea of ghost writing."[^6] What do you notice in these verses that suggests a later voice? What does it mean that even very traditional readers already accept some editorial work?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Elle compares two ways of reading the US Constitution: in layers ("that line came from a Jeffersonian idea of freedom, and it has been changed since") or as "one cohesive, finished document." The notes say the same tension shows up in how people read the Bible.[^7] Which way do you instinctively read Scripture? What does that instinct give you, and what might it make harder to see?

</div>

### Art, Science, and Six Objections <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#art-science-and-six-objections}

*Both hosts and their guest agree that the question is not whether to think critically, but how.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes summarize the Documentary Hypothesis as proposing four sources behind the Torah (J, E, D, and P), and the Neo-Documentary Hypothesis as proposing one compiler who combined them. Elle credits the approach with real benefits: it shows biblical texts "in conversation with each other," it breaks down a rigid "descended from on high" picture of how Scripture was written, and it reminds us that whether a prophet wrote before or after the exile changes whether the words are a warning or a reflection.[^8] Which of these benefits seems most useful to you for ordinary Bible reading, and why?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Elle quotes Meir Sternberg: "Rarely have such grandiose theories of origination been built and revised and pitted against one another on the evidential equivalent of the head of a pin, and rarely have so many worked so long and so hard with so little to show for their trouble."[^9] What do you think makes a question about the Bible worth the effort of studying it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Elle's six objections are that the theory becomes art rather than science, that no J, E, D, or P manuscript has ever been found, that academia rewards endless re-dividing, that the work has little practical payoff, that it becomes a quick fix for any difficulty, and, most seriously, that it hides ancient literary forms behind modern expectations, as when two creation accounts are assumed to mean two authors. Marty agrees the dangers are real but notes that "subjectivity cuts both ways," and that everyone has different thresholds for when evidence "tips" the burden of proof. Elle adds that she does not think Scripture appeared all at once, pointing to Persian loanwords in Jonah.[^10] Where do you find yourself agreeing with Elle, and where with Marty? What sets your own threshold for being persuaded?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Drawing on Peter Enns and Bruce Feiler, the notes say we are always "painting a picture" with Scripture by choosing which stories to tell, how, and when: "the question is what picture we paint and why." Brent asks, "Without the art, how would any of this change our lives?"[^11] What picture of the Bible have you been painting, and what has shaped it? What color would you like to add?

</div>

### Diagnosis and Healing <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#diagnosis-and-healing}

*However many sections we see, Isaiah moves from naming what is wrong to healing it.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 39:5–8 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Hezekiah answers Isaiah's warning: "The word of the Lord you have spoken is good… for he thought, 'There will be peace and security in my lifetime.'" Brent closes the episode with "May it be so for us," but the notes add that the verse "can also be read as warning against complacency: Hezekiah settles for peace in his own lifetime while exile hangs over the next generation."[^12] What do you notice in Hezekiah's response? Which reading seems closer to the text to you, and why?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Most scholars, including most conservative ones, see at least two sections in Isaiah, 1–39 and 40–66, with 36–39 as a narrative bridge. Elle borrows two Mesopotamian priests: the *baru* (a diviner who reads where you stand) for Isaiah 1–39, and the *ashipu* (a healer who breaks curses) for Isaiah 40–66. She links Hezekiah's name to *chazaq* ("strength") as a strength that can *bind*, and she presents all of this openly as her own hypothesis.[^13] What does the picture of God as first diagnosing and then healing help you see in Isaiah?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes suggest a way to apply Isaiah's two movements: in personal or community darkness, Isaiah 1–39 "names honestly what we have reaped," and Isaiah 40–66 "insists that this is not the end." A group might ask where it is "found wanting" and where God is "breaking the curse."[^14] Share as much or as little as you like: where in your life, or in a community you belong to, do you need both an honest diagnosis and a word of healing?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Tonight's episode was an argument among friends about how Scripture came to be. Elle showed what source theories offer and where they go wrong, and Marty stayed open to multiple voices while admitting the dangers. Both agreed that honest scholarship and faithful, creative reading need each other. Whether we see two sections in Isaiah or four, the book moves from diagnosis to healing. We are always painting a picture with the text, so the important questions are what picture we are painting and why.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, name one question about the Bible you want to keep asking, with this group or someone else, rather than settle alone.

</div>
