### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** East and West split over how to preserve truth, icons or creeds, and Marty asked whether Christendom's imperial mindset has ever really ended.[^1]

**This week:** In one of the darkest chapters of church history, the Crusades, the episode asks Christians to own their history rather than disown it.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

After the East-West Schism, the Western church faced a crisis: if the East could tell the Pope "no," what did papal authority mean?[^3] Marty offers what he calls his own hunch, not received teaching, that the Crusades helped unify a fragmenting Christendom around a common enemy. He takes care to say the radical movement within Islam heading toward Palestine "did not represent the Islamic faith as a whole," and he names a double standard: Christians refuse to be defined by their own fringe while painting others by theirs.[^4] The notes describe the Crusades as Empire thinking, with the wealthy and powerful sending the poor to fight, and compare it to Israel making bricks in Egypt.[^5]

The episode contrasts the Jewish habit of owning history ("WE were in Egypt") with a Western faith that is "just personal," and adapts a familiar saying: "A dark history disowned by the descendants of it will be bound to repeat itself."[^6] It closes with the rise of Scholasticism and the university, which created a new divide based on education, and with Thomas Aquinas's synthesis of learning through Greek philosophy, which leads Marty to ask what the church had lost a thousand years after Jesus.[^7]

### The Crusades: Empire in Jesus's Name <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-crusades-empire-in-jesuss-name}

*The episode refuses to soften what was done in Jesus's name and asks what kind of kingdom it served.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes describe a Western church shaken by the schism, its claim of apostolic succession challenged, and Marty's hunch (in the words of a common saying) that "Nothing brings people together like a common enemy."[^3] What makes a common enemy such a powerful way to unify a divided group, and what does it cost?

*Read Exodus 1:8–14 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes observe that not everyone fought in the Crusades: "the wealthy and powerful sent the poor, uneducated, and commoners to do the fighting," a pattern that echoes "the Israelites making bricks in Egypt under Pharaoh - this is Egypt, not the way of Jesus."[^5] As you listen to the Exodus passage, what do you notice about who decides, who labors, and why the powerful act as they do? Report one detail that sounds familiar from the Crusades as the notes describe them.

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty describes three movements within Islam during the canonization of the Quran (progressive, moderate and radical) and notes that "we paint Islam with the brush of its most radical adherents while rejecting that same standard for Christianity."[^4] Why is that double standard so easy to fall into, and what helps us resist it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes set Empire (force, coercion, hierarchy, the stick rather than invitation) against Shalom (invitation, voice, living on every word that comes from the mouth of God).[^8] The Crusaders believed their cause was holy. When you care deeply about a good cause, what Empire tools (pressure, fear, using people) are you most tempted to reach for, and what would choosing invitation look like instead?

</div>

### Owning Versus Disowning History <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#owning-versus-disowning-history}

*Jewish tradition says "we" about the whole story; much Western faith says "me."*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Deuteronomy 6:20–25 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes contrast the Jewish approach, "WE were in Egypt... WE were in the desert," which owns every part of its history as corporate identity, with a Western faith that is "just personal," a matter between "God and I" or "me and Jesus."[^6] In this passage, a parent answers a child's question about the commandments. What pronouns does the answer use, and what does that suggest about who the story belongs to? Report what you notice.

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** On an Israel trip, a Jewish guide was asked why he did not follow Jesus. He answered: "For us, Jesus is connected to 1,800 years of brutal Church history, much of which has been just filled with the slaughter of people in the name of this Jesus."[^9] What is your honest reaction to hearing that? How does it change the way you think about how Jesus is seen by people outside the church?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Donald Miller's *Blue Like Jazz* describes a "reverse confessional" at Reed College, where Christians confessed on behalf of their faith, and readers pushed back: "Why would I apologize for something that happened centuries ago?"[^10] What would you say to someone who asked that question?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Marty adapts the familiar saying: "A dark history disowned by the descendants of it will be bound to repeat itself."[^6] Every tradition in our room has chapters it would rather not claim. Thinking diagnostically rather than with blame, what would it look like for you, or for your tradition, to own a hard piece of its history in a way that changes how you live now?

</div>

### Scholasticism and What Was Lost <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#scholasticism-and-what-was-lost}

*While the poor fought, a small educated elite built the foundations of the modern university.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say university education was so exclusive that Brent suggests "1% might even be overstating the case," with perhaps five people in a room, and that it gave "an incredible, almost inconceivable advantage."[^7] Where do you see knowledge or education creating a gap between people today, including in churches?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** Thomas Aquinas "changed the face of Christian history by bringing into a logical order the many fields of education," and our education system is largely shaped by his perspective. Yet Marty hopes listeners will "critically examine just how lost we are 1,000 years after the Judaic movement of Jesus."[^11] Looking back over the last seven weeks, what do you think the church had lost by this point? Which of those losses would you most like to recover in your own walk with God?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> This week we faced the Crusades without looking away. A church shaken by schism found unity in a common enemy, and the powerful sent the poor to fight in Jesus's name, repeating the pattern of Pharaoh rather than the way of Jesus. The episode asks us to say "we" about this history the way Jewish tradition says "WE were in Egypt," because a dark history disowned by its descendants is bound to repeat itself. At the same time, learning flourished for a privileged few, and Greek logic took the place of much the church had received from its Jewish roots. Owning the story is the first step toward not repeating it.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one piece of the church's history you are willing to say "we" about this week?

</div>
