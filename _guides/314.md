### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** In the Good Samaritan, the difference among the three travelers was how close each one came to the wounded man, and Jesus turned "Who is my neighbor?" into "Who am I being called to be a neighbor to?"[^1]

**This week:** Brent and Reed (Marty is absent) look at the parable Reed calls "the barn builder," a title borrowed from Eugene Peterson, which Jesus tells after a man in the crowd demands his share of an inheritance.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The parable sits at the center of the season's series on the parables, and it starts with an interruption.[^2] A man in a huge crowd demands that Jesus make his brother divide their inheritance. Jesus refuses to be a "divider" between the brothers, warns against greed, and tells a story about a man who gets a windfall harvest, stores it all for himself, and dies that same night with no one to leave it to.[^2]

Reed argues that the parable is less about whether wealth is moral and more about relationships. The rich man talks only to himself, and God's final question is a "who" question. Possessions can become a bridge that connects us to other people or a gulf that leaves us isolated, and a strict demand for fairness can choke out grace.[^3]

### Opening <span class="guide-time">10 min</span> {#opening}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-opening" markdown="1">

**Opening Question:** Reed observes that the things we buy carry an unspoken promise ("Get this and you'll be set"), and that at Christmas the anticipation often turns out to be more exciting than owning the thing.[^4] Think of something you wanted badly and finally got. How long did the satisfaction last, and what surprised you about it?

</div>

### An Interruption in the Crowd <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#an-interruption-in-the-crowd}

*The parable answers a real interruption, and Jesus refuses the role the man hands him.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Luke 12:13–15 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The hosts point out that the man does not ask a rabbi to help mediate a fair settlement, which was a normal request. He issues a command: "*Tell* my brother to divide the inheritance with me."[^5] Brent notes how little we actually know about the older brother's side.[^5] What do you notice about how the man phrases his request? What does his wording suggest about how he sees his brother, and himself?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The Greek word translated "arbiter" or "judge" here is *meristēs*, "divider," and it appears only here in the New Testament.[^6] Reed hears Jesus refusing to divide people as well as property: "Don't make me do something that will further break an already broken relationship." If Jesus had forced the issue, the older brother might have handed over the share and then cut ties.[^6] Why might Jesus refuse to settle what looks like a reasonable request? What would have been lost if he had simply ruled?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say that quarrels over a will usually expose problems that already existed in the relationships, then as now.[^7] Where have you seen a fight over things (in a family, a church, or a workplace) that was really about something else? If this touches a recent loss, feel free to listen rather than share.

</div>

### The Man Who Talked to Himself <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-man-who-talked-to-himself}

*The story's details show a man who receives a windfall, plans alone, and dies with no one to receive what he stored.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Luke 12:16–21 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** As the passage is read, count every "I," "my," and "myself" in the rich man's words. The notes observe that the ground produced the harvest, that he consults no family, friends, or community (which would have been culturally unusual), and that building bigger barns is not wrong in itself.[^8] What do you notice when you count? Which other details show how alone he is?

*Read Ecclesiastes 8:8–15 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The rich man's "Take life easy; eat, drink and be merry" quotes Ecclesiastes 8:15, but the notes point out that he ignores its context: no one knows the day of their death, and sometimes the wicked receive what the righteous deserve.[^8] Brent adds Psalm 49, where the wise and the foolish both die and leave their wealth to others.[^9] How does the wider passage in Ecclesiastes change how the rich man's plan sounds?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes say "life" in verse 15 is *zoē*, the life of the kingdom and the age to come.[^10] Reed observes that wealthy listeners quickly ask whether it is immoral to have a retirement account or a big house, but he does not think Jesus is interested in drawing that line. The real question is "How's that working for you?" or, as the notes put it, "Is your stuff actually giving you the life you want?"[^10] Why do you think our first reaction to this parable is so often to ask where the line is? How would you answer Reed's question about your own possessions, large or small?

</div>

### Bridge or Gulf <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#bridge-or-gulf}

*God's final question is a "who" question, and it points from possessions to people.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes stress that God does not ask how the wealth will be distributed or why the man stored it. God asks whose it will be, and the implied answer is "no one."[^11] Reed cites Augustine: "The bellies of the poor would have made better storehouses for this man's grain than his barns."[^11] Then Jesus turns the question back on the man in the crowd: if you get the full inheritance and it costs you your brother, who will get what's yours when you die?[^12] How does that "who" question reframe the brother's demand? What does it suggest wealth is for?

*Read Jeremiah 22:15–16 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Brent and Reed connect being "rich toward God" to being generous toward people, pointing to Matthew 25 and to Jeremiah 22:16, where defending the poor and needy is called knowing God.[^13] The notes call this a recurring BEMA theme: faith "is about people."[^13] Our traditions have described knowing God in different ways. What does it add to hear justice for the poor described as knowing God?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** Kenneth Bailey tells of two brothers who inherit their father's land. Each believes the other deserves more, and each sneaks out at night to move the boundary stone so his brother's field grows; they meet in the dark and embrace in tears.[^14] Reed adds Paul's "Why not rather be wronged?" (1 Corinthians 6:7) and warns that "fairness can really kill grace and mercy in our relationships." His everyday example is a friend who still owes $17.50 for a shared gift: "Just let it go and see what happens."[^14] Where is insisting on fairness costing you a relationship, or threatening to? What would moving the boundary stone look like in one small way this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The barn builder is not condemned for being rich or for building barns. His folly is that he did both alone: he talked only to himself, planned only for himself, and died with no one to receive what he stored. Jesus told the story to a man so focused on getting what was his that he was willing to lose his brother over it. God's question, "Whose will it be?", is aimed at us too. Whatever we have, a lot or a little, can become a bridge to other people or a gulf between us and them.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: name one thing you have (time, money, a skill, a possession) that could become a bridge to someone this week.

</div>
