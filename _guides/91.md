### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Matthew and Luke set the newborn Jesus beside Herod and Caesar, and we asked whether we would have recognized the King in a stable or kept looking for a better Caesar.

**This week:** The adult story opens with John the Baptist. The episode reads him as a first-century Jew: dressed like Elijah, shaped by the Essenes, and calling people to a public turning.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The title, *Tavilah T'Shuvah*, means "baptism of repentance." The episode shows John deliberately wearing Elijah's camel-hair garment and leather belt, so that his Jewish audience would understand his role without a sermon.[^1] It traces likely links between John and the Essenes, the community of priests in the wilderness at Qumran, and explains two kinds of Jewish immersion (*mikveh*): the frequent, prayerful washing practiced by the Pharisees, and the Essene immersion of repentance that marked a decision to turn and walk a new way.[^2]

The episode also lets John's fiery language stand: axes, winnowing forks and fire. It leaves an honest question open for the weeks ahead. John was right to call people back to faithfulness and right about who Jesus was, but did he fully understand what the Messiah had come to do?[^3]

**Opening Question:** Think of a time someone communicated something important to you without saying a word: a uniform, a gesture, a gift, the place they chose to meet you. What was the message, and how did you know?

### John in Elijah's Clothes <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#john-in-elijahs-clothes}

*John did not need to announce who he was; his clothes and his location said it for him.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read 2 Kings 1:5–8 and Matthew 3:1–6 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Compare the two descriptions. The notes explain that camel's hair worn with the hide against the skin felt "like wearing a Brillo pad," and that Elijah wore it "as a constant reminder of how irritated God was with the sin of his people."[^4] What do you notice about John's clothing, food and location, and what is he telling people about himself without saying it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes call this Eastern communication: "John didn't need to preach a sermon explaining 'I am like Elijah' - he simply showed up in the Elijah costume and his Jewish audience immediately understood."[^5] The original notes add that Matthew's Jewish readers "would have immediately picked this up. No need to preach a 3-point sermon about who he is."[^6] What does this suggest about details in the Gospels that we tend to skim past, and what might we be missing when we read only for explanations?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes give reasons to connect John with the Essenes: his father Zechariah is called a "righteous priest," not a Sadducee; he baptized less than three miles from Qumran; and his introduction uses Isaiah 40, "the exact passage the Essenes used to explain their desert community."[^7] Read Isaiah 40:3 aloud. The notes say John "took the Essene worldview but went on mission with it," bringing "the path to the people" instead of waiting in the desert.[^8] Why might that shift matter so much?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The Essenes waited for people to come to them; John went out. Where in your own faith are you waiting in the desert for people to come to you, and what would it look like to bring the path to them this month?

</div>

### A Baptism of Turning <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#a-baptism-of-turning}

*In the first century, immersion could be a daily prayer or a once-for-all public turning.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 3:5–10 and Luke 3:10–14 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes describe two kinds of *mikveh*. Pharisaic immersion was "Ritualistic, liturgical cleansing performed frequently," more prayer than repentance, and the original notes say Jesus "likely performed it all of the time" even while critiquing an external version of it.[^9] The Essene kind was "A baptism of repentance performed when making a decision to turn from sin," a public declaration: "I have been disobedient, and today I start walking in a new way."[^10] Look at John's answers in Luke 3. What does "fruit in keeping with repentance" look like in practice for each group that asks him?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes stress that John "wasn't doing a Christian baptism (Jesus wasn't around yet). Instead he was doing a Jewish baptism," and that in Jewish practice "individuals immersed themselves; baptism was self-performed."[^11] Our traditions practice and explain baptism in different ways, and this is not the place to settle that. What does this Jewish background add to how you understand your own tradition's practice, and what questions does it raise for you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say John refused to baptize "those merely putting on a show" and "rejected reliance on ethnic identity."[^12] They also report a view that John offered *mikveh* in the desert to "tax collectors, soldiers, and cultural outcasts" who were shut out of the temple system.[^13] The notes apply this to any religious community: institutions "can become gatekeepers who exclude those on the margins."[^14] Without pointing at any one tradition, how can a church tell when it has started guarding a door that God meant to open?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** John's baptism was a public statement: from today, I walk a new way. What is one specific turning you sense you need to make, and who is one person you could tell so that they can watch you walk it?

</div>

### Fire and an Open Question <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#fire-and-an-open-question}

*John was right about a great deal; the episode asks whether he was right about everything.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 3:10–12 and John 3:17 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes collect John's images: "The ax is already at the root of the trees," a winnowing fork in hand, a baptism "with the Holy Spirit and fire," and a threshing floor, the site where the temple was built.[^15] They connect water to the flood and fire to Sodom and Gomorrah, both images of judgment.[^16] Based on these verses, what does John expect the coming Messiah to do?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes set John's expectation beside Jesus's words that he came "not to condemn the world but to save it."[^17] They recall Elijah's lesson: "fire and passion don't always change the human heart."[^18] Where do you think John could be right about repentance and still incomplete about the Messiah, and what does it mean that the Bible may be inviting us to notice that?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q11:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty argues that John functioned as Jesus's rabbi, and the original notes say "Every interaction Jesus has with John would be an interaction a person would expect a disciple to have with his Rabbi."[^19] That raises the question the notes ask: "how does a disciple honor their rabbi while correcting their theology?"[^20] Think of a teacher, pastor or parent who formed your faith. How have you honored them in places where you now see things differently?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q12:** The notes draw a lesson from John: "Prophetic courage doesn't require complete understanding, but it does require humility to learn and grow when corrected."[^21] Where in your life do you carry Elijah's fire, and what would it look like to hold that fire with humility this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> John stepped into the wilderness wearing Elijah's itchy garment, and everyone who saw him knew what he meant. He took the Essene call to repentance out of the desert and offered it to the crowds, to tax collectors and soldiers, and to anyone willing to turn and walk a new way in public. He demanded real fruit, not performance. Yet the episode leaves John's fire and judgment standing beside a Messiah who came not to condemn the world but to save it. John got much right, and the story ahead will show that Jesus's mission was larger than even his rabbi expected.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one way you want your life to show fruit this week rather than performance?

</div>
