### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Pergamum was a city of thrones, where some believers held to "the teaching of Balaam," the idea that they could serve God and still join the city's festivals. John answered with a sword sharper than Rome's and a white stone with a new name, grounding every image in Scripture through *remez*.

**This week:** Thyatira was the smallest of the seven cities and the most dominated by trade guilds. John's letter addresses its guild feasts and a local prophetess, and the episode names the two questions that unlock all of Revelation.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The notes describe Thyatira as a hardworking, blue-collar city of 25,000 to 30,000 people with one of the longest guild registries ever discovered.[^1] Guilds offered economic security and belonging, but their feasts involved idolatry and sexual immorality, so refusing them meant losing work and community.[^2] A Jewish prophetess named Sambathe ran a sibyl shrine there and promoted guild participation, and the notes suggest John calls her "Jezebel."[^3]

The headline of the episode is John's "dual-layer" writing. Every line of the letter, the notes say, points both to Thyatira's culture and to an Old Testament text, "Not half-and-half, but fully both at once."[^4] The Jezebel of 1 and 2 Kings, Psalm 2, Jeremiah 17, Daniel 10 and Balaam's oracle in Numbers 24 all appear, linked in a chain the notes trace back to Solomon.[^5] The episode leaves us with two questions for every passage: "What's the culture?" and "What's the Text?"[^6]

### A Blue-Collar City of Guilds <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#a-blue-collar-city-of-guilds}

*Thyatira was small and unsophisticated, a working town where your trade guild was your livelihood and your social world.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Revelation 2:18–21 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes describe Thyatira as "Hardworking, unsophisticated, industrial," contrasted with sophisticated cities like Smyrna.[^7] Its guilds included leather workers, weavers, dyers, potters, bronze smiths, blacksmiths, slave merchants and more, and they functioned as "a cross between labor unions and college fraternities."[^8] Its leading specialty was burnished bronze, polished into the closest thing the ancient world had to mirrors.[^9] Look at how Jesus describes himself and what he praises in verses 18–19. Which details seem chosen for this particular city?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes explain that refusing guild participation meant economic marginalization, social isolation and family hardship, and that the feasts involved food sacrificed to idols and sexual immorality, "Both presented as normal business networking."[^10] "This wasn't theoretical," the notes add; "it was the daily reality of making a living in Thyatira."[^11] How do you imagine a believer in a Thyatira guild weighed the choice between faithfulness and feeding their family?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes list modern parallels: networking events that compromise values, business practices that require "ethical flexibility," social acceptance that requires moral compromise, and charismatic leaders teaching accommodation.[^12] Where in your own working life, past or present, have you felt pressure like this, and how did you respond?

</div>

### That Woman Jezebel <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#that-woman-jezebel}

*The letter's sharpest words are aimed at a teacher inside the church, and John gives her a name from Israel's history.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Revelation 2:20–25 aloud, then 1 Kings 16:29–33.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes identify a local figure, Sambathe, a Jewish prophetess running a sibyl shrine (a "minor league oracle"), who promoted the Roman labor system and guild participation and whose practice involved witchcraft forbidden by Levitical law.[^13] They describe the biblical Jezebel as a Phoenician high priestess of Asherah, married to King Ahab, who led Israel into idolatry and sexual immorality through Asherah worship.[^14] Compare Revelation 2:20 with 1 Kings 16:29–33. What parallels do you notice between the two situations?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes trace a chain of events behind John's choice of name. Solomon outsourced bronze smith and stone cutter work for the temple to the Phoenicians, trading twenty Galilean towns to Hiram; later generations kept that alliance, which led to Ahab's marriage to Jezebel and widespread Asherah worship.[^15] The notes report that "Many rabbis trace Israel's idolatry problems back to Solomon's choice to use foreign workers," and point out that the very trades Solomon outsourced were prominent in Thyatira's guilds.[^16] What does this long chain suggest about how a compromise made for good reasons can shape a community for generations?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes highlight the phrase "I have given her time to repent" as a window into "God's patience and judgment," and raise the question of corporate responsibility for "a community tolerating sin."[^17] The traditions in this room handle discipline and correction in the church differently. Without deciding whose approach is right, what do you think a church owes both to a teacher leading people astray and to the people being led?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes ask, "What forms does 'Jezebel' teaching take in contemporary church contexts?" and "How can churches discern between legitimate cultural engagement and compromise?"[^18] Where do you hear voices today, inside or outside the church, telling believers that economic survival or success justifies a compromise, and how do you test what they say?

</div>

### What's the Culture? What's the Text? <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#whats-the-culture-whats-the-text}

*The episode's breakthrough is seeing how a single phrase can point to the city and to Scripture at once.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Revelation 2:26–29 aloud, then Psalm 2:7–9 and Numbers 24:17.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes list cultural references in the letter alongside Old Testament ones. "Son of God," used only here among the seven letters, may echo the city's god Tyrimnos, a son of Zeus, or Domitian's deceased infant son, and it also quotes Psalm 2, "the 'Son of God' Psalm."[^19] The "iron scepter" and "pottery" fit the blacksmith and potter guilds and also come from Psalm 2; the "morning star" appeared on coins held by Domitian's son and also recalls Balaam's last oracle in Numbers 24.[^20] Read Psalm 2:7–9 and Numbers 24:17 and find the words John uses. How does knowing both layers change what verses 26–28 promise?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes call this layering "complex brilliance" and quote a teacher who says, "It's almost like the author had help."[^21] They recommend avoiding "reading modern newspapers into ancient texts."[^22] How does this two-question approach compare with the way you were first taught to read Revelation?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes describe John's method as "using biblical quotations that already teach to the cultural context at hand."[^23] If you asked the two questions of your own life, "What's the culture?" around you and "What's the Text?" that speaks to it, what passage of Scripture would you choose for the pressure you are facing right now?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Thyatira was a working town, and its believers were known for their love, faith, service and perseverance. But making a living there meant joining a guild, and the guild feasts meant idols and immorality, and a prophetess in the church was telling them it was fine. John names her Jezebel, and in doing so he calls up the whole story of how one compromise in Solomon's day opened the door to Asherah in Ahab's. Every line of this letter points to the city and to Scripture at once. That is the invitation for us too: ask what the culture is, ask what the Text is, and hold fast to what we have until he comes.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one area of your work or daily life where you want to "hold fast" this week?

</div>
