### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Marty and Brent read Isaiah 1–6 as a father pleading with comfortable children, heard God's sincere "come now, let us reason together," and ended with Isaiah's "Here am I, send me."[^1]

**This week:** With Marty away, Brent, Elle and Josh stop at Isaiah 6 to look more closely at Isaiah's call, and at how it compares with the rituals of Assyria's diviner-priests.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode opens with loose ends from last week: what the Hebrew behind "let us reason together" means, and what archaeology says about Israel's early kings. The hosts then review what a prophet is: someone who feels deep grief over the world, shares a vision of something better, and tries to put an overwhelming experience into human words. They also place prophets and priests on a spectrum.[^2]

The second half looks at Isaiah 6:1–7: the meaning of Uzziah's name and death, Isaiah's confession of "unclean lips," and the tension between collective responsibility and personal guilt. Elle sets Isaiah's mouth-cleansing beside the purification ritual of Assyrian *bārû* diviner-priests and shows how Isaiah's vision borrows a familiar pattern and then reverses it.[^2]

### What Kind of Voice Is a Prophet's? <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#what-kind-of-voice-is-a-prophets}

*The hosts soften the "thundering from Mount Sinai" picture of prophecy and suggest a humbler, more human voice.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 1:18 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Elle explains that the verse opens with a plural imperative ("come," addressed to everyone together), softened by the particle *na* ("please"), and that "let us reason together" is in the cohortative, a Hebrew form that expresses desire. The result reads more like "Oh, that we might reason together": a heartfelt wish, not a legal summons.[^3] Read the verse again with that sense in mind. What changes in how you picture God saying it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The phrase usually translated "Thus says the Lord" (*koh amar*) carries the sense of "This is *like* what God says," and Josh notes it sounds a lot like the modern "God was like…" The Hebrew word for prophet (*navi*) is linked to an image of dripping: God is the honeycomb, and the prophet catches one drop and holds it up.[^4] How does this picture of a prophet compare with the one you brought into this series?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Josh places priests and prophets on a spectrum. Priests are collaborative and depend on consent; prophets are disruptive and don't wait for permission. He compares prophets to the body's pain response: necessary, but a sign something is wrong if it is happening all the time. The hosts' application is to be "more priestly" in conversations, especially on social media, since being loud or obnoxious is not the same as being prophetic.[^5] In your conversations (online, at work, at home), where do you default on that spectrum? What would being "more priestly" look like in one conversation you are likely to have this week?

</div>

### In the Year the Fierce Strength of God Died <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#in-the-year-the-fierce-strength-of-god-died}

*Isaiah's call comes at a moment of lost hope, and his first words take responsibility for a whole people.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read 2 Chronicles 26:16–21, then Isaiah 6:1 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** *Uzziah* combines *oz* (fierce strength) and *Yah* (God), so "in the year that King Uzziah died" can be heard as "in the year the fierce strength of God died." Uzziah was a great king until he offered incense in the temple, a priestly act, and was struck with leprosy. The high priest who confronted him was Azariah, whose name contains *ezer*, "help."[^6] From the Chronicles passage, what did Uzziah do, who tried to stop him, and what happened? The hosts suggest that fierce strength is not supposed to replace help. Where have you seen strength push aside the people who could have helped?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Isaiah's call comes in chapter 6, not chapter 1, after five chapters of failure. In the middle of that pain, God invites Isaiah into his presence. Elle says we leave sin behind not by obsessing over it but by seeing how weighty (*kavod*) God is; next to that, our sin is like "walking around with our pants down," and we pull them up and get to work.[^7] What do you make of the order: failure first, then an invitation into God's presence?

*Read Isaiah 6:5 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Isaiah says he is a man of unclean lips who lives among a people of unclean lips. He does not say "I'm one of the good Israelites." Josh and Elle name the individualist trap ("I'm a Christian, but not *that* kind of Christian") and point to phones and clothing that depend on exploited workers in the Congo and Bangladesh: collective responsibility without personal guilt. Elle adds that when a culture ranks groups by suffering, people may try to prove their own suffering to re-establish their "purity."[^8] Where do you most instinctively separate yourself from "those people" in your own group? What would it cost you to say "I live among a people of unclean lips" about it?

</div>

### Three Reversals <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#three-reversals}

*Torah has no purity law about "unclean lips." Elle suggests the image makes more sense against Assyrian ritual practice.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** In the *bārû* ritual, the diviner-priest washes, puts on clean clothing and is anointed, purifies his lips with cedar while repeating "I am the one whose lips are pure," sets the cedar on fire to summon the gods, and then finds them seated on great thrones. The hosts note two similarities with Isaiah 6: a mouth-cleansing act involving wood or fire, and a deity on a throne.[^9]

*Read Isaiah 6:1–7 aloud.*

Lay the two scenes side by side. Besides the similarities above, what differences do you notice before we look at the hosts' list?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Elle names three reversals. First, the coal is already burning: God is present before Isaiah calls, seeks or asks. Second, Isaiah says "I am impure" where the *bārû* says "my lips are pure." Third, one touch of the coal is completely sufficient, with no list of ritual steps.[^10] Which of the three reversals says the most to you about God, and which says the most about how we come before him?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** Elle describes the urge to add layers of steps after sin (telling the small group, setting up accountability texts, and so on). These can be good, she says, but they are not prerequisites for intimacy with God: the coal is enough. The hosts also note that the Assyrian model puts the person in control of when they meet the divine, while Isaiah's already-burning coal says God has been there the whole time.[^11] What "steps" do you tend to require of yourself before you let yourself come near God? What would it look like to receive the coal this week, then stop dithering and get on with what God is asking?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Prophets are not angry people shouting from a mountaintop. They are humans catching one drop of honey from an enormous hive and saying, "This is *like* what God is saying." Isaiah's call comes in the year Israel's "fierce strength of God" died, after five chapters of failure, and he confesses that he and his people are unclean without trying to prove he is one of the good ones. Against diviner-priests who lit fires to summon their gods and declared their lips pure, Isaiah's vision reverses every step: God is already present, honesty replaces claims of purity, and one burning coal is enough. The invitation is to be more priestly than prophetic, to take collective responsibility without drowning in guilt, and to say, "Here am I. Send me."[^12]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what would it mean for you to say "Here am I" this week?

</div>
