### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Moses felt disqualified because he was unsure he belonged to God's people, yet God asked for his availability rather than his qualifications. We also saw that the "wrath" stories of Sodom and the Exodus are stories of God hearing the cry of the oppressed and acting to restore.[^1]

**This week:** Using Ray Vander Laan's teaching videos, the episode makes Egypt our first metaphor for empire and sets it against God's way of *shalom* (peace, wholeness, everything in its proper place).[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode walks through three of Ray Vander Laan's *That the World May Know* videos. The first compares Egypt's creation story with Genesis. In Egypt's version, Pharaoh holds open the cartouche, the ordered space between the waters above and below, and some people must be pushed to the edges to keep it open. Ray asks whether we have bought into the wrong story. The second video visits Goshen, the richest farmland on earth, where the Israelites were first given a comfortable life before that same system enslaved them. The third turns to the Exodus itself: Moses' shepherd's staff swallowing the staffs of Pharaoh's magicians, plagues that take down Egypt's gods one by one, and a Passover that asks Israel to choose publicly.[^3]

The episode's big claim is that empire and *shalom* are built on different foundations: fear and coercion on one side, trust and invitation on the other. The notes put the question this way: not only "How does God get His people out of Egypt?" but "How does God get Egypt out of His people?"[^4] This material touches politics, economics and our own comfort. The aim tonight is honest self-examination, not taking sides on current events.

### Two Creation Stories <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#two-creation-stories}

*Two accounts of how the world began produce two very different answers about who matters.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 1:26–31 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes summarize the Egyptian story Ray describes: Ra emerges from chaotic waters, and humanity is an afterthought, made from his tears of joy. People exist to serve the gods, and only Pharaoh bears the divine image. Pharaoh holds the cartouche open so the waters do not flood back in.[^5] As you listen to Genesis 1, what do you notice about who bears God's image, why the world is ordered, and how God describes what he has made?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** Ray points out that when everything depends on keeping the vault open, some people become expendable: the poor, the sick, the alien, the elderly. The notes record his warning about a culture where "life is cheap and sacrifice of others is acceptable to sustain your own obsession with pleasure and entertainment and wealth."[^6] Why does a story in which humans hold the world together tend to make some people disposable?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Ray asks whether we have bought into a story of fear, and whether we have put our trust in Pharaoh or in anyone other than God.[^7] In your own life, who or what plays the role of "holding the vault open," the thing you feel would let everything collapse if it stopped?

</div>

### The Gift of Goshen <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-gift-of-goshen}

*Empire rarely begins with chains. It often begins with a gift.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Exodus 1:6–14 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes describe Goshen as lush land with about a hundred feet of topsoil, given to nomadic Israelites who could move during flood season. In Ray's telling, Pharaoh provided ten-day work weeks with two- or three-day weekends, a generous middle-class wage and three-room apartments. Over time the Israelites gave in to the Egyptian story, and a new pharaoh "who knew not Joseph" turned on them.[^8] What do you notice in this passage about how quickly things change, and about who ends up doing the building?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Egypt led the world in metalwork thanks to furnaces hot enough that the slaves working them "dropped like flies." The Israelites would have used the tools those furnaces produced. The notes draw the lesson that empire creates complicity even among the oppressed, and that those who buy into empire eventually find themselves at its bottom.[^9] Why might a comfortable system be harder to leave than an obviously cruel one?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Ray asks where we would say God is: in the lush barley fields or in the desert. The notes describe the temptation as standing with "one foot in waist-deep barley and one foot in barren desert," enjoying empire's benefits while claiming God's way. They suggest asking what costs our comfort, security and luxury place on others.[^10] This is not a question about any political party. Where in your own life are you standing with a foot in each, and whose cost makes your comfort possible?

</div>

### Two Staffs and a Choice <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#two-staffs-and-a-choice}

*The contest with Pharaoh is a contest between two kinds of power.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Exodus 7:8–13 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes say Egyptian hieroglyphics typically show Pharaoh holding a staff over his slaves in a threatening posture. In this story, Moses' staff swallows all the others, which Ray reads as "the crooked staff versus the shepherds staff," Empire's coercion against the shepherd's care.[^11] What do you notice in the passage about where each staff comes from and what happens to them? What kind of power does each one represent?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes explain that God's aim in the plagues is for the Hebrews, the Egyptians and even Pharaoh to *yada* (know intimately, by experience) that he is God. Ray describes God as at war with Egypt's gods rather than angry at Egyptians, taking them down one by one.[^12] How does that change the way you hear the plague stories, compared with how you first learned them?

*Read Exodus 12:21–28 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** In Ray's telling, the chief Egyptian god was represented by a goat, and God asks the Israelites to slaughter a lamb or goat and put its blood on their doorposts. That was a public rejection of Egypt's religious system, and the notes say many, but not all, Israelites chose God's story. The episode concludes: "Empire and Shalom are built on fundamentally different principles. You cannot have both and you cannot have a Godly empire."[^13] What would one concrete, visible act of choosing *shalom* over empire look like for you, or for our group, this month?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Egypt tells a story in which Pharaoh holds the world together and some lives are cheap. Genesis tells a story in which God orders the world as a gift and every person bears his image. Israel received Egypt's comfort in Goshen and then discovered what that comfort cost. God's rescue had to reach their hearts as well as their bodies, which is why the Passover asked them to choose. The notes leave us with Ray's call: "We need to be people who hear the cry."[^14]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one place where you suspect a little Egypt is still living in you?

</div>
