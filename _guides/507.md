### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Elle Grover Fricks described discipleship as spiritual parenting: taking real responsibility for someone while protecting their independence. We carried forward the hydrangea that can't be made into a tulip, the "I do, we do, you do" pattern, and the call to name the good.

**This week:** Reed Dent, who spoke on community in Week 8, returns to talk with Brent about discipleship as a slow work that depends on paying attention.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Reed has spent 18 years in campus ministry with Campus Christian Fellowship (CCF). He doesn't offer a step-by-step program. His main claim is that discipleship is slow: it means paying humble attention to who a person really is instead of who we think they should be, inside a shared life that builds trust over time. He draws on Eugene Peterson's memory of learning to cut meat with the grain in his father's butcher shop, and on Robert Farrar Capon's practice of simply sitting with an onion.

His central image is a blacksmith's forge. Shared meals, games and ordinary days bring a relationship "up to temperature," and then a moment of openness comes when a word of wisdom, rebuke, encouragement or call can land. He ends by naming the fruit he hopes for: following the self-giving way of Jesus, becoming fully alive, and partnering with God to bring shalom into the world.

### Opening <span class="guide-time">10 min</span> {#opening}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-opening" markdown="1">

**Opening Question:** Think of something in your life that only got good slowly (a friendship, a skill, a garden, a home, a craft). What would have been lost if someone had tried to hurry it?

</div>

### A Slow Work <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#a-slow-work}

*Reed begins by refusing to rush the work, and by insisting that a mentor has to learn who a person actually is.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Reed borrows the phrase "the slow work of God" from Pierre Teilhard de Chardin's prayer, which accepts that growth passes through "stages of instability." He contrasts real discipleship with a Domino's conveyor belt, where a program goes in at one end and a finished disciple comes out the other. Much of mentoring, he says, is "bearing patiently with one another in the progress we're not making"[^1]. Where have you seen the conveyor-belt picture in church life, and what does Reed's alternative ask of the people on both sides of the relationship?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** Reed tells how Eugene Peterson learned in his father's butcher shop that meat has a grain, and that hacking at it by force gives messy, wasteful results. Peterson calls humility "to yield to the thing rather than imposing your will on it." Reed admits that the hidden expectation in mentoring is often "they should be like me," and he turns to Paul for the corrective[^2].

*Read 1 Corinthians 3:6 aloud.*

What does Paul say each person did, and who does he credit with the growth? If God gives the growth, what is left for the person doing the planting and watering?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Brent notes that none of the Twelve became like Jesus in every way; Peter, John, James and Thomas kept very different personalities. Reed says the goal isn't "a bunch of Reed Dents walking around who read out loud to each other," but the spirit of Christ, which gives itself for the sake of the world in love, expressed in a way true to each person[^3]. Think of someone you have hoped would become more like you. What might God be growing in them that does not look like you at all?

</div>

### Heat, Then Strike <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#heat-then-strike}

*Reed's central image is the forge: the iron has to be brought up to heat before it can be struck.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** At CCF, Reed says, discipleship stands on three legs: friendship, conversation and spiritual practice. Brent admits he wouldn't have counted a Sunday night gathering of 200 students as discipleship, but Reed's framework explains why it counts: it builds the friendship the deeper work depends on[^4]. In the forge picture, meals, games, weddings, funerals and late nights all heat the iron, and you come to know someone by how they react when they're fouled in basketball or think someone is cheating at a board game[^5]. Why might ordinary time like this tell you more about a person than their answers to Bible questions?

*Read Matthew 18:21–35 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Reed points to this passage as an example of the forge. Peter asks how many times he must forgive his brother (Reed suggests he may literally mean Andrew), and Jesus sees that "the iron is hot" and tells the parable of the unforgiving servant[^6]. What in Peter's question suggests a moment of openness? What do you notice about how Jesus answers: with a rule, a story, or something else?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Reed describes Robert Farrar Capon's long reflection on sitting with an onion, paying attention to a thing for what it is rather than what it can do for us. People, he says, aren't blank slates waiting for a 12-month curriculum, and when people truly see each other, "it almost doesn't even matter what the program is"[^7]. He adds that moments of openness are mutual, and that he benefits from his students as much as they benefit from him[^8]. Think about the person you paid attention to this past week. What did you notice about them that you had not seen before?

</div>

### The Fruit of Discipleship <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#the-fruit-of-discipleship}

*Reed ends by naming what he hopes discipleship produces.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Philippians 2:5–11 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Reed's first hoped-for fruit is following the way of Jesus, the mind of Christ in this hymn of self-emptying: giving ourselves in love for the world, out of real life rather than grim duty[^9]. What do you notice about the direction Jesus moves in this passage? What is the difference between living that out of real life and living it out of grim duty?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Reed's other two fruits are becoming fully alive (John 10:10; John 15:11; John 14:27) and partnering with God to bring shalom, including through our work and careers[^10]. He cautions against walking up to a coworker and asking, "Do you want me to disciple you?" Brent's example is a new McDonald's employee who is already learning from how you treat customers, so being intentional in ordinary tasks "brings the temperature up on the forge"[^11]. In your own work or daily routine, who is already watching how you do things, and what is one way you could bring more care to that this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Reed's main point is that people aren't projects; they are gifts to pay attention to. Discipleship isn't putting someone through a program or making copies of ourselves. It is the slow, patient, humble work of learning who a person really is and joining in what God is already growing in them. Shared meals, games and ordinary days bring a relationship up to temperature, and then, sometimes after years, a moment opens where a word of truth can land. Whether we're parents, coworkers, friends or mentors, the call is to slow down, linger, notice, and trust that God gives the growth[^12].
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, name one person whose "grain" you want to pay closer attention to this month.

</div>
