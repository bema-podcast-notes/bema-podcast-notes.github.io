### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Looking at the story of Mars Hill, we turned the question from "why did he do it?" to "why do we do it?" and asked how our own measures of success (numbers, platforms, personalities) help build cultures where spiritual abuse can grow.[^1]

**This week:** The fourth and final episode of the spiritual abuse series is not a lesson but a liturgy: prayers of lament, confession and intercession about domestic violence and spiritual abuse, written to be prayed again and again.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This short episode works differently from almost every other BEMA episode. Instead of teaching, the hosts lead listeners through adapted prayers and intercessions, a space for lament, confession, listening and corporate repentance that draws back to Session 1 and the idea of "creating intentional space for God's presence".[^3] The prayers name the many forms abuse takes (emotional, verbal, physical, sexual, economic, spiritual and psychological) and state plainly that a survivor's suffering is not their fault, not a punishment from God, and not a cross to bear.[^4]

The liturgy also calls the church to repent of its own part: protecting comfort and reputation, misusing Scripture, being slow to believe and act, and telling survivors what to do instead of supporting them.[^5] It prays for survivors, for perpetrators to recognize their behavior and seek help, and for witnesses to become as compassionate as Jesus would be.[^6] This is tender ground. Some in the room may carry these experiences, and no one is ever asked to share their own story. The episode points to help that is available, including RAINN (Rape, Assault and Incest National Network) at rainn.org and the National Domestic Violence Hotline.[^7]

### A Liturgy, Not a Lecture <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#a-liturgy-not-a-lecture}

*This episode asks to be prayed, not just heard, so we begin with why that matters.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The hosts expect listeners might "come back to and play more than once" and use it "on a routine basis," in personal devotion or even in corporate worship and small groups.[^8] Why might a topic like abuse need a liturgy and not only a teaching? What can praying the same words repeatedly do that learning the facts once cannot?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe the space this liturgy creates in four parts: lament and reflection, confession and listening (to God, to ourselves and to others' stories), intercession and corporate repentance, and contemplative pauses.[^3] Our traditions in this room practice prayer in quite different ways, some with written liturgy and some without. In your own tradition, which of these four comes most naturally, and which is rarely given room? What do you think explains the difference?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** If you were to take this liturgy seriously as a practice rather than an episode, what would it look like for you? Think concretely: when, where, how often, and what you would hope it slowly changes in you.

</div>

### What the Church Is Called to Confess <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#what-the-church-is-called-to-confess}

*The liturgy names specific ways the church has failed survivors and asks us to confess them as our own.*
{: .guide-note}

*This passage comes right after an account of sexual violence; these verses describe only what happened afterward. Read 2 Samuel 13:19–22 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes observe that Scripture includes stories of violence against women, and that their presence "should prompt honest storytelling, not silence"; Tamar's story in 2 Samuel 13 is one the notes point to.[^9] In these few verses, notice what Tamar does, what her brother tells her to do, and what the king feels and does. What do you see about who speaks, who is silenced, and who acts?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The liturgy names four areas for the church's repentance:[^5]

- an "addiction to comfort, convenience, and power" that protects reputations rather than the vulnerable;[^10]
- misusing Scripture in ways that justify or encourage violence, particularly against women;
- being slow to believe and act, with doubt, little compassion and paralysis when survivors need support most;
- dictating to survivors instead of supporting them and respecting their decisions.

This is a diagnosis, not a verdict on anyone's church. Which of these four do you think churches like the ones we come from are most vulnerable to, and why? What makes that particular failure feel reasonable to the people inside it at the time?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The prayers state three things abuse is not: not the survivor's fault, not a punishment from God, and not a cross to bear. The notes say this "directly counters harmful theology that has kept survivors trapped."[^4] Where have you heard suffering described as a cross to bear, or as something God sent to teach a lesson? What might those words sound like to someone living with abuse?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes say true repentance involves the church community, not only perpetrators.[^11] Thinking about yourself rather than others: if a friend told you something hard about what was happening at home or in a church, what instinct would you most need to resist (doubt, fixing, advice, silence), and what would you want to do instead?

</div>

### Truth for Survivors, Work for Witnesses <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#truth-for-survivors-work-for-witnesses}

*The liturgy closes by praying for healing and for a church that acts on what it prays.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** For witnesses and the church, the liturgy prays for people who resist judgment, believe survivors and act on their behalf, affirm the gifts and personhood of women and girls, and work for justice and an end to violence.[^6] The notes add practical responses: safe spaces to tell stories, tangible help like shelter and counseling referrals, holding perpetrators accountable, training leaders to respond to disclosures, and ongoing support rather than quick fixes.[^12] Which of these do you think would make the biggest difference in a congregation, and what would it take to begin?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The prayers say that children who witness violence are victims themselves and need protection and love, "understanding that violence is never an expression of love."[^13] Why is that sentence so important for a child to hear? Who in a church community is positioned to say it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The episode insists that "prayer must be accompanied by practical pathways to safety and support."[^7] What is one practical thing you could do this month so that, if someone ever came to you, you would be ready (for example, knowing where the resources are, or learning how abuse works)?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> This week we did not study abuse so much as pray about it. The liturgy holds two things together: a clear word to survivors that what happened is not their fault and not God's punishment, and a clear call to the church to confess its comfort, its silence and its slowness to believe. It asks for repentance from perpetrators and from the systems that should have protected the vulnerable. It is meant to be returned to, because a heart that has been slow to believe is not changed in a single hearing. The hope underneath it all is a church where violence is banished and everyone can flourish safely in God's abundant life.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, name one line, idea or posture from this liturgy that you want to carry into your prayers this week.

</div>
