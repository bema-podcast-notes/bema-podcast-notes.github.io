### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** A second walk through Psalm 23 found God answering sin, fear and scarcity with rest and closeness: a table set in front of our enemies and goodness that chases us down.

**This week:** The Psalms series wraps up with two big ideas: the Psalms free us to bring our whole selves to God, and they always aim that honesty at God.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This episode closes the Psalms series, with Brent, Reed Dent and Elle Grover Fricks (Marty is absent). Reed draws out two main points. First, the Psalms free us from "decorum," so we can speak what we feel rather than what we think we ought to say, and that works in both directions, for lament and for joy. Second, that expression is never just for its own sake, because every psalm is aimed at God. Elle explains why we hold back, why bringing everything to God is an act of trust, and why venting without turning toward truth can backfire.

Elle also gives the academic answer to the series' opening question, "What is poetry?": Hebrew poetry shares structures like parallelism and chiasm with other ancient poetry, it is brief, and it uses special vocabulary. Reed reads from three modern poets who still write in a psalm-like way, and the hosts close with practical ways to keep living with the Psalms. Before all that, they check in on a running joke from the series: did the podcast ever actually explain "life psalms"? (It remains unresolved.)

### Done with Decorum <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#done-with-decorum}

*The Psalms give permission to drop the act, in both directions.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Psalm 13 aloud, then read Psalm 100 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Reed says some people need Psalm 13 ("How long, O Lord?") to admit they doubt, while others need Psalm 100 ("Shout for joy to the Lord") to let themselves celebrate. He admits he is in the second group: he doesn't dance at wedding receptions because unfiltered joy feels exposing[^2]. What do you notice about the two psalms side by side? Which one would be harder for you to pray honestly, and why?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say you can fake feelings in either direction. Gushing, sugary praise can be fake, and so can dramatic, edgy complaining. The goal is sincerity, not provocation[^3]. Where have you seen worship or prayer slip into performance in one direction or the other? What helps you tell the difference in your own prayers?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Elle traces both performing and staying silent to the same root: "a fear of being seen, or a wish to control how we are seen," and sometimes a fear of losing control of ourselves. Bringing it all to God, rather than venting alone, is a statement of trust that God can hear it and work through it with us[^4]. She compares it to marriage: a spouse may read your body language, but the relationship is still stronger when you say plainly how you are doing and why[^5]. What feeling do you most often keep from God, and what would it look like to say it plainly this week?

</div>

### Aimed at God <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#aimed-at-god}

*The Psalms never treat feelings as the final word; they keep turning back to God.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Psalm 8:3–4 aloud, then read Psalm 4 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Reed points out how the Psalms turn experience toward God. Not "nature is cool," but God stretching out the heavens like a tent (Psalm 104). Not "I am sad," but "How long, O Lord?" (Psalm 13). Not abstract thinking about human nature, but "what is mankind that you are mindful of them?" (Psalm 8). Because they speak in first person to "you," praying them as written "forces us to the table" with God[^6]. Look at Psalm 4 again. Where do you see the psalmist speaking about God, and where speaking to God? What changes at the turn in the last verses?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Elle explains that brevity and special vocabulary are marks of Hebrew poetry, and that the Psalms don't follow a fixed prayer formula. She mentions being taught an ordered pattern of thanksgiving, adoration, confession and so on, and notes that overly formal, hedged prayer ("if it be within your sovereign will...") can be as performative as anything else[^7]. Our traditions pray in different ways, some with set forms and some without. Without ranking them, what has your tradition's way of praying given you, and where has it made honesty harder?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Elle warns that every time we retell a story we strengthen it in memory, so "just getting it out of my system" can lock in a harmful story. As a teenager she filled journals with angsty prayers and came away more anxious, because she never got around to reminding herself what was true about God. Psalm 4 complains for several verses and then turns to "In peace I will lie down and sleep."[^8] Share only what you are comfortable sharing. What story have you retold so often that it may be holding you, and what truth about God would you need to preach to yourself to make the turn?

</div>

### Living with the Psalms <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#living-with-the-psalms}

*The psalm-writing tradition is still alive, and there are simple ways to join it.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Reed reads three modern poets who write "psalmically," including Franz Wright, whose poem ends with God answering: "though your own heart condemn you / I do not condemn you." Reed's ministry ends each semester by asking, "What did God see?"[^9] The notes call imagining God's reply a creative practice anyone can use. If God answered the prayer you named in Q3 or Q6, what do you imagine God might say?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The hosts offer practical paths forward: let the Psalms be your prayer life for a season by praying one every day for a month; write your own psalm using Psalm 8 or Psalm 51 as a template, or write a psalm on one half of a page and your response on the other; put psalms up in your home; and let psalms mark key moments, so your Bible becomes a "road map" of where God showed up[^10]. Which of these practices will you try in the coming month, and what psalm will you start with?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> This session's Psalms series has carried us from the refuge of Psalm 91, through Psalms 139, 51 and 32, to the silent heavens of Psalm 19 and the table of Psalm 23, and this final episode gathers it into two things to carry. First, we don't have to perform for God. Whether we are stuck in anger and doubt or can't let ourselves feel joy, there is a psalm that can draw out what we have been holding back, and offering it to God is an act of trust. Second, the Psalms never treat feelings as the final word; they keep pointing us back to God, invite God to answer, and often carry us from complaint to a renewed sense of who God is. As Elle put it, you are never just feeling down alone in your room. You're feeling down "with a great cloud of witnesses."[^11]
{: .guide-summary}

**Closing Question:** Going around the room in one sentence each, with no comments until everyone has spoken: share the psalm line you brought tonight and the season of your life it now marks.

</div>
