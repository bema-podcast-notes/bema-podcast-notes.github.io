### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Under the night sky of Psalm 8 we held two truths together, that we are dust and that the world was made for our sake, and saw that ruling well begins with seeing.[^1]

**This week:** Brent, Reed and Elle work through Psalm 137, a psalm of exile that moves from weeping by the rivers of Babylon to one of the most disturbing curses in Scripture.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Psalm 137 is a psalm of disorientation that never turns to trust. The hosts read it in Elle's translation, which brings out Hebrew wordplay that English versions often lose. They see the psalm making room for communal grief, honoring the role of memory after loss, and letting victims refuse to perform their pain for the people who hurt them.[^3]

The episode then takes on the psalm's closing curse. Drawing on Eugene Peterson, Walter Brueggemann and Miroslav Volf, the hosts argue that hate needs to be prayed rather than suppressed, and that honest accusation may be a necessary first step toward real forgiveness, though never the end of the road.[^4] This session touches grief, abuse and violence. Every question can be answered from what you observe in the text rather than from your own story, and passing is always welcome.

### By the Rivers: Grief and Memory <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#by-the-rivers-grief-and-memory}

*The psalm opens with a community grieving together in a foreign land.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Psalm 137:1–6 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The psalm says "*we* sat and *we* wept," and the notes see this as pushing back on cultures that keep grief private. Reed calls it a kind of "anti-psalm": the people are *there*, not *here*, and silent instead of singing. It also reverses the Song of Moses in Exodus 15, where Israel sings over defeated enemies. In Jewish practice the psalm is traditionally recited before the grace after meals on weekdays, keeping lament in the community's regular rhythm.[^6] What do you notice about the pronouns, places and sounds in these verses? What might it be like for a church to keep lament in its regular rhythm rather than saving it for funerals?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** Elle renders the opening as "over the sparkling rivers poured together." The Hebrew *Bavel* can name Babylon, picture waters flowing together through its canals, and recall Babel and confusion. Trauma, she says, brings a "sparkling confusion" that keeps catching your eye: *How did this happen? Why? This wasn't supposed to be my story.* Brent and Reed singled out her line about the lyres hanging on the willows "as hanged men."[^7] Which of these images lands most strongly for you, and why?

*Read Joshua 4:1–7 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Elle describes several biblical ways of remembering: monument memory, like the stones by the Jordan; grieving and then deliberately moving on, as David did; and a healing kind of forgetting, where the past is woven into your story but no longer sits at the center of your identity. "Ebenezer," stone of help, suggests that remembering helps even when it is sad.[^8] Which of these ways of remembering has helped you, or someone you love, after a loss? Share only what you choose.

</div>

### How Could We Sing? <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#how-could-we-sing}

*The captors demand a song, and the exiles refuse.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Reed sees two kinds of captors: those who ask for a song, perhaps out of curiosity or to ease their own conscience, and those who gloat and demand one for entertainment. Eugene Peterson sees the exiles' silence as dignifying, the one form of agency they have left. The self-curse ("may my right hand forget… may my tongue cling") shows the silence comes from love, not forgetfulness. Elle notes that the verb for "cling," *davaq*, is the same one used when a man clings to his wife in Genesis 2:24.[^9] What does the self-curse tell you about how the exiles feel toward Jerusalem and its songs? Why might silence be a more faithful response than singing here?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Elle reads the refusal through the experience of abuse: people who cause harm often want their victims to reassure them ("I did a good job, right, honey?") or to share their inner world, and the victim can rightly say, *You don't get to experience my inner world with me.* Elle also notes that the Hebrew Bible makes room for at least three responses to suffering: questioning God, blaming the human perpetrator, as Psalm 137 does, and self-examination, often the prophets' role.[^10] Which of those three responses did the churches you grew up in teach or model most? Which was hardest to voice there, and what effect did that have?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Reed notes that hatred can at least identify where the evil is, a necessary first step before any talk of mercy.[^11] How can we honor someone else's refusal to "sing," or to explain their pain on demand, without walking away from them? What might that look like with someone you know?

</div>

### Praying Our Hate <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#praying-our-hate}

*The closing verses give words to rage, and the hosts ask what to do with that.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Psalm 137:7–9 aloud, then Luke 23:34. Sit together in silence for thirty seconds.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The closing "beatitudes" echo Psalm 1 but turn it upside down. Brent finds it disturbing and asks how God could take pleasure in this. Elle points out that the text does not necessarily mean God is the one carrying it out, and she suggests that people without serious adverse experiences may not understand this language, because for victims of severe and recent injustice, it does not feel like justice without real accountability. The hosts call the wish to make perpetrators feel what they feel natural, but not holy and not a place to stay.[^12] What is your honest first reaction to these verses? What do you make of Elle's point about who finds them hardest to understand?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Eugene Peterson writes, "Our hate needs to be prayed, not suppressed." Hate that isn't admitted "can quickly and easily metamorphose into the evil that provokes it," and prayer "doesn't legitimize hate; it utilizes it." Brueggemann pictures God as a kind of therapist: we say these things to God so we do not act on them. Miroslav Volf asks, "Can forgiveness be a mode of coming to terms too easily?" and holds that genuine forgiveness follows genuine articulation of hatred.[^13] Traditions differ on whether anger belongs in prayer at all. How were you taught to handle anger before God? What would change if you took Peterson's line seriously?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The episode does not stop at anger. Elle calls the Rwandan survivors who forgave, and even adopted, their family's killers "insanely glorious," a partnership with the Holy Spirit and not a box ticked out of duty. Two practices are offered: gratitude for who we have become, never toward the perpetrator, as in 2 Corinthians 1 and Joseph's "you meant it for evil," and curiosity about the "who and why" behind those who hurt us, which Reed says turned his anger into compassion without excusing what was done.[^14] Which of these practices seems within reach for you, and which seems far off?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Brent's closing advice is not to process these things alone: find community, and get therapy if you need it. The notes also explain that rehearsing hate alone fixes memories more firmly, while bringing anger to God and processing it in community turns it into a dialogue the Spirit can work in.[^15] Where could you bring something you have been carrying alone, and to whom? You do not need to name it here.

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Psalm 137 does not ask anyone to tidy up their pain. It lets a community sit and weep together, honors the memory of what was lost, and refuses to perform grief for those who caused it. It also says out loud the rage that brutalization produces, and the hosts suggest this honesty is not a lapse in faith but may be where faith has to start: hate that is prayed can be shaped, while hate that is denied can quietly become the evil it hates. From there, slowly and in our own time, gratitude, curiosity and the Spirit's power can move us toward Jesus' prayer, "Father, forgive them."
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, name one thing you will bring to God honestly this week, or simply say "pass."

</div>
