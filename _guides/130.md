### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We read Matthew 26–27 whole, from a Passover meal to a borrowed tomb. Marty admitted the crucifixion is too sacred to be "merely an academic exercise," and we saw crucifixion as Rome's theater of humiliation.[^1]

**This week:** We ask a question that has shaped centuries of Christian history: who killed Jesus? The episode answers carefully, and the answer matters deeply.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode's central claim is that "The Jewish people did not kill Jesus - they loved and revered him, which is why the plot had to happen outside the public eye."[^3] It places responsibility on a small, corrupt group of chief priests, described as seven ruling families operating like a "religious mafia," who used an informal Sanhedrin meeting at the high priest's house and pressured Pilate into the execution.[^4] It also notes that not all Pharisees were involved, and that some had earlier tried to save Jesus' life.[^5]

Marty speaks with gravity about what happens when this story is misread. The false teaching that "the Jews killed Jesus" gave religious cover to persecution, pogroms, and genocide, and the notes say "The Holocaust cannot be separated from centuries of Christian anti-Semitism."[^6] Today's conversation will ask how we read these chapters, how we may have heard them taught, and how we talk about Jesus' own people when we tell this story.

### A Trial in the High Priest's House <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#a-trial-in-the-high-priests-house}

*Where the trial happened tells us a great deal about who held the power.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 26:57–68 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Look at where this trial takes place, who is gathered, what kind of testimony they look for, and where Peter is sitting. What do you notice about the setting and the process?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes distinguish two Sanhedrins. The formal one had 70–72 leaders, half Pharisees and half Sadducees, and met with established protocols. The informal one was made up of the chief priests, run by the high priest, and met at his house, where it "Operated like a mafia with backroom deals" and "Made decisions that the formal Sanhedrin simply ratified." This, the notes say, "is where Jesus was actually tried," and the trial broke more than 15 Jewish commandments about proper legal proceedings.[^7] How does this change your picture of the night Jesus was arrested? What does it suggest about whether Jewish law itself, or a corrupt few who ignored it, put Jesus on trial?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes trace how the chief priesthood, originally hereditary through Aaron's line, became purchasable, so that Herod the Great sold it to the highest bidder and seven wealthy families came to control it.[^8] They observe that "Jesus survived three years confronting the Pharisees theologically but lasted less than one week confronting the corrupt power of the chief priests," and conclude that theological disagreement "can coexist with mutual respect and ongoing dialogue" while "Corrupt power, however, eliminates threats rather than engaging them."[^9] Why is corrupt power so much more dangerous than strong disagreement? Where do you see that difference today?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q4:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say this informal power structure "mirrors many modern power dynamics," including "Religious denominations where unofficial power brokers control decisions despite official structures," and they warn about church structures that "protect abusers or maintain injustice to avoid scandal or loss of influence."[^10] This is not a verdict on any of our churches. In the communities you belong to, how can ordinary members tell when formal structures are serving people and when they are only ratifying decisions made elsewhere?

</div>

### Pilate in a Corner <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#pilate-in-a-corner}

*Rome held the power of the cross, but Rome was pushed.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read John 19:12–16 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes describe Pilate as one of Rome's best governors, in Jerusalem for Passover to keep order, who "Repeatedly tried to find charges against Jesus and failed" and did not see a peasant rabbi teaching forgiveness as a real threat.[^11] The chief priests pressed him with the loaded line "If you let this man go, you are no friend of Caesar."[^12] Read the passage again slowly. What pressures do you hear being applied to Pilate, and what choices was he weighing?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes say crucifixion was reserved for Zealots, and the chief priests "didn't just want Jesus dead - they wanted him publicly branded as a Zealot." They could have stoned him, but crucifixion would discredit his movement by associating him with violent revolution. The notes add that the two "robbers" crucified with Jesus were actually Zealots, and that one of them "gets it," seeing that his violent agenda had failed while Jesus' kingdom represented truth.[^13] Jesus' kingdom was, in the notes' words, a war "against Empire as a system, not Rome as a political entity."[^14] How does knowing the message behind the cross sharpen your sense of what Jesus' kingdom was, and was not?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say Pilate's hand-washing "shows the human tendency to seek absolution through gestures while taking the pragmatic path that compromises integrity."[^15] Where are you most tempted to make a gesture that lets you feel innocent while taking the easier path?

</div>

### Who Killed Jesus? <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#who-killed-jesus}

*The text itself draws a line between the people and the powerful few.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 26:3–5 aloud, then read Matthew 27:1–2 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes say the plot "required secrecy specifically because the Jewish public would have opposed it," that every earlier attempt to arrest Jesus failed "because the people protected him," and that Matthew 27:1 identifies "the chief priests and elders" as the ones who plotted.[^16] They also argue that the crowds who welcomed Jesus and the crowd that called for crucifixion were different groups.[^17] Look at who is named in these verses and what they fear. What do you notice about the relationship between the leaders and the people?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes say "Millions of Jews have been murdered throughout history based on this false interpretation," and that the narrative "ignored the text's clear distinction between the Jewish people and the corrupt leadership."[^18] Many of us absorbed some version of "the Jews killed Jesus" without anyone stating it outright, through sermons, passion plays, hymns, or simply the way the story was told. Without blaming anyone, how did you first hear this part of the story told, and what, if anything, needs to be unlearned?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode closes with an Eastern Church tradition that Pilate and his wife converted, fled to Egypt, and founded the Coptic Church. Marty acknowledges it as tradition rather than proven history but finds it "deeply hopeful," a sign of "the possibility of transformation even for those complicit in Jesus's death."[^19] Why might such a hope matter, even if we cannot confirm the story?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q11:** The notes call for "historical honesty that properly understands the past to prevent repeating its most destructive errors."[^20] Jesus, his disciples, and the crowds who loved him were Jewish. When you next read or hear the passion story, or tell it to a child, a friend, or a class, what will you do differently in how you speak about the people involved?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Jesus was not killed by a people. The crowds loved him, and the plot had to be hatched at night, at the high priest's house, away from them. A small group of chief priests, who had bought their power and would do anything to keep it, used an informal court, broke their own law, and pushed a reluctant governor into a corner. They demanded the cross to brand Jesus a Zealot, yet even a Zealot dying beside him could see he was not one. Misreading this story has cost millions of Jewish lives. Reading it honestly is part of following the Jewish rabbi who died there.[^21]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one thing you will carry from this conversation into the way you read or tell the story of Jesus' death?

</div>

**[Ep. 131 — Betrayal](/guides/131)** (30:50): The episode looks at the betrayals, plural, of Jesus' final night. It explores Judas as a likely Zealot from Keriot who may have believed he was forcing Jesus' hand, and argues that from a rabbinical perspective Peter's public denial of his rabbi was the deeper betrayal. It closes on second chances and the difference between a story that ends in despair and one that allows a new tomorrow.[^22]
