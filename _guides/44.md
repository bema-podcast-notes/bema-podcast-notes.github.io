### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Naaman's new faith was real and incomplete, and Elisha sent him home with "Go in peace," trusting God's patience with a messy journey.

**This week:** We step out of the story of the kings and into Israel's songbook. The Psalms are tools for the journey, songs that give God's people words for every season, including grief and rage.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode calls the Psalms the "pop music" of ancient Hebrew culture: songs meant to be sung together, with "a Psalm for that" no matter what you are feeling. The Psalms of Ascent (Psalms 120–134), sung on the road to Jerusalem, hold both distress and celebration in one pilgrim songbook. The imprecatory Psalms, which call down vengeance on enemies, give voice to anger so that we hand vengeance over to God rather than taking it ourselves. A close reading of Psalm 109 suggests such a song can even turn the singer toward self-examination.

The hosts also notice that much modern worship leaves little room for lament, and Marty tells of a grieving family whose worship in their loss sustained him for years. The title comes from Psalm 137, when the exiles in Babylon hung their harps in the trees, having nothing left to sing. Our traditions worship in different ways, so this is a good week to listen to one another's experience of song.

### There's a Psalm for That <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#theres-a-psalm-for-that}

*Israel's songbook makes room for every mood a pilgrim might carry.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes say the Psalms functioned as "pop music," and that "when people are struggling, they don't seek comfort in theological propositions or church doctrinal statements. Instead, they turn to music, poetry, and contemplative practices."[^1] The hosts prefer to call this part of Scripture "tools for the journey."[^2] Does that match your experience? What do you reach for first when life falls apart?

*Read Psalm 120 aloud, then read Psalm 128 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** Both of these are Psalms of Ascent, songs for pilgrimage to Jerusalem. The notes observe that "Some years the journey to Jerusalem would be joyful, other years filled with despair or frustration, and the Psalms of Ascent provided appropriate songs for each mood."[^3] What do you notice about the feelings, the images, and the picture of God in each song? What does it say that both belong in the same pilgrim songbook?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes conclude that "authentic worship doesn't require pretending to feel a certain way but rather expressing truth to God regardless of emotional state."[^4] If you were walking to Jerusalem this year, would your song sound more like Psalm 120 or Psalm 128? What would one honest line of it say?

</div>

### Songs of Vengeance <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#songs-of-vengeance}

*The hardest Psalms may be doing quiet, transforming work in the people who sing them.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Psalm 109:1–20 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Listen carefully to the pronouns. The notes point out that verses 1–5 speak of enemies (plural), while verses 6–19 call for judgment on "my enemy" (singular).[^5] What do you notice when the voice shifts? How does the middle section feel different from the opening?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes say verse 20 can be translated two ways: "May this be the LORD's payment to my accusers" or "This is what my accusers want Adonai to do," the second supported by David Stern's Complete Jewish Bible. On that reading, the middle section may be the Psalmist quoting what his accusers say about him.[^6] The episode connects this to David's near-revenge on Nabal, stopped by Abigail in 1 Samuel 25.[^7] If this reading is right, what kind of journey might a person take by singing this Psalm over and over?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes list three purposes for imprecatory Psalms: "Authentic Expression," "Preventing Violence" (because "leave vengeance to me," says the Lord, in Romans 12:19), and "Transformative Reflection." They also admit the episode "doesn't fully address the ethical questions they raise," such as how these Psalms relate to Jesus' teaching to love enemies.[^8] People of faith have answered that question differently. What do you think is gained, and what is risked, by bringing anger like this to God in prayer?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes say Psalm 109 "moves from external anger to internal reflection - a sophisticated emotional and spiritual technology," leading a singer to ask, "What would my enemies say about me? Do they have a valid point?"[^9] Think of someone you are at odds with. If that person wrote a song about you, what might be fair in it? You may answer this one privately.

</div>

### Singing for One Another <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#singing-for-one-another}

*Sometimes we sing for others, and sometimes others sing for us.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Marty describes a family from his church who had just lost their 20-something son. The songs that Sunday were all "God is good, God is awesome," with no space for grief, yet the family closed their eyes, tears streaming, and lifted their hands. Watching them taught him: "if they can praise God in their grief, I'm going to be okay."[^10] The notes say modern church culture "has largely eliminated space for lament."[^11] Our traditions worship in different ways. Where, in the worship you know best, is there room for lament, and where is it missing?

*Read Psalm 137:1–4 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes call this "one of the greatest, most tragic moments in biblical history," when God's people had "nothing to sing about and nothing to sing for."[^12] What do you notice about where the exiles are, what they are asked to do, and why they cannot? What makes this moment more tragic than other losses in Israel's story?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes describe singing as a spiritual discipline: "Sometimes when attending worship, you sing not because you feel like it, but to bear witness for others who are struggling," and in seasons of despair, "watching others sing with authentic faith can sustain your own faith."[^13] Who might you sing for this week? Or who has been singing for you without knowing it?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The Psalms were Israel's songs for the road: songs of joy and songs of distress, songs of praise and songs of rage. They show us that God can handle the whole truth of what we feel, and that even a song of vengeance can turn us, over time, toward honesty about ourselves. They remind us that we sing for one another, carrying each other when faith runs thin. The saddest moment in the story is not defeat but silence, when God's people hung their harps in the trees. The hope of this episode is that we will always find our harps, pull them down, and bring God whatever worship we need to bring.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what song (or what kind of song) do you need to bring to God this week?

</div>
