### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** At Mary's Sabbath table, Jesus took "Elijah's seat" among people the religious world had excluded and let Mary lead in her own home, while honored rabbis competed for seats at Nicodemus's dinner.[^1]

**This week:** Before he gathers any adult disciples, Jesus spends his days building, camping and teaching a group of bold, curious children.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This episode is creative backstory that the Gospels do not narrate, and the hosts use it to reflect on three things. First, the full humanity of Jesus: he struggles to light a fire, sweats, washes his feet, bandages a wound and prays a bedtime blessing. Second, childlike faith: the children, especially Abigail, ask direct questions without pretense, and Jesus wonders aloud whether his future adult disciples will understand as well as they do.[^2]

Third, a tension the hosts feel in their own ministry: Jesus tells the children to honor their teacher Rabbi Josiah, then adds that "smart men lack wisdom." The episode also shows Jesus naming a quiet boy "Joshua the brave" at his first small act of courage, and it includes a line about vocation that the hosts have mixed feelings about.[^3]

### A Jesus Who Sweats <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#a-jesus-who-sweats}

*The episode insists, through small physical details, that Jesus was fully human.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes list the ordinary moments the episode lingers on: Jesus struggling to build a fire in humid conditions, sweating from the effort, washing his feet after a long day, bandaging a wound on his arm, and waking up disoriented when the children surprise him. The hosts even discuss debates over whether Jesus could have hit his thumb with a hammer.[^4] Which of these details surprised you, or would surprise people in your church? Why do you think ordinary scenes like these feel unusual in portrayals of Jesus?

*Read Hebrews 4:15 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Among its questions for further study, the notes ask what "tempted in every way as we are" means for Jesus's human experience, and they describe the show's portrayal not as weakness but as "the profound reality of God entering into complete solidarity with human existence."[^5] What words in this verse stand out to you? How does a Jesus who gets tired and hurt change the way you hear it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** As he lies down to sleep, Jesus prays: "Blessed are you Lord our God, King of the universe, who brings sleep to my eyes." The notes explain that Jewish practice has blessings for waking, sleeping, eating and much more, so that spirituality "permeates all of ordinary life rather than being compartmentalized."[^6] What ordinary moment in your day (waking, a commute, a meal, lying down) could carry a short blessing? What would you want it to say?

</div>

### What the Children Understand <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#what-the-children-understand}

*The children's faith is simple, but the episode suggests it may be closer to what Jesus wants than ours is.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Deuteronomy 6:4–9 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** In one scene Jesus hears the children reciting the Shema, which the notes describe as "God hearing his own words spoken back to him by his people."[^7] As you listen to the passage, notice who is speaking, who is listening, and where these words are supposed to live. What in the passage itself makes that moment in the episode land?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes name five marks of the children's faith: boldness (Abigail asks Jesus about his finances), curiosity, lack of pretense, receptiveness, and a lack of accumulated preconceptions. The hosts say this quality gets "beat out of us" as we mature in religious settings, leaving a more "prim and proper" faith.[^8] Which of these marks do you see in children you know? Which do you think fades first in adults, and what fades it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Near the end of his time with the children, Jesus becomes emotional and says of his future disciples: "I hope that they will ask the questions that you do and listen to me like you have done, but I suspect they won't have the understanding that you do." The notes call the irony sharp: "those with more religious education and scriptural knowledge will actually understand less than these children."[^9] How can more knowledge make understanding harder? Where has your own learning helped you hear Jesus, and where might it have gotten in the way?

</div>

### Honor and Wisdom <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#honor-and-wisdom}

*Jesus honors teachers and still calls the children to a wisdom that intelligence alone cannot supply.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** When the children repeat Rabbi Josiah's teaching about the Messiah, Jesus first affirms honoring teachers and elders, with "no chip on his shoulder." Then he says: "Rabbi Josiah is a smart man. At many times, smart men lack wisdom."[^10] The hosts describe the goal as avoiding "both dismissive rebellion and uncritical acceptance."[^11] What do you think is the difference between being smart and being wise in this scene? What does it look like to honor a teacher you think has missed something?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Jesus tells the children, "Everyone has a much larger job than just their trade." The hosts have mixed feelings: the line could reinforce a sacred/secular divide that their ministry works against by teaching "the sanctity of vocation," or it could affirm that everyone takes part in God's larger purposes.[^12] How do you hear the line when you apply it to your own work: as something that shrinks your daily job or something that enlarges it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** When Joshua, silent and uncomfortable all day, finally asks a question, Jesus immediately names him "Joshua the brave." The notes stress that this happens at his first act of courage, "calling forth identity rather than waiting for it to fully manifest," and they add that this "doesn't mean false flattery but rather recognizing the true identity emerging beneath current limitations."[^13] Has someone ever named something in you before you fully lived it? What did it do? Whose emerging courage, generosity or faith could you name out loud this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> This episode shows us a Jesus who sweats over a stubborn fire, prays before sleep, and spends his days with children who ask whatever they want. Their faith is bold, curious and unpretentious, and Jesus suspects his future disciples will understand less than they do. He honors their teacher while warning that smart men can lack wisdom, and he calls a timid boy brave at the first sign of courage. The notes put the invitation plainly: we are to "recover rather than transcend" the faith of children.[^14]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one question you want to start asking Jesus again?

</div>
