### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** The bowls of wrath poured out the plagues of Egypt again, and readers who knew the Exodus would have heard deliverance rather than terror. In the middle of it all came a blessing on the faithful dead: their deeds will follow them.

**This week:** John announces the fall of Babylon, the city on seven hills, while Rome is at the height of its power. He calls God's people to come out of her, and then shows the rider on the white horse, the binding of the dragon and the books opened before the throne.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The notes call Revelation 17–18 "a funeral dirge for the mightiest empire in the world," written while that empire was still at its peak.[^1] Babylon, the great prostitute seated on seven hills, is Rome, and through Rome every empire built on oppression, materialism and false security.[^2] The long economic lament of chapter 18, drawn from Ezekiel's lament over Tyre, stings modern readers, and Marty admits, "I'm not sure we would pick the right side of this imperial showdown."[^3]

The episode then moves through chapters 19–20: the rider whose sword is the word of God, a battle that is over almost before it starts, and the much-debated thousand years. Marty argues this "pretty small reference" should not carry entire end-times systems and that its point is vindication for the faithful.[^4] The final judgment closes with the assurance that "Not one name is forgotten and everything is put in its appropriate place."[^5]

### Babylon on Seven Hills <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#babylon-on-seven-hills}

*John names the target of his vision almost openly, and the notes stress how dangerous and audacious that was.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Revelation 17:1–9 and 17:15–18 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** List everything you notice about how the woman is dressed, what she holds and what she is drunk on. Then look at verse 9. The notes say "the seven heads are seven hills on which the woman sits" was a clear reference to Rome, the city famously built on seven hills: "not subtle coding" but "a direct and dangerous claim."[^6] What does John's portrait suggest about how Rome looked to the people it persecuted?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes say Augustus ushered in the Pax Romana with eighty-four years of uninterrupted economic growth, so announcing its fall "would have seemed absurd to average listeners."[^7] In verses 16–17 the beast turns on the woman. Marty comments: "Rome itself attacks itself. Rome ends up attacking, devouring its own empire. That's how it works."[^8] Why might it have strengthened persecuted believers to hear that empire carries the seeds of its own destruction?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty repeatedly emphasizes John's audacity in proclaiming the fall of an empire "that at this point in history, nobody would see coming."[^9] What system or power in your world seems too big to fall, and what would it take to see it the way John saw Rome?

</div>

### Come Out of Her <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#come-out-of-her}

*Chapter 18 is a long lament over Babylon's wealth, and the call at its center is addressed to God's own people.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Revelation 18:1–5 and 18:9–19 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Look closely at the list of goods in verses 11–13. What categories of things are there, and what is the last item on the list? The notes say the catalog shows that empire touches every aspect of life, highlights the injustice at the heart of the system by ending with human beings sold as slaves, and echoes Ezekiel 27's lament over Tyre.[^10] Marty encourages reading Ezekiel 27 to see "how much time Ezekiel took in proclaiming the economic ramifications of Tyre's arrogance."[^11] What does it tell you that the merchants are the ones weeping?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty admits: "This is one of those passages that stings a little to read in our culture if we're honest. Not only do we cry persecution in places where those true martyrs would bark at our struggle. I'm not sure we would pick the right side of this imperial showdown."[^12] He also observes that "We put so much of our own stock, no pun intended, in the stability of our economy and its ability to provide us with security."[^13] Where, if anywhere, does this passage sting for you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** "Come out of her, my people" (Revelation 18:4) echoes Isaiah 48 and Jeremiah 50. Read Jeremiah 50:8. The notes describe four layers to the call: physical separation, spiritual disengagement from empire's injustice, economic non-participation in exploitative systems, and ideological rejection of the story that empire provides ultimate security.[^14] Marty puts it vividly: "Maybe there is a mark on our forehead, that we'd like to head to the restroom and start scrubbing off."[^15] Which of the four layers do you think is most needed in your life, and what is one concrete step toward it?

</div>

### The Last Word <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#the-last-word}

*The final battle is strangely brief, and the most debated passage in the book turns out to be about vindication.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Revelation 19:11–16 and 20:4–6 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Notice the rider's names and the weapon he carries. The notes connect the sword from his mouth to Paul's "sword of the Spirit, which is the word of God," and say Jewish readers would hear Torah, God's word, getting the final say: "Torah is getting the last word. It's what they always craved. It's what they always longed for."[^16] The battle itself is over almost instantly, which the notes take to mean that judgment comes through God's word, "not through human violence or military might."[^17] What do you make of a victory won by a word rather than a weapon?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** On the thousand years, Marty observes that "it's a pretty small reference" to carry the weight of whole systems, and that "numbers should make us think more like an Easterner and less like a mathematician." He reads the thousand years as "a Jewish equivalent of talking about an era or an epoch," whose real point is vindication: those who refused the beast "get to reign with Christ."[^18] This draws on the Jewish hope, rooted in Daniel, that the righteous who died unjustly would be raised to help restore the world.[^19] People in this room may hold premillennial, postmillennial or amillennial views, or have never settled on one. Without trying to decide between them, what has your tradition taught about Revelation 20, and what does the emphasis on vindication offer, whatever view you hold?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> At the great white throne, the sea, death and Hades all give up their dead, and the notes sum up John's point: "Everything is being made right. Not one name is forgotten and everything is put in its appropriate place."[^20] Read Revelation 20:11–13. Whose name, forgotten by the world, are you glad God does not forget, and how could you honor that person this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> John wrote a funeral dirge for Rome while Rome was at its height, naming the city on seven hills and mourning a wealth built on everything from gold to human beings. At the center of the lament is an invitation to God's own people: come out of her. The battle that follows is over in a moment, won by the word from the rider's mouth, and the thousand years tell the faithful who refused the beast that they will be vindicated. God's word gets the last say, not one name is forgotten, and everything is put in its appropriate place.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one way you want to "come out" of empire this week?

</div>
