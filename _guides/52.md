### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** First Isaiah rejected Judah's correct but unjust worship and pictured God's people as a terraced vineyard, where no one can see their own wall and every terrace depends on the others[^1].

**This week:** We turn to a book that speaks into the aftermath of Assyria's conquest of the north, asking why God has not destroyed Israel's enemies, and answering with a backwards story about potential[^2].

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The northern kingdom ignored the warnings of Amos and Hosea and fell to Assyria; Judah, under Hezekiah, listened. The episode places Jonah in this setting: a book for a conquered people asking, "Why hasn't God destroyed our enemies?"[^3] Rather than dwelling on the fish, it reads Jonah as a deliberately backwards story. The prophet runs while pagan sailors worship; a one-line sermon brings a whole city to repentance; the "hero" is angry while the "villains" show faith[^4].

The episode draws on Rabbi David Fohrman's warning about the "lullaby effect," where familiar stories lose their strangeness, and on his reading of the book's structure, which puts repentance at the center[^5]. It notices what Jonah leaves out when he quotes God's character from Exodus 34, how the word *gadol* ("great") is used for everything except God's judgment, and a midrash about the vine at the end. It closes with the reason Jonah is read on Yom Kippur: God keeps forgiving because he sees potential[^6].

### Everything Is Backwards <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#everything-is-backwards}

*Familiar stories hide their strangeness; this one is built on surprises.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Jonah 3:1–10 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes call Jonah the "worst prophet." Other prophets bring poetry, imagery and acted-out signs, but Jonah's entire sermon is one line[^7]. What do you notice about the length of his message, and about who responds, how quickly and how completely? Point to the details that surprise you.

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes list the book's reversals: the prophet disobeys, pagan sailors worship God while Jonah wants to die, Ninevites repent at a terrible sermon while Israel ignored beautiful ones, the king of Nineveh leads repentance where Pharaoh hardened his heart, and God sends judgment on his prophet while sparing the enemy city[^4]. Which reversal strikes you most, and why might the author have built the whole book backwards?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes stress that Nineveh was the capital of Assyria, the empire that had just destroyed the northern kingdom through brutal conquest, so God's mercy toward it would have felt offensive to Israelite readers[^8]. When the episode was recorded in 2017, the hosts named enemies of that moment and asked, "Who are the 'Ninevites' today?"[^9] Without naming anyone in this room, who is a "Nineveh" you would find it hard to want God to spare, and what makes that so hard?

</div>

### Dove, Son of Truth <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#dove-son-of-truth}

*Jonah quotes God's own words back to him and leaves something out.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Exodus 34:6–7 aloud, then Jonah 4:1–3.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes say that when Jonah quotes God's self-description from Exodus 34, he omits one word: "faithfulness" (*emet*, often translated "truth")[^10]. Compare the two passages. What does Jonah include, what does he skip, and what does his complaint seem to be about?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes observe that *gadol* ("great") is used for the city, the wind, the fish and the sailors' fear, but not for the east wind of judgment that strikes Jonah in chapter 4. Everything is great except divine wrath[^11]. What does that pattern suggest about how the book wants us to see God?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Jonah's name means "dove" and his father Amittai's name means "truth" or "faithfulness," so he is "Dove, son of Truth." The notes read Jonah's omission as a complaint: if God really cared about truth, he would destroy their enemies[^10]. The notes also say the book is about Israel itself, since a nation angry that God spared its enemies may fail to see its own sin[^12]. When have you shared Jonah's complaint, wanting mercy for yourself and judgment for someone else, and what did it take to see your own need for mercy?

</div>

### Should I Not Care? <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#should-i-not-care}

*The book ends with a question, not an answer.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Jonah 4:5–11 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Jonah sits east of the city (east being the direction of judgment) to watch. God grows a vine over his shelter, then takes it away. The notes report a midrash that the vine had 153 leaves, a number that came to symbolize the pagan nations, and read it as a reminder that Israel was blessed "to be a blessing"[^13]. The book ends on God's open question about the great city[^14]. What do you notice about how Jonah feels about the vine compared with how he feels about Nineveh? Why might the book end without telling us Jonah's answer?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Jonah is read on Yom Kippur. The notes say that when someone asks why God keeps forgiving them year after year, Jonah's answer is "because you have potential," and repentance is what unlocks it[^6]. Whose potential, including your own, do you find hardest to believe in right now, and what would it look like to see that person the way God sees Nineveh?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Jonah is the strangest prophet in the collection: running, sulking and preaching a one-line sermon, while sailors pray and an enemy city repents in sackcloth. Written for a people who had suffered at Assyria's hands, it dares to say that God sees potential even in Nineveh. It also holds up a mirror, because a people angry at God's mercy for others may forget how much mercy they need. The book ends with God's question still hanging in the air, waiting for our answer.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, how would you answer God's last question to Jonah if he asked it of you this week?

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 53 — Nahum — Diyn](/guides/53)** (31:04): Nahum is the counterbalance to Jonah. Written to an oppressed Israel after the Assyrian conquest, it announces God's judgment on Nineveh for its persistent cruelty and introduces *diyn*, judgment with finality, alongside the restorative justice we have been learning about[^15]. Holding Jonah and Nahum together will be the work of next week.

**What to bring next week:** Read Nahum 1:1–8. Underline every phrase that describes God's patience and circle every phrase that describes God acting.
