### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** In Colossians, Paul answered a philosophy that despised the physical world by insisting that God's fullness lived in Christ's body and that "all things" are reconciled through him, so the Kingdom is engaged with "this world of flesh and blood, soil and spirit."[^1]

**This week:** We read Paul's best-known passage about Christ's return through a practice everyone in Thessalonica would have recognized: the emperor's *parousia*.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

In the earthquake-prone Roman Empire, the *parousia* was a familiar imperial event. After a disaster, the emperor visited to survey the damage, left a large deposit for reconstruction, and everyone knew he would come back to inspect what had been done with it. Thessalonica experienced at least two such visits in the first century.[^2] The episode shows how Paul's description of Christ's return in 1 Thessalonians 4:16–18 follows the same pattern: a trumpet, honor for the dead, and citizens going out to meet the arriving ruler and escort him back into the city.[^3]

From there, the episode argues that the passage calls Christians to active, accountable work in the world, and it challenges "rapture theology" for repeating the Thessalonians' own mistake of disengaging from that work.[^4] People in this room may have been taught this passage in quite different ways. Our goal is to understand what each of us received and what the *parousia* background adds, not to win an argument about the end times.

**Opening Question:** Think of a time someone trusted you with something (money, a project, a home, a child) and then came back to see how it had gone. What did that return feel like as it approached?

### What Everyone in Thessalonica Knew <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#what-everyone-in-thessalonica-knew}

*Before we read Paul, we picture the imperial event his first readers had watched with their own eyes.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes describe the pattern of a Roman *parousia*. Lookouts sounded trumpets when the emperor's entourage was spotted. Entering the city, he first honored the dead at the necropolis near the gates. Then came the *eis apantesien* ("going out to meet"): citizens went out from the city to meet him "with joy, then escorting him back into the city to show him their accomplishments." Finally he inspected how his deposit had been used, ideally finding improvements "beyond what existed before the disaster."[^5] If you lived in a city that had received such a deposit, how would you spend the years between his first visit and his return?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** *Read 1 Thessalonians 4:13–18 aloud.* Line the passage up against the four parts of the *parousia*: trumpet, honoring the dead, going out to meet, returning together. Which parts can you find in Paul's words, and where? The notes say the passage follows "the exact pattern of a Roman *parousia*."[^6]

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes draw attention to direction: "Christ's return is described as coming 'down' while believers go 'up' to meet Him," and then they "return together to earth."[^7] When citizens went out to meet the emperor, where did they all end up? How does that change the picture of where the story finishes?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The emperor's deposit was "understood as a loan/investment requiring accountability," and the notes say Christians have been given a "deposit" in the Holy Spirit and "will need to show Jesus what they accomplished with it."[^8] What has been deposited in your life (gifts, resources, relationships, the Spirit's empowerment), and does the thought of showing it to Jesus feel more like dread or more like eagerness to you right now?

</div>

### Escape or Engagement? <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#escape-or-engagement}

*The episode argues that some popular readings of this passage repeat the very problem Paul was addressing.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** According to the notes, some in the Thessalonian church believed Christ's return was "so imminent that physical work became pointless," and members were "quitting their jobs and vocations" and "expecting immediate evacuation from earthly concerns."[^9] *Read 1 Thessalonians 4:9–12 aloud.* What do you notice Paul saying about daily life and work just before he describes Christ's return? Why might he have put those two things side by side?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say that using this passage to support "rapture" theology "commits the same error as the Thessalonians: disengagement from worldly work," and that the passage teaches "physical participation" rather than "disembodied evacuation."[^10] Look at the sentence or two you wrote about how you were taught this passage, or think back to it now. What did that teaching emphasize, and how did it shape the way you or your church related to the world? What does the *parousia* reading highlight that your earlier teaching did or did not?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** *Read Matthew 6:9–10 aloud.* The notes connect the *parousia* to Jesus' prayer, "Your will be done on earth as it is in heaven," calling it "an active request for present transformation, not future escape."[^11] When you pray those words, what are you actually asking for? What would change in your week if you prayed them as a request to take part in the work?

</div>

### Something to Show Him <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#something-to-show-him}

*The episode ends with a picture of joyful accountability.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The episode shares Ray Vander Laan's story of American visitors who left a monetary "deposit" with the mayor of a Turkish village. Years later they returned to find the village had become the top-ranked school district in Turkey, "with the mayor eagerly showing them the accomplishments."[^12] What makes the difference between an inspection you fear and one you run across a field to welcome?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes link this to *tikkun olam*, the Jewish idea of "repairing the world," and describe Christians as partners in making creation "more ordered, more shalom-filled, more holy" while awaiting Christ's return.[^13] Where in our town or our church do you see something broken that a few of us could help repair?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** Picture Christ's return the way the Thessalonians would have pictured the emperor's. Name one concrete thing you want to be able to show him, something you could begin or continue this month with what he has deposited in you.

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The people of Thessalonica knew what a *parousia* looked like: a trumpet, honor paid to the dead, a city pouring out to meet its ruler and leading him back in to show him what they had built with his gift. Paul used that picture to describe Christ's return. The point was never to abandon the city but to have something to show when he arrives. We have been given a deposit, the Spirit, and a prayer that God's will be done on earth. The time between his first coming and his return is the time for rebuilding.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one thing you want to have to show Jesus, and what is your first step toward it?

</div>
