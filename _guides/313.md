### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Reed opened the parables series by describing a parable as "something thrown alongside," a story that comes at us slant and invites us to live in it like a house with windows rather than dissect it like a bird.[^1]

**This week:** Brent, Reed, and Elle (Marty is not on this episode) read the Good Samaritan as Jesus' answer to a Torah expert who asks how to inherit eternal life and then asks, "And who is my neighbor?"[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This is the first full parable discussion in the series and the first time two BEMA co-hosts have recorded without Marty.[^2] Drawing on Kenneth Bailey's cultural insights and Elle's knowledge of Hebrew and Jewish tradition, the hosts resist readings that make the priest and Levite simple villains, and they explain why a Samaritan hero would have shocked Jesus' first listeners. Elle uses the idea of a "near enemy" to make that shock land today.[^3]

The episode centers on two observations. The first is how close each traveler's feet carry him to the wounded man. The second is the Greek word *splagchnizomai*, gut-deep compassion, which in the Gospels belongs only to Jesus or to a God figure and always leads to rescue.[^4]

**Opening Question:** Reed admits he rarely stops for cars with their hazard lights on, even though it would cost him little more than running late.[^5] Think of a time you stopped to help a stranger, or a stranger stopped for you. What made the difference in that moment?

### A Torah Expert's Question <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#a-torah-experts-question}

*A teacher of Torah asks Jesus how to live the life of God's kingdom, and Jesus answers with a question of his own.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Luke 10:25–29 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The man is a *nomikos*, an expert in Torah. Bailey notes that standing was a student's sign of respect for a teacher, yet this student is testing Jesus.[^6] The hosts offer two readings: a test of whether Jesus' fast-growing ministry is built on sound teaching, or the normal back-and-forth between a student and a rabbi, much like checking a church's doctrinal statement on its website.[^6] What do you notice about how the conversation unfolds in these verses? Which reading seems more likely to you, and why?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> In the episode, "eternal life" is not mainly about how long life lasts or going to heaven after death. It is a quality of life, the life of *Olam Haba* ("the world to come"), so the question amounts to "How can I live in the next world's reality now?"[^7] Elle adds that an inheritance is a gift by definition, which makes "What must I *do* to inherit?" a bit of a paradox.[^7] How does hearing the question this way change what the lawyer is asking, and what we mean when we use the phrase?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The hosts suggest two motives behind "Who is my neighbor?" Elle wonders whether the lawyer is "auditioning," wanting to impress Jesus, something she remembers from her own past. Reed wonders whether he is trying to fence in a command Jesus applies without limit.[^8] As Elle puts it, "If we get too jacked up on which one's correct, we might miss the point."[^8] Which motive exposes more of your own blind spot: the wish to be noticed, or the wish to draw a boundary around whom you have to love?

</div>

### A Priest, a Levite, and a Samaritan <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#a-priest-a-levite-and-a-samaritan}

*The hosts resist caricaturing the first two travelers and explain why the third would have shocked Jesus' audience.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Luke 10:30–35 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The road from Jerusalem to Jericho is steep and full of switchbacks, so travelers would have seen the man from far above and kept seeing him after they passed.[^9] Bailey points out that people placed strangers by dialect and dress, and this man is naked and unconscious, so he offers neither.[^9] What do you notice about what Jesus tells us, and leaves out, about the wounded man? Why might that matter for a question about who counts as a neighbor?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Reed admits he was raised to look down on priests, Levites, and Pharisees, and Elle warns that reflexive caricatures of Jewish figures can slide into antisemitism.[^10] Elle notes that ritual impurity was a routine part of life, that Jericho had an unusually large number of *mikvehs* (ritual baths), and that *pikuach nefesh* ("saving a human life") overrides other commandments.[^10] The notes conclude that Jesus' audience would have felt both sides, the same internal argument we have when deciding whether to stop.[^10] What real concerns tend to win that argument for you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Elle connects "Samaritan" to the Hebrew root *shamar*, "to guard." Samaritans saw themselves as guardians of the original faith, accepting only the Torah and rejecting the "fences" Jewish teachers built around it, so the conflict was something like a fight between denominations where each side believed it had God's word right.[^11] Elle calls this a "near enemy," harder to love than a distant, theoretical one.[^11] Reed suggests that today the third traveler would be someone who claims the name "Christian," seems to embody everything wrong with the world, and is the one actually living out Jesus' way.[^11] Every tradition in this room has had its own near enemies. Without naming anyone, why is a near enemy harder to love than a distant one, and what would it mean to see that person as the hero of Jesus' story?

</div>

### Where Their Feet Take Them <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#where-their-feet-take-them}

*Reed's key observation is how close each traveler comes, and the word for the Samaritan's compassion points to who he portrays.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Luke 10:31–34 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Reed calls this the main thing about the parable for him. The priest "passed by on the other side" before getting near; the Levite "came to the place" and still crossed; the Samaritan came to where the man actually was.[^12] Reed compares the first to a "30,000-foot" response built on proof texts, the second to a well-researched but theoretical opinion formed without meeting the person, and the third to getting close enough to the "stink and the mess" to see a real human being.[^12] Trace the three travelers' movement in the text. Which of the three responses do you most recognize in yourself when a hard issue involves real people?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> *Splagchnizomai* comes from *splanchna* (inner organs) and means being moved from deep inside. In Gospel narratives it is used only of Jesus, and in three parables of the God figure: the Samaritan, the prodigal's father, and the king who forgives a servant. Each time, saving action follows.[^4] Elle adds the Hebrew *rachamim* (compassion), from *rechem* (womb), a picture of making room for someone else to flourish.[^4] What does it do to the parable to see God portrayed in the person the audience saw as the enemy?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** Elle links the Samaritan's "coming upon" the man to Genesis 1:2, where the Spirit of God hovers over the deep, moving over the chaos and entering it.[^13] Following that Spirit means entering other people's messiness even when it "might even make me need to take a bath later."[^13] Jesus ends by turning the question around: not "Who is my neighbor?" but "Who am I being called to be a neighbor to?"[^14] Think of a person or group you tend to know only from 30,000 feet. What small, concrete step would bring you closer to where they actually are?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> A Torah expert asked how to live the life of God's kingdom and then tried, quietly, to limit whom he had to love. Jesus answered with a story that strips the wounded man of everything but his humanity, makes the hated near enemy the hero, and measures faithfulness by how close each traveler is willing to come. Only the Samaritan comes to where the man is, and only he is moved by the gut-deep compassion the Gospels reserve for Jesus and for God. "Go and do likewise" turns the question back on us.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: name one place where you sense an invitation to come closer this week. (You are welcome to keep the details general.)

</div>
