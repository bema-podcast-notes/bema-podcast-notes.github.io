### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** In Pisidian Antioch, Paul spoke to brothers, converts and God-fearers. The episode argued that opposition came less from rejecting Jesus than from discomfort when Gentiles filled the synagogue.

**This week:** The question finally comes to Jerusalem. Must Gentile believers become Jewish to belong fully to God's household?

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode gives the group a framework for the whole session ahead. Second Temple Judaism, Marty explains, recognized three kinds of law: cultic law tied to the temple, ethical law true for everyone, and *miqsat ma'asay haTorah* ("works of the law"), the practices that made a person distinctly Jewish. The council's debate was about that third category. The notes credit the Dead Sea Scrolls and the "New Perspective on Paul" with correcting centuries of reading Paul through later Western lenses.

The council itself is presented as a model of binding and loosing, a Jewish practice of deciding gray areas as a community that Jesus endorsed in Matthew 16. Peter speaks from his experience with Cornelius, James draws on Amos and on the covenant with Noah, and Paul stays surprisingly quiet. The letter that results says it "seemed good to the Holy Spirit" and to the leaders not to burden the Gentiles. Then, almost immediately, Paul and Barnabas have a sharp disagreement and part ways. Because this episode touches how churches make decisions and why they split, every tradition in the room will find something to recognize.

### Three Kinds of Law <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#three-kinds-of-law}

*Before we hear the council's decision, the episode clarifies what exactly was being debated.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Acts 15:1–5 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Notice exactly what is being required of the Gentile believers, and who is requiring it. How would you put the question in your own words?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The episode explains three categories of law recognized in the Second Temple period:[^1]

- **Cultic law:** requirements tied to the temple and altar, such as sacrifices and the priesthood. Christians believe Jesus fulfills this category.
- **Ethical law:** principles true for everyone "because of how God designed the universe to function."
- ***Miqsat ma'asay haTorah*** ("works of the law"): practices that make a person distinctly Jewish, such as circumcision, kosher eating, wearing tassels and cleanliness laws, given to make Israel "a kingdom of priests."

The notes say the central debate was whether this third category is "required for justification."[^2] How does this framework change your sense of what Acts 15 is arguing about? Which category had you assumed it was about?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say the Dead Sea Scrolls "revolutionized understanding of Second Temple Judaism and Paul's writings," and that the New Perspective on Paul "corrected centuries of misreading Paul through later Western theological lenses."[^3] What is one thing you were taught about "works of the law" that you would like to test as we move into Galatians and Romans?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The works of the law were good gifts that marked Israel as distinct. Christian traditions also have practices that mark who we are. Name one distinctive practice in your own tradition that you value. How do you keep it a gift for your community rather than a requirement you place on others before they can belong?

</div>

### Binding and Loosing in Jerusalem <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#binding-and-loosing-in-jerusalem}

*The council listens to experience and to Scripture, and reaches a decision it attributes to the Spirit and to themselves.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Acts 15:6–21 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Notice who speaks, in what order, and what each one appeals to. The notes describe Peter leading "with personal experience (Cornelius)," James representing "the most Jewish perspective" yet championing inclusion, and Paul as "surprisingly quiet during the council," even though he was the issue's catalyst.[^4] What do you notice about how the decision is reached?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode explains that the school of Shammai required Gentiles to convert, while the school of Hillel held that justification comes by faith, like Abraham in Genesis 15. The notes say the council "sided with Hillel but went even further toward full inclusion."[^5]

*Read Genesis 15:6 aloud.*

Why would Abraham be such a natural example for this debate?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes explain that James's requirements drew on the covenant with Noah, which applied to all humanity: no idolatry, sexual purity, and the sanctity of life (no blood, nothing strangled).[^6]

*Read Genesis 9:1–7 and Acts 15:28–29 aloud.*

What connections do you notice? Why might a covenant made with all humanity be a fitting basis for what Gentiles were asked to do?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Binding (restricting or requiring) and loosing (permitting or releasing) was a Jewish process for deciding gray areas "where Scripture doesn't give explicit direction," and Jesus endorsed it in Matthew 16.[^7] The notes suggest the phrase "it seemed good to the Holy Spirit" means God "participates in community discernment rather than dictating absolute positions on gray areas."[^8] Our traditions handle authority and decision-making quite differently, and none of them is being singled out here. How does your tradition decide questions Scripture does not settle explicitly? What would it look like for your church to reach a decision together that it could honestly say "seemed good to the Holy Spirit"?

</div>

### Agreement, Then a Sharp Disagreement <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#agreement-then-a-sharp-disagreement}

*Even after the council, faithful leaders could not agree on what to do next.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Acts 15:36–41 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Notice what Paul and Barnabas disagree about, and what they do not disagree about. The notes observe that "even apostolic leaders like Paul and Barnabas struggled with implementation despite theological agreement."[^9] What does this scene suggest about the difference between agreeing on the gospel and agreeing on how to carry it out?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes say denominational splits often result from poor binding and loosing, decisions made "out of ignorance," and that "healthy disagreement requires respecting community decisions while maintaining dialogue."[^10] Four traditions are represented in this room, each with its own history of agreements and divisions. Think of a disagreement you are part of right now, in church, family or work. What would it look like to disagree well in it this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The Jerusalem Council was not deciding whether Gentiles should be ethical or whether Jesus fulfilled the temple; it was deciding whether the practices that made Israel distinct were required for Gentiles to belong. Peter brought his experience, James brought Amos and the covenant with Noah, and the community reached a decision it believed seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to them. They chose not to burden the Gentiles. And then Paul and Barnabas, agreed on the gospel, parted over a person, a reminder that unity and disagreement can live in the same room.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each: what is one burden you could stop placing on someone else?

</div>
