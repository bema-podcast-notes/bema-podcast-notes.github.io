### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Job's friends sat in silence and then failed him with explanations. Job never learned why he suffered, and the poem at the center of the book told us that wisdom belongs to God alone.

**This week:** We return to Isaiah for its third voice, the "servant discourse" written to people in Babylonian exile, and read Isaiah 53 within it.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This episode looks at what BEMA calls Third Isaiah (chapters 40–54), the "servant discourse," in which God repeatedly calls Israel and Jacob "my servant."[^1] The hosts read Isaiah 53 first in that setting, as an explanation of why Israel was suffering in exile: God's servant people suffer so that future generations might experience healing and restoration.[^2]

Many of us learned Isaiah 53 mainly as a prophecy about Jesus, so this reading may feel unfamiliar. The episode does not set Jesus aside. It argues that Jesus did not fulfill Isaiah 53 to replace our calling but to demonstrate perfectly how God's people should live as suffering servants.[^3] Our traditions in this room read this chapter with different emphases, and the goal tonight is to understand the episode's reading and what it asks of us, not to force agreement.

### You Are My Servant <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#you-are-my-servant}

*Before we reach Isaiah 53, the chapters around it keep telling us who the servant is.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 41:8–10, Isaiah 43:10, and Isaiah 44:1–2 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes say Third Isaiah "consistently identifies the servant as Israel/Jacob, God's chosen people," and that this repeated identification "establishes the interpretive context for understanding Isaiah 53."[^4] As you listen to these three passages, what names does God use for the servant? What else does God say about the servant besides "servant"?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes connect "You are my servant" back to the Torah, where God's relationship with Israel was a partnership from the beginning: "The word that sums up Torah is 'partners' - God was looking for partners in his redemptive work in the world."[^5] How does hearing "servant" as "partner" change the tone of these passages for you? What would it have meant to exiles in Babylon to hear "I have chosen you and have not rejected you"?[^6]

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> These words were spoken to people who had lost everything and were living with the consequences of their own failures.[^7] Where in your life do you most need to hear "I have chosen you and have not rejected you," and what would change if you believed it?

</div>

### Isaiah 53 in Its Setting <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#isaiah-53-in-its-setting}

*Read in the flow of chapters 40–54, Isaiah 53 explains the purpose of the exile.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 52:13–53:12 aloud, slowly.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes point to the shifting voices in this passage. The servant is "he," but the people speak as "we" and "our": the servant suffers "for our transgressions" and "our iniquities," and through his suffering "we are healed."[^8] As you listen, mark where you hear "he" and where you hear "we." What do you notice about who is speaking, and about how their view of the servant changes over the course of the passage?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes explain that Jewish readers approach this chapter differently: they read it within chapters 40–54, they see themselves as participants ("we were in Egypt," not "they were in Egypt"), they hear the singular "he" as Israel acting as one unified servant, and they keep the original audience in view.[^9] On this reading, the passage functions as an almost "out-of-body" reflection explaining the exile itself: the exilic generation perseveres so that future generations will experience restoration.[^10] Set beside the passages we read earlier, how does this reading help you hear Isaiah 53 in a new way? What questions does it raise for you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes summarize Third Isaiah's message to the exiles this way: "You're in exile now, suffering for past failures, but if you persevere as my servant, your children will see healing."[^11] Return to the person you named in the opening. How is their story like this one, and what would it mean for you to persevere so that someone after you will see healing?

</div>

### Jesus Shows Us How <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#jesus-shows-us-how}

*The episode's reading of Isaiah 53 leads back to Jesus, but by a different road.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes contrast two ways of describing Jesus' relationship to the Old Testament. One says "The Old Testament points toward Jesus," which can imply the Old Testament was incomplete or pointless without him. The other says "Jesus points back to validate and perfectly demonstrate Scripture," which affirms the ongoing relevance of Israel's story.[^12] Jesus "didn't fulfill Isaiah 53 to exempt his followers from its calling."[^13] Our traditions have taught us to read Isaiah 53 in particular ways, and those readings have mattered deeply to people in this room. What does each framing protect or emphasize? What, if anything, shifts for you when you hold them side by side?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes name a danger in individualistic reading. Western individualism tempts us to read Isaiah 53 as "Something Jesus did instead of us" or "A completed transaction we merely observe." The participatory reading hears it as "Something Jesus showed us how to do" and "An ongoing invitation to redemptive suffering."[^14] Where have you seen your own faith lean more toward observing than participating, and where has it leaned the other way?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes turn the passage into questions for us: "Are we willing to suffer inconvenience or discomfort so others can experience healing?" and "How does our suffering on behalf of the marginalized bring healing to our communities?"[^15] Think concretely about the coming month. Where could you carry a cost, large or small, so that someone else might be healed?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Third Isaiah was spoken to exiles in Babylon, and again and again God told them, "You are my servant. I have chosen you." Isaiah 53, read in that flow, explains their suffering: the servant people endure so that the generations after them might be healed. Jesus lived that calling perfectly, not so that his followers could watch from a distance, but to show us how to live it. The servant's story is an invitation, and it is still open.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one inconvenience you are willing to carry this week so that someone else might experience healing?

</div>
