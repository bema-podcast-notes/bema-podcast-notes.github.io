### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** John Whittaker gave us "blue jeans theology" and Psalm 1's picture of a tree planted by streams of water, and reminded us that God is more interested in what He does in us than through us.[^1]

**This week:** The first character study of Session 6 rereads Jacob's early story as a family drama rather than a simple tale of deception.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Marty and Brent revisit material from 2017 with new insights drawn mainly from Rabbi David Fohrman's *Genesis: A Parsha Companion*.[^2] They notice that Rebekah receives a word from God about the twins that she apparently never shares with Isaac, that both parents name Esau but only Isaac names Jacob, and that Isaac connects with Esau the "doer" while struggling to value Jacob's quieter temperament.[^3]

Fohrman's reading of Genesis 27 suggests that the goatskins may have been meant as props to give an insecure son confidence rather than as a serious disguise, and that the plan went wrong when Jacob panicked at Isaac's question, "Who is it?"[^4] At Bethel, God promises to be with Jacob, and the episode stresses what God does not say: God never approves of how Jacob got there.[^5]

**Opening Question:** Think of two siblings you know well (in your family, among friends, or in a book or film) who are very different from each other. How did the people around them handle the difference?

### Secrets and Names <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#secrets-and-names}

*The family's trouble starts before the twins are born, with a word only one parent hears.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 25:19–28 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Look at who receives God's word in verse 23, and look for any sign that it is passed on. Then look at verses 25–26 for who does the naming. The notes point out that the text says "they named him Esau" but "he named him Jacob," suggesting that only Isaac named the second son; *Ya'akov* means "heel-grabber" or "deceiver."[^3] What do you notice in your translation, and what does the episode suggest these details reveal about the family?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The text presents Esau as a hunter and man of action and Jacob as "content to stay at home among the tents." The notes insist this "is not about masculinity or femininity, but about different personality types," and that the dysfunction comes when Isaac "clearly favors and understands Esau's personality" but struggles to value Jacob's.[^6] How have you seen families, or churches, value one personality type over another? What tends to happen to the person who does not fit?

*Read Genesis 25:29–34 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes read the birthright scene as evidence that Jacob "has learned to survive through shrewdness," a coping pattern formed by someone "who has never measured up in his father's eyes."[^7] Without naming anyone, where have you seen a pattern formed early in life become someone's automatic response under pressure later on?

</div>

### Who Is It? <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#who-is-it}

*Fohrman asks whether the famous deception was ever meant to be a deception at all.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 27:1–13 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Look closely at Jacob's objection in verses 11–12. The notes point out that his concern is not that they are tricking Isaac but that he would appear to be tricking him, and that the goatskin plan is "absurdly unlikely to work."[^4] What exactly is Jacob afraid of? How well does his fear fit the idea of a carefully planned deception?

*Read Genesis 27:14–29 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** In Fohrman's reading, Rebekah wanted Jacob to bring the meal himself and prove that he, too, could be a "doer"; the clothes and skins were confidence-boosters, and she expected Isaac to realize it was Jacob and bless him as Jacob. Instead, when Isaac asked "Who is it?", Jacob "panicked" and fell back on deception.[^4] The notes say his answer, "I am Esau," shows "he was not ready to be comfortable in his own skin."[^8] Where in this passage do you see the turning point? How does this reading change the way you see Jacob, and Rebekah?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe Rebekah's good intentions as poorly executed: "She cooked the meal he was supposed to cook, dressed him in clothes that weren't his own, and gave him props to hide behind. Her over-functioning prevented his growth while trying to facilitate it."[^9] Where have you seen love over-function and keep someone from growing? What is the difference between helping someone and hiding them?

</div>

### Bethel: Present, Not Approving <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#bethel-present-not-approving}

*Jacob runs from home with a stolen blessing, and God meets him on the road.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 28:10–22 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** List everything God says to Jacob. Then notice what God does not say. The notes observe that God never says "you did the right thing" and never says "I orchestrated these events," but does say "I am with you," "I will watch over you," and "I will not leave you."[^5] Why do you think that silence matters as much as the promise?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode compares the story to the Prodigal Son, with Esau as the elder brother who "keeps trying to do everything right." When he learns his Canaanite wives displease his parents, he tries to fix it by marrying into Abraham's line through Ishmael (Genesis 28:6–9).[^10] How does reading Esau as the elder brother change your sympathy for him? The notes add that this "is not to justify Esau's later hatred, but to understand the human dynamics at play."

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes put it plainly: "God's faithfulness does not equal His approval of our methods."[^5] Where do you need to hear "I am with you" without pretending you got where you are the right way? How might that change the way you treat someone who arrived somewhere by a crooked path?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Jacob grew up in a family with a secret: a mother who held a word from God and a father who loved the son he understood. Fohrman suggests that the goatskins were meant to help an insecure son feel brave, and that the plan collapsed when Jacob could not yet say who he was. Jacob ran from the consequences, and at Bethel God met him with a promise of presence, not a word of approval. God does not wait for us to get it right before showing up. His presence is not permission, but it is a promise not to leave.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one place in your life where you want to become more comfortable in your own skin?

</div>
