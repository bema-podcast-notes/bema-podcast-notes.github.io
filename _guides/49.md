### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Amos held God's plumb line against a prosperous northern kingdom and found a wall too crooked to stand, a people who had become the "anti-story" by exploiting the poor[^1].

**This week:** Hosea, the second pre-Assyrian prophet to the north, lives his message in his own marriage, showing both Israel's unfaithfulness and God's refusal to let them go[^2].

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Where Amos used a picture, Hosea uses his life. God commands him to marry Gomer, a prostitute, and their marriage and the names of their three children become "real-life theater" for Israel's unfaithfulness[^3]. The children's names (Jezreel, Lo-Ruhamah and Lo-Ammi) announce judgment, yet hope follows even the harshest of them[^4].

The episode shows Hosea holding together two concerns that earlier weeks separated: wrong worship and the mistreatment of people. Idolatry, the notes say, is not merely choosing the wrong god but adopting narratives that enable exploitation and injustice[^5]. At the center stands Hosea 6:6, "I desire mercy, not sacrifice," which Jesus later quotes[^6]. The episode ends in hope: God's anguished "How can I give you up?" in chapter 11, the Valley of Achor becoming a door of hope, and the *V'erastich* prayer from Hosea 2:19–20, which Jewish tradition prays daily[^7].

### Prophetic Theater: Names That Preach <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#prophetic-theater-names-that-preach}

*Hosea's household becomes a living sermon to the northern kingdom.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Hosea 1:2–10 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes give the meanings of the three names: Jezreel ("scattered"), Lo-Ruhamah ("not loved") and Lo-Ammi ("not my people")[^4]. As you listen, what do you notice about the order of the names, and what comes immediately after the last one? What would it have been like to grow up with one of those names in a small community?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes contrast Amos's plumb line, a literary metaphor, with Hosea's "real-life theater," in which his marriage becomes the canvas for Israel's story. They leave open whether God literally commanded the marriage or Hosea's experience inspired the message[^3]. What does a message lived out in a household communicate that a spoken message cannot?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes acknowledge that the marriage metaphor "can be misused to justify abusive relationships," and say that properly understood it emphasizes God's faithful commitment despite human unfaithfulness[^8]. The notes on Ep. 47 add that Hosea describes the covenant of a whole people, not counseling for individual marriages[^9]. How can we hold this image so that it reveals God's faithfulness without ever being used to keep someone in harm?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q4:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Hosea's children carried his message in their names every day. What message do you think your daily life is preaching to the people closest to you right now, and how close is it to the one you want to preach?

</div>

### Mercy, Not Sacrifice <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#mercy-not-sacrifice}

*Hosea refuses to separate how Israel worships from how Israel treats people.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Hosea 4:1–2 aloud, then Hosea 6:6–9.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Hosea says there is no faithfulness, love or acknowledgment of God in the land, and then lists cursing, lying, murder, stealing and adultery. In chapter 6, bands of priests "lie in ambush" on the road to Shechem[^5]. What do you notice about who is doing harm in these verses, and how worship and violence sit side by side?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes say Hosea integrates Source A (wrong worship) and Source B (injustice): idolatry is "adopting narratives that enable exploitation and injustice," and worshiping false gods lets people escape God's demands for mercy and justice[^5]. Why might the stories we believe about God shape how we treat people? Can you think of an example, ancient or modern?

*Read Matthew 9:10–13 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Jesus quotes Hosea 6:6 and tells the Pharisees to "go and learn what this means." The notes say the temple exists for mercy, and sacrifice merely facilitates mercy[^6]. Each of our traditions treasures practices that help us worship well, such as the way we observe the Lord's Supper, baptize, sing or keep liturgy. Without ranking anyone's tradition, how can a faithful practice slowly drift into "sacrifice" without mercy, and what are the signs that it has?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe Israel's calling as "a kingdom of priests," mediating God's mercy rather than only performing rituals[^6]. Who in your world needs mercy mediated to them this week, and what would that look like in a concrete act?

</div>

### God and Not Man: A Door of Hope <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#god-and-not-man-a-door-of-hope}

*Even in judgment, God speaks of starting over.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Hosea 11:8–9 aloud, then Hosea 2:14–20.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes describe chapter 11 as the angst of a faithful spouse longing for reconciliation; because God is "God and not man," his mercy outlasts human patience. In chapter 2, God leads Israel back to the desert, their "honeymoon" after the exodus, and makes the Valley of Achor, where Achan's sin was judged in Joshua 7, a door of hope[^7]. We read Joshua early in this session. What does it mean to you that God chooses a place of judgment as the doorway to a new beginning?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The *V'erastich* prayer (Hosea 2:19–20) is recited daily while wrapping tefillin, and the notes say it puts God's faithfulness first, so that the Shema becomes the believer's "I do" in response. It is God's commitment that lets people truly know (*yadah*, "to know, to experience") him[^10]. How would it change your prayer life to begin each day by hearing God's promise before making your own?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Hosea lived the pain of loving someone unfaithful, and in doing so he showed Israel how God felt about them. He refused to let worship float free from the way people are treated, insisting that God wants mercy more than sacrifice. Yet the book does not end in despair. "Not my people" will be called "sons of the living God," the valley of judgment becomes a door of hope, and every morning begins with God's promise before our response.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one promise of God you want to hear first thing tomorrow morning?

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 50 — Micah — Judge](/guides/50)** (36:58): Micah brings God's word to the southern kingdom of Judah, picturing God as a judge who actively sets things right. The episode revisits the famous Bethlehem prophecy in its original setting and builds to Micah 6:8: act justly, love mercy and walk humbly with God[^11]. Unlike most prophetic warnings, Micah's was heeded, through King Hezekiah's reforms.

**What to bring next week:** Read Micah 6:6–8 slowly twice. Write down which of the three requirements (justice, mercy, humility) feels most natural to you and which feels hardest.
