### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Jesus entered The Chosen quietly, placing a hand on Mary Magdalene's and speaking the words of Isaiah 43 her father had taught her: "I have called you by name. You are mine." Nicodemus asked a question that still hangs in the air: "When did Shabbat become theater?"[^1]

**This week:** The second episode answers Nicodemus's question with a series of Sabbath tables, some opulent, some broken, some empty, and one where Jesus sits down among people nobody else would invite.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Marty calls this episode potentially "one of the best episodes of a series that I have ever seen." It opens in 948 BC with a family keeping *Shabbat* (Sabbath) with real joy, then cuts a thousand years forward to Capernaum, where the same day has fractured into very different experiences: a wealthy Pharisee's dinner, a woman trying to remember how to celebrate, a tax collector eating alone with a stray dog, a fisherman choosing his scheme over his wife, and Jesus gathering with outcasts in Mary's home.[^2]

Along the way the hosts reframe the *eshet chayil* ("woman of valor") of Proverbs 31 as a proclamation rather than a checklist, argue that the Pharisees were devout people trapped by a system rather than simple hypocrites, and name shrewdness as a biblical virtue Western Christians often neglect. The episode closes on a table full of people the religious world had excluded, with Jesus sitting in an empty chair meant for Elijah.[^3]

### One Day, Many Tables <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#one-day-many-tables}

*The episode sets an ancient, joyful Sabbath beside the Sabbaths of Jesus's day and lets us feel the difference.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The opening scene, set in Kinnereth (ancient Gennesaret) in 948 BC, shows a father blessing his wife, then his children, and the family watching together for the first star. The notes call this "Shabbat as proclamation rather than recitation."[^4] The show then moves to AD 26 and cuts between five Sabbath experiences:[^5]

- Nicodemus's dinner with fellow Pharisees: wealthy and proper, but lacking heart and joy
- Mary Magdalene trying to remember how to celebrate after years of being cast out
- Matthew, disowned and alone, sharing his meal with a stray dog
- Simon choosing his illegal fishing scheme over the Sabbath with his wife
- Jesus gathering with outcasts in Mary's home

Which of these tables did you find yourself watching most closely, and what has it kept or lost compared with the family in the opening scene?

*Read Proverbs 31:10–31 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes say this poem has often been read in Western Christianity as a checklist or set of minimum requirements for godly wives. In Jewish practice it is spoken over a wife at the Sabbath table, a proclamation of what "erupts out of you" when you witness her character, followed by the blessing of the children.[^6] As you listen, notice the tone of the passage and who seems to be speaking. What in the text itself sounds like praise rather than demand? How does it change the poem to hear it spoken aloud over a real person at a meal?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes mention that Rabbi David Fohrman connects the *eshet chayil* to the Book of Ruth as a commentary on her character, and they observe that the checklist reading "has burdened many women with an impossible standard."[^7] If you know Ruth's story, where do you hear her in this poem? What difference does it make to read the poem as a celebration of a particular woman rather than a measuring stick for every woman?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes ask a pointed question of all of us: "Do we speak blessings over family members because we genuinely see and celebrate their character, or do we go through motions because we're 'supposed to'?"[^8] Where in your own life (a home ritual, a church practice, a habit of prayer) has something drifted toward recitation, and what has helped it, or might help it, become proclamation again? Share only what you want to.

</div>

### Trapped by Devotion <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#trapped-by-devotion}

*The religious leaders in this episode are sincere and serious, which makes their blind spot more sobering, not less.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The *av beit din* (master of the court, second-ranking member of the Sanhedrin) calls for an investigation into Mary's healing, while Nicodemus shows curiosity and humility. The notes argue that the Pharisees' problem was not mainly corrupt hearts: they were "trapped by their own devotion and commitment to a system that is actually going to be getting in the way."[^9] What changes when we read these leaders as devout people caught in a system rather than as hypocrites? What does each reading make easier for us, and what does each let us avoid?

*Read Matthew 10:16 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Matthew, an outcast with obvious gifts for reading power, navigates Roman politics shrewdly. The notes list Esther, Daniel and Joseph as biblical examples and insist that "Shrewdness is not manipulation but wise understanding of how systems work and strategic engagement." The soldier Gaius models the opposite kind of power: "There's only one language that keeps the peace, Marcus. Learn to speak it."[^10] How would you describe the line between shrewdness and manipulation? Why do you think Western Christianity has tended to "emphasize morality over engagement with power structures"?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes turn the lens on us: Christians "must examine how church traditions, denominational dogmas, and cultural Christianity might obstruct genuine Kingdom vision. Devotion to the system is not the same as devotion to God."[^11] This is a diagnostic question, not an accusation, and every tradition in the room has gifts worth keeping. Thinking only of your own tradition or habits, what is one good thing that could, if held too tightly, keep you from noticing what God is doing in front of you?

</div>

### The Table at Mary's House <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#the-table-at-marys-house}

*The final Sabbath scene gathers the people who would never receive an invitation.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** At Mary's house sit Barnaby (likely a *mamzer*, a person of illegitimate birth with severe social and religious restrictions), Shula (a blind woman carrying social stigma), Mary herself (formerly demon-possessed, from "the red quarter"), and James and Thaddaeus, two relatively unknown students of Jesus. Meanwhile, at Nicodemus's dinner, a rabbi states his intention to "try to get the seat near the head of the table."[^12] What do you notice about how each table decides who matters and where people sit?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Mary, confused about Sabbath and Passover customs, sets out the empty chair kept for Elijah, and Jesus takes "Elijah's seat." He delights in the company, winks at Barnaby's joke, and defers leadership to Mary in her own home.[^13] Which of these details stays with you, and what does it suggest about how Jesus handles honor?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes ask modern churches "who the 'mamzerim' are today": people kept out of religious gatherings by stigma, judgment or social convention.[^14] Without naming individuals, who in your world is unlikely to get a Sabbath invitation? What is one concrete step toward a table like Mary's that you could take this month?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> This episode answers Nicodemus's question about Sabbath theater by showing us the alternatives side by side. The oldest table is the most alive, full of blessing that erupts rather than recites. The most impressive table is the emptiest, full of people competing for seats. And the table Jesus chooses belongs to the people the system left out, where he sits in a chair set aside for someone else and lets his host lead. Authentic Sabbath, the notes conclude, is found "wherever Jesus gathers the broken and forgotten and restores their dignity and belonging."[^15]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, name one person you could bless this week by saying out loud what you genuinely see in them.

</div>
