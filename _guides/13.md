### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Rebekah watered ten camels for a stranger, and Isaac gave up well after well rather than fight, until the Philistines came asking for peace. The promise to bless the nations was starting to show.[^1]

**This week:** We meet Jacob, a man who wants the birthright badly and chases it by every means available. The episode asks a hard question: why would God build his people through someone like this?[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Marty admits that he struggles with the tough parts of Jacob's story, and that his reading of Jacob owes a great deal to Rabbi David Fohrman. The episode walks through Jacob's early life: the birthright Esau despises and Jacob craves, the blessing Jacob takes from his blind father with his mother's help, a dream of a ladder in a place that is "nowhere significant," and twenty years with an uncle who out-tricks him.

The notes are careful not to endorse Jacob's methods. They describe the chaos and broken relationships that follow him. Yet they also suggest that God would rather work with someone who has a fire in the belly than with someone who does not care. Some of us were raised to value careful rule-keeping, and some of us were raised to value zeal, so this week is a good chance to listen to each other.

### The Birthright and the Blessing <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-birthright-and-the-blessing}

*Two brothers relate very differently to what their father has to pass on.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 25:27–34 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes explain that the firstborn, the *bechor*, received a double portion: with two sons the estate was split three ways and the *bechor* took two shares. That meant a double portion of wealth and also a double portion of responsibility for carrying on the family's covenant with God.[^3] With that in mind, what do you notice about how each brother is described and what the last line says about Esau?

*Read Genesis 27:30–38 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes say Isaac would have carefully crafted a different blessing for each son. They contrast the Western saying about sticks and stones with an Eastern view: "to Eastern Jews, words are power. Once these words leave his mouth, he cannot just take them back."[^4] What does Esau's bitter weeping, and Isaac's inability to undo what he said, tell you about how this family understood spoken words?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes raise questions about Rebekah rather than answering them: whether she acted because she favored Jacob or because God had told her the older would serve the younger, whether she ever told Isaac, and even whether it took faith and trust in the story to make it happen, knowing what kind of person Jacob was at the time.[^5] How do you read Rebekah's choice, and what in the text leads you there?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Isaac's words could not be taken back. What words were spoken over you, by a parent, teacher, coach or pastor, that still carry weight in your life, for good or for ill?

</div>

### God in the Middle of Nowhere <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#god-in-the-middle-of-nowhere}

*Running from the brother he cheated, Jacob meets God in a place no one would have chosen.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 28:10–22 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes describe Bethel as effectively "nowhere significant," and say that when Jacob wakes he realizes God is everywhere, even in places where we do not think he is.[^6] What do you notice about what God says to Jacob in the dream, and about what God does not say, given what Jacob has just done to Esau and Isaac?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe God's response to Jacob here as "stupidly gracious."[^7] Why might God meet a man fresh from deceiving his family with promises instead of rebuke, and what makes that grace hard for some of us to accept?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes sum up Jacob's waking thought as "God was in this place and I didn't know it."[^8] Where is an ordinary, unremarkable place in your life (a commute, a kitchen, a hospital waiting room) where you later realized God had been present?

</div>

### Meeting His Match <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#meeting-his-match}

*In Laban, Jacob meets someone as cunning as he is.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 29:21–27 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes say Jacob "meets his match" in Laban, who tricks him into marrying Leah first because culture dictated that the older daughter be married off first. From there the two men compete to see who can outdo the other.[^9] Listen to Laban's explanation in verse 26. What do you notice about the reason he gives, and how might it have landed on a younger brother who took his older brother's place?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> When Jacob finally leaves (Genesis 31), the two men share a meal meant for reconciliation, yet they cannot even agree on what to call the place. The notes point out that the repeated "And Laban said..." implies Jacob is listening and not responding.[^10] What does this uneasy ending suggest about what deception does to relationships, even after a truce?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes close with an observation: "It also seems that G-d prefers those that have a fire in their belly over those that are more apathetic. The Ya'akovs over the 'Esavs."[^11] Elsewhere they say God can "steer a moving target" more easily than he can motivate the apathetic.[^12] Our traditions weigh faithful rule-keeping and passionate zeal differently, and both have something to teach. Where do you see a little Jacob and a little Esau in yourself, and what would it look like for God to steer your fire this year?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Jacob is hard to like. He bargains for a birthright, deceives his blind father, and leaves a trail of broken relationships. Yet he wants what his family carries, while his brother shrugs it off. God meets him in a nowhere place with promises he has not earned, and then sends him to an uncle who shows him his own reflection. The notes do not excuse Jacob's methods, but they suggest God would rather steer a fire than kindle one from nothing, and this is only Part 1.[^13]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one thing you deeply want from God right now?

</div>
