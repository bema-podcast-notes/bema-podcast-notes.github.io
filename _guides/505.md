### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Elle Grover Fricks described community as witnesses who tell God's story "again," like a bride sharing letters from her absent groom. She named three barriers that keep people away (church hurt, intellectual superiority and self-sufficiency) and invited us to come home anyway.

**This week:** Marty turns to the third pillar, discipleship as Jesus practiced it, and separates the rare calling of intensive discipleship from principles any of us can live.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Marty begins with humility. He admits he has spoken too confidently in the past about how rabbinic discipleship worked, and he places BEMA's teaching within the scholarly "quests for the historical Jesus." He then describes three features of discipleship in Jesus' world: a full-time commitment, a way of learning built on imitation, and a progression from copying the rabbi, to interpreting, to friendship, to being sent. He traces these through five Gospel passages, including a fresh reading of Peter sinking in the water.

The second half compares this with how churches usually use the word "discipleship." Marty tells the story of his own intensive discipleship in campus ministry, admits he is not doing it right now, and insists that not being in this kind of discipleship says nothing about anyone's standing before God. He closes with principles open to every believer.

### Following the Rabbi <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#following-the-rabbi}

*Marty describes what discipleship looked like in Jesus' world, starting with how much we can and cannot know.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q1:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty says his language about discipleship has "softened" over the years: no less passionate, but more careful. He thinks the critique of "received" history is valid but often goes too far, and that the truth is usually "somewhere in the middle"[^1]. Why might a teacher's admission that he once overstated things make the rest of what he says easier to trust rather than harder?

*Read Matthew 4:18–22 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** Marty describes discipleship in this world as a 24/7/365 commitment, not "show up on Tuesdays and Thursdays." Disciples went where the rabbi went, slept where he slept and ate where he ate[^2]. List everything these four men leave in the passage. What does the text show you about what following Jesus cost them, and what it did not tell them in advance?

*Read Matthew 14:22–33 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Marty's reading of Peter is that he reasons, "If it's really you, you'd want me to do this too," because a rabbi doesn't do anything he doesn't want his disciples to copy. Peter really does walk on water. When he sinks, he has lost faith in himself, not in Jesus, so "Why did you doubt?" means "Why don't you believe you can do what I've called you to do?"[^3] We often say, "I can't be like Jesus." Where in your own life do you hear yourself saying that, and what would change if Jesus believed you could?

</div>

### From Copying to Being Sent <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#from-copying-to-being-sent}

*Marty traces how a disciple grows from repeating the rabbi to being sent out under his authority.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Marty reads "the keys" in Matthew 16 as likely permission to interpret and teach independently while staying aligned with the rabbi; before this, a disciple could only say "I've heard my rabbi say..."[^4] In John 15 the disciples move from students to something close to peers, and Jesus reverses the usual pattern by choosing them instead of waiting to be asked[^5].

*Read John 15:12–17 aloud.*

What words in this passage mark a change in the relationship? What does it suggest to you that Jesus took the initiative with the Twelve?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty points out two unusual things in the Great Commission. Jesus keeps his authority instead of passing it on, and he sends out all of them, including those described in "But some doubted"[^6].

*Read Matthew 28:16–20 aloud.*

What does it mean to you that the doubters were sent too? How might that shape the way you treat your own doubts, or someone else's?

</div>

### What We Mean by Discipleship <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#what-we-mean-by-discipleship}

*Marty compares rabbinic discipleship with the ways churches use the word today.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Marty names three common modern meanings of discipleship: disciple as believer (common, he says, in his own Christian Church/Church of Christ tradition), discipleship as spiritual formation, and discipleship as church assimilation through small groups. He considers each one fine as language, but none of them is what Jesus did with Peter, James and John. Rabbinic discipleship was rare, and not being in it says nothing about anyone's standing before God[^7]. Which of these meanings did you grow up with? What did that use of the word get right, and what might it have made harder to see?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty admits his campus ministry discipleship "could get real culty real quick," so the group stayed tied to the wider church through worship, small groups, service and peer accountability[^8]. What other safeguards would you want around any close mentoring relationship, so that it stays healthy?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Marty admits, "I'm not doing discipleship right now," and reminds himself, "I'm not letting Jesus down"[^9]. Even so, he offers principles for everyone: follow someone a step or two ahead of you, reach back to someone a step or two behind, and remember that "It's one thing to follow Jesus. It's another thing to know that somebody is watching you follow Jesus"[^10]. Think of the person you brought to mind this week. Who is a step or two behind you, and what is one small, "two-degree" step you could take with either of them?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Marty starts by admitting he has sometimes claimed more certainty than the evidence supports, and then shows what still holds up. In Jesus' world a disciple gave the rabbi their whole life and learned by imitating him, moving from copying, to interpreting, to friendship, to being sent. When Jesus calls us to imitate him, he believes we can, even when we don't. That intensive kind of discipleship was always rare, and not being in it does not make anyone less faithful. The principles are still open to all of us, and as Brent reminds us, there are no shortcuts: even Jesus took about three years[^11].
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, where do you most need to hear Jesus say, "Why don't you believe you can do what I've called you to do?"

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 506 — Talmid & Morah](/guides/506)** (56:25): Elle Grover Fricks joins Marty and Brent to look at the Hebrew, Greek and Latin words for "disciple," and she traces a pattern where the language of discipling keeps appearing next to the language of children. From it she argues that making disciples often means becoming a spiritual parent, taking responsibility for someone while protecting their independence. The episode also gives the "I do, we do, you do" model and a strong case for naming what people do well.

**What to bring next week:** Read 1 Corinthians 4:15 and 1 Timothy 1:2 and notice what Paul calls the people he invested in. Then think of one person who said out loud what you were doing right, and what it did for you.
