### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We heard that to "fulfill" Torah, in Jesus's world, meant to interpret it rightly and walk it out, and that Jesus, with the authority affirmed at his baptism, invites us to watch him to learn how to read the whole Bible.

**This week:** Jesus shows what that looks like, with six examples that move from murder to love for enemies.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This episode finishes Matthew 5. Through six examples (murder, adultery, divorce, oaths, retaliation and love for enemies) Jesus shows that the commandments were always meant to change who we are and how we treat people, not only to regulate outward behavior.[^1] The notes insist that Jesus is not "upping the ante" on God's expectations but revealing what God always intended.[^2] He is taking part in a lively conversation within Judaism about how to read Torah, and his examples all turn toward people: reconciliation, dignity, keeping our word, and creative, non-violent responses to injustice.[^3]

The episode ends with the line usually translated "Be perfect, therefore, as your heavenly Father is perfect." It reads this as an echo of Leviticus 19:2, "Be holy," with holiness (*kadosh*) meaning set apart or different. What sets God's people apart, the episode argues, is love that reaches even enemies.[^4]

### Anger, Lust, and Divorce <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#anger-lust-and-divorce}

*Jesus shows that the commandments against murder and adultery were always about how we see and treat people.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 5:21–32 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Look closely at verses 21–26. The notes say Jesus gives "proactive expectations: be reconciled with your brother or sister before offering gifts at the temple, settle legal matters before they escalate to court, and resolve disputes quickly."[^5] What does Jesus tell people to actively do here, and what does that suggest the commandment against murder was about all along?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes describe the commandment against adultery as being "about recognizing the full humanity of others and not treating people as objects," because "Lust reduces a person to something to be used rather than someone to be honored."[^6] They also say "Jesus is not 'upping the ante' on God's expectations but revealing what God always intended through Torah."[^7] What is the difference between a teacher who raises the bar and a teacher who reveals what the bar was for, and why does that difference matter?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes explain that the rabbis had built "fences" around the law, which "came from their experience in Babylon, where they concluded they hadn't followed God's commands well enough."[^8] Fences were a sincere attempt never to repeat the exile. What fences have you or your church built to protect yourselves from sin, and how can you tell when a fence is still serving its purpose?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** On divorce, the notes grieve that "this passage has been weaponized to judge and condemn divorced people, when Jesus's entire point is about treating people with love and dignity."[^9] They offer a test for any reading of Scripture: "Is my interpretation leading me to love people better, or am I using this text to justify judgment and exclusion?"[^10] No one needs to share personal history here. Where have you seen a Bible passage used as a weapon, and what would it look like for you to read that passage in a way that helps you love people better?

</div>

### Oaths and the Other Cheek <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#oaths-and-the-other-cheek}

*Jesus offers a third way between taking abuse and returning it.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 5:33–42 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes describe a system of oaths in Jesus's day with built-in loopholes: "You could swear by the temple, then later swear by the gold of the temple to override your previous promise."[^11] Jesus cuts through it, and the notes say the point is "becoming people whose word means something."[^12] What do you notice about how short and simple Jesus's alternative is, and what makes it so hard in practice?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes explain the three examples in verses 39–41. A blow to the right cheek "requires a backhanded slap—the way you'd strike a slave or inferior," so turning the other cheek says, "If you're going to hit me, hit me like an equal, like a human being."[^13] Handing over your undergarment in court exposes "the complete injustice of the situation," and Roman soldiers could force civilians to carry their packs one mile, a practice called *angaria*, but no farther.[^14] Read verses 39–41 again with this background. What changes in how you hear "turn the other cheek"?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say "eye for an eye" was "originally given as a protective measure to prevent the cycle of vengeance from escalating," that it "was meant to limit retribution, not license it," and that people had turned it "from a defensive posture into an offensive one."[^15] Where do you see that same drift today, when a limit meant to restrain harm gets used as permission to cause it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes suggest responses "that break expected patterns and create space for transformation."[^16] Think of a conflict you are in right now, at work, at home or online. What would a "second mile" response look like there: one that neither retaliates nor simply gives in?

</div>

### Love Your Enemies <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#love-your-enemies}

*The only thing that truly sets God's people apart is love that reaches enemies.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 5:43–48 and Leviticus 19:2 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes point out that the phrase "love your neighbor and hate your enemy" "was not found anywhere until the discovery of the Dead Sea Scrolls, which revealed this teaching in the Essene community's code."[^17] Look at the reasons Jesus gives for loving enemies in verses 44–47. What do they reveal about God, and about why loving only those who love us is not enough?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes say "perfect" is "a Greek philosophical concept (particularly Platonic) that doesn't exist in Hebrew thought," and that Jesus is quoting Leviticus 19:2, where *kadosh* means "set apart" or "different," not flawless.[^18] They conclude: "The command is not to be flawless but to be different—set apart by how we love."[^19] Compare Matthew 5:48 with Leviticus 19:2. How does hearing "be holy" instead of "be perfect" change what Jesus is asking of you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q11:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes connect this to Israel's calling as a "kingdom of priests" in Exodus 19, whose role is "to put God on display."[^20] They observe that American Christian culture "often emphasizes national identity, political alignment, and cultural values as markers of Christian distinctiveness," while Jesus names radical love for enemies.[^21] Without pointing at any one group, what markers have you been taught signal that someone is a "real" Christian, and how do they compare with this one?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q12:** Think of one person you would honestly call an enemy, or at least someone who does not wish you well. What is one concrete act of love, as specific as a prayer by name, a kind word or a meal, that you could offer them this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> In six examples, Jesus shows what it means to fulfill Torah. "Do not murder" becomes a call to go and be reconciled. The command against adultery becomes a call to honor the full humanity of every person. Oaths give way to a simple yes and no, and "eye for an eye" opens into creative, non-violent responses that expose injustice without repeating it. At the end Jesus echoes Leviticus: be holy, be different, as your Father is different. What sets God's people apart is not flawless rule-keeping but love that reaches even our enemies, the same love that sends sun and rain on everyone.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: which of the six examples do you most want to grow into this week, and why?

</div>
