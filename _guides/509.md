### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Marty pictured wrestling as scaffolding around a cathedral, and pointed to Moses turning aside at the bush and Peter following God rather than his belief system.[^1]

**This week:** Elle Grover Fricks offers three arenas for wrestling, each building on the last: the text, our community and tradition, and God himself.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Elle opens with Jacob at the Jabbok, who came away blessed because he would not let go. She then shows what wrestling with the text looks like by turning one passage, the transfiguration, through eight different lenses. Jewish tradition speaks of the "70 faces" of Torah, and Elle's point is not to show off but to build a humble, curious posture that can hear a traditional sermon with compassion rather than contempt. Brent adds that repetition is itself a faithful practice.

The second arena is community and tradition. Through a parable about someone troubled by the Apostles' Creed line "he descended into hell," Elle names cynicism as the main alternative to wrestling and lays out a patient path of study instead. The third arena is God himself: a daily wrestling, since Jesus is our *daily* bread and God's mercies are new every morning. The episode closes with a blessing to grip the word, our community and the Spirit, and not let go.

### Wrestling with the Text <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#wrestling-with-the-text}

*Elle shows how a familiar passage can be turned like a gemstone to catch new light.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Luke 9:28–36 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** What details do you notice about the timing, Jesus's face and clothes, who appears with him and what the disciples do? Elle says the common Trinitarian reading is valid but leaves questions open: "Why is he glowing? Why are his clothes like lightning? Why the specific count of days?"[^3] Which details in the passage seem to be asking for more attention?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** Here are four of Elle's eight readings.[^4]
- **Fulfillment:** Moses stands for the Law and Elijah for the Prophets; Jesus fulfills both.
- **The bridge between death and life:** Moses died without entering the land; Elijah never died. Jesus connects the two.
- **Access to the Holy of Holies:** Moses's face glowed and he wore a veil (Exodus 34). Jesus *is* God face to face and needs no veil.
- **The new circumcision:** Luke says "about eight days after" Jesus taught about taking up the cross, and circumcision happens on the eighth day.

Which lens opens something new for you, and which detail in the text does it rest on?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Brent says BEMA endorses doing the *same* thing over and over. Marty prays the same daily prayers; many days nothing stands out, but over time the same words speak in new ways. Elle compares it to driving the same road to the grocery store and one day noticing a beautiful tree that was always there.[^5] Where has repetition, in prayer, reading or worship, surprised you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Elle says building many lenses produces "a humble, curious learner's posture," so that when a pastor preaches a traditional reading we can respond with compassion rather than contempt. She adds that a traditional reading is not a "wrong" one, and that pastors carry meetings, hospital visits and funerals, not just study time.[^6] When you hear Scripture taught this coming Sunday, what might it look like to ask what fruit a reading produces instead of what it is missing?

</div>

### Wrestling with Community and Tradition <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#wrestling-with-community-and-tradition}

*Wrestling with our community doesn't mean fighting; it means digging into the history behind what troubles us.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Elle tells a parable. Someone leaves an unhealthy church setting, lands in a liturgical church that feels life-giving, then hits the Apostles' Creed line "he descended into hell." If they keep saying it, they feel alienated from themselves; if they stop, they feel alienated from their community. Either way, cynicism can move in.[^7] Why do you think both dead ends lead to the same place?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Elle's alternative is to become an expert in the issue, even though it takes time: find the scripture behind the line (for this one, usually 1 Peter 3:18–20 and Ephesians 4:8–10), read commentaries and trusted authors, study reception history (how different traditions have read the verse over time), learn the history of the creed itself, and ask why other creeds such as the Nicene leave the line out. The notes observe that "anger often comes from not seeing the complexity."[^8] Our traditions relate to creeds differently; some recite them every week and some intentionally do not. Without ranking those choices, where have you seen learning the backstory of a practice change how you felt about it?

*Read Acts 9:1–6 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Saul thought he was fighting a community, yet Jesus asks, "Why are you persecuting *me*?" Elle pairs this with Moses, who thought he was looking at a bush and was actually meeting God; walking away in cynicism, she says, is walking away from the bush. Her conclusion is that when we wrestle with community, we are really pursuing God, not trying to prove ourselves right.[^9] Name one line, hymn, practice or habit in your own church or tradition that troubles you. If you spent "eight minutes a day" studying it, as Elle suggests, where would you start?[^10]

</div>

### Wrestling with God Himself <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#wrestling-with-god-himself}

*The deepest arena is a daily encounter with God, who sets his own agenda.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 6:11 and Lamentations 3:22–23 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Elle points out that homemade bread goes stale or moldy in about three days, and that only modern preservatives and refrigerators let us "live off" one encounter with Jesus for a week. God's mercies are new **every morning**, not every Sunday or with every new book. She suggests that if our practices produce no "glorious friction," it may be time to change them.[^11] What does your "daily bread" with God look like right now, and does it feel fresh or stale?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Elle says the Spirit may keep raising something uncomfortable, such as reconciliation with a former friend, integrity at work, how we parent, or how we spend money and time, and that it is easier to drown it out with sermons, audiobooks, videos and podcasts. She also notes that God sets the agenda: we plan to work on kindness, and God says the lesson is patience, then sends a long delayed train ride.[^12] If you are willing to share, what has been coming up for you again and again lately?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Elle gives us three places to wrestle, and each one leads to the next. With the text, we keep turning the gem, and the many faces we find keep us humble toward people who read differently. With our community and tradition, we choose curiosity over cynicism and dig until we see the complexity, often finding God there, as Moses did when he turned aside. With God himself, we wrestle every day, because yesterday's bread goes stale. The episode ends with a blessing: grip the word, your community and the Spirit, and don't let go, trusting that God already has a grip on you.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: which of the three arenas (text, community, God himself) is calling for your attention this week?

</div>
