### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Reed Dent reframed deconstruction as pruning, map-making and clearing scaffolding, done best among friends who give each other permission to question and to challenge.[^1]

**This week:** Elle Grover Fricks joins the teaching team with a Hebrew word study on law and a close reading of Judges 11 that asks what a community owes those it sends away.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Elle Grover Fricks brings a master's degree in Biblical Hebrew and the Ancient Near East from Hebrew University of Jerusalem, experience in mental health, and research on religious texts and violence against women. Marty explains that the teaching team is expanding because they want "no cult of Marty and Brent," and Elle speaks honestly about the extra credentials women often feel they need to be taken seriously in church spaces.[^3]

Elle then shows how five Hebrew words usually translated "law" or "commandment" each picture God differently: as King, Creator, Judge, something precious, and Teacher. She closes with Judges 11, where she sees a parallel that is often missed: Jephthah and his daughter are both rejected through no fault of their own and both go into the wilderness, but only the father is pursued and brought back.[^4] This story involves the killing of a young woman. Share only what you choose, and be gentle with one another.

### Five Pictures of God in the Words for Law <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#five-pictures-of-god-in-the-words-for-law}

*Elle shows that Hebrew words for God's commands carry pictures, and each picture reveals something about God.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Elle says Hebrew is "very, very, very image-based" and that she has "never run into a word" that "doesn't have a picture behind it." She walks through five words: *mitzvah* ("decree" or "charge," presenting God as King); *choq* (something cut or engraved, connected to carving and presenting God as Creator); *mishpat* ("the place of judgment," presenting God as a biblical judge like Deborah); *mishmarti* (things to guard, from *shamar*, "to guard" or "to keep watch," presenting God's commands as something precious); and *Torah* ("teaching," with the picture of flowing water, presenting God as Teacher).[^5] Which picture is new to you, and how does it change the way the word "law" sounds?

*Read Genesis 26:5 aloud, in as many different translations as the group has.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Elle points to this verse, where Abraham "kept my things that should be guarded" (*veshamar mishmarti*), and says provocatively that "all translation is mistranslation."[^5] Compare the translations in the room. What English words do they choose for each of the terms in this verse? What is gained, and what is lost, when several different Hebrew words all become "law" or "commandment"?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Elle sums up the word study: "He is a King. He is a creator. He is a judge. He is something precious. He is our teacher."[^5] Think diagnostically about the tradition you grew up in or know best. Which of these pictures of God did it emphasize, and which did you rarely hear? Which one do you most need to remember this week, and why?

</div>

### Jephthah and His Daughter <span class="guide-time">40 min</span> {#jephthah-and-his-daughter}

*Elle reads Judges 11 by setting the father's story beside the daughter's.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Judges 11:1–11 aloud, then Judges 11:29–40.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Elle describes Jephthah as one who receives "ostracization from his family and community because of something that he did not do wrong," goes into the wilderness, finds friends there, and is later brought back by his community's leaders, who tell him he is needed.[^6] Using both passages, list the ways the daughter's story echoes her father's: what happens to each, where each goes, and who goes with them. Then list the ways her story ends differently. What do you notice?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Elle notes that the word for Jephthah's vow, *zavach*, is often softened to "sacrifice" but means "slaughter." When his daughter comes out to celebrate him, Jephthah says, "How could you do this to me?" which Elle hears as him centering himself as the victim. The daughter is never named in the text.[^6] What do you notice about how the father speaks and how the daughter responds? Why might it matter that she has no name?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Elle's research asks how religious interpretation can perpetuate harm. She cites the statistic that "85,000 women every year across the globe will be murdered, usually by their partner or family member," and notes "those numbers aren't different in the church." Yet she insists this is not importing a modern agenda, because "God has always been ahead of us in these things" and God's values are already in the text: "If we just pay attention, we can see it."[^7] Why do you think careful attention to hard stories like this one matters for the safety of real people?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Elle argues that the usual takeaway ("don't make stupid vows") lets most of us off the hook. The real question is communal: "How many of us have seen somebody get kicked out of our community, get sent away for whatever reason, whether it's their fault or not and we haven't gone out after them." Nobody from the daughter's community goes out for her.[^8] Without naming anyone, who might be in the "daughter position" in the communities you belong to, excluded, unnamed, or not pursued? What would it look like to go out after them this month?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Elle Grover Fricks reminds us that the words for God's commands carry pictures: a King, a Creator, a Judge, a treasure to guard, a Teacher whose instruction flows like a river. She then sets Jephthah's story beside his daughter's. Both were pushed out for something that was not their fault, and both went into the wilderness with friends; only the father was sought and brought home. The story asks less about our vows and more about our communities. God's values, Elle says, have always been ahead of us, there in the text if we pay attention.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, name one way you could go out after someone this week.

</div>
