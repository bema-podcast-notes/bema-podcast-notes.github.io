### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** John ate the scroll as Ezekiel did, sweet to receive and bitter to carry, and was told to prophesy again to many peoples and nations. The two witnesses, Moses and Elijah, pictured the whole community of Jews and Gentiles testifying, dying and being raised.

**This week:** Revelation's most vivid images arrive: a woman clothed with the sun, a great red dragon, and two beasts. The episode grounds them in the streets and marketplaces of Ephesus under Domitian.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode reads the woman of Revelation 12 on several levels at once (Mary and Jesus, Israel, the early church and Eve) and identifies the dragon with Rome and the larger reality of empire, which John calls "that ancient serpent called the devil or Satan."[^1] When the woman escapes on eagle's wings, John is echoing God's words at Sinai about carrying Israel out of Egypt.[^2]

Chapter 13 then comes into focus through archaeology. Domitian's gymnasium in Ephesus, the largest in the Roman world, was finished in four years instead of a century, and contemporaries called it "the beast rising from the sea." The mark of the beast was likely the stamp citizens received after offering incense to the emperor, without which they could not buy or sell.[^3] The repeated refrain is the heart of the passage: "This calls for patient endurance and faithfulness on the part of God's people."[^4]

### The Woman and the Dragon <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-woman-and-the-dragon}

*John draws a cosmic picture of conflict, and the notes suggest its central figure holds several identities at once.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Revelation 12:1–6 and 12:13–17 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes offer four ways to read the woman: Mary giving birth to Jesus; Israel, crowned with twelve stars for the twelve tribes, who gives birth to Messiah; the early church of Jewish and Gentile believers; and Eve, whose offspring Genesis 3 says would crush the serpent's head. Each reading fits some details better than others. Mary fits the birth but breaks down when the woman flees into the wilderness; the church fits the wilderness better than the birth.[^5] Go through the passage and note which details fit which reading. What happens when you let them all stand together, as the notes suggest?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** Read Exodus 19:4. When the woman is given "two wings of a great eagle" to escape into the wilderness, the notes say John is linking Rome to Egypt as another oppressive power and calling to mind God's pattern of deliverance, with the wilderness as a place of provision and protection.[^6] The dragon, meanwhile, draws on Genesis 3 and on the prophets' oracles against the proud kings of Tyre and Babylon.[^7] What would it have meant to believers in Ephesus to find themselves inside this old story of Egypt, the wilderness and the serpent?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> In the war in heaven, victory over the accuser comes "by the blood of the Lamb and by the word of their testimony," and the dragon's defeat means his time on earth is short.[^8] Read Revelation 12:10–12. When has telling the story of what God has done helped you stand against something that seemed to rage against you?

</div>

### Beasts from Sea and Earth <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#beasts-from-sea-and-earth}

*The second half of the vision comes into focus in the buildings and marketplaces of Ephesus.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Revelation 13:1–8 and 13:11–18 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes describe what John's readers could see from their own streets. Domitian's gymnasium was the largest in the Roman world and was finished in four years when it normally would have taken over a century; it dominated the view from the city center to the harbor, and contemporaries described it as "the beast rising from the sea." Pliny called Domitian himself "the beast of the sea, whose teeth drip with the blood of good Romans."[^9] The citizens of Ephesus built a temple to honor Domitian on a platform held up by twenty-four pillars, each depicting one of Rome's legal gods, so that the gods held up the emperor. It stood between the city's two marketplaces.[^10] Look at verses 16–17. What does the second beast control, and how would that have touched a believer's daily life?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** According to the notes, citizens were required to swear allegiance to the divine emperor, offer incense, and receive a stamp or mark that allowed them to trade. Without it, believers could not buy or sell in either marketplace. The notes call this "a present reality for John's original audience," not a future prophecy.[^11] On 666, the notes offer several layers: a number tied in Hebrew Scripture to the adversary, *gematria* (the practice of giving letters numerical values), "the number of a man" showing the emperor is merely human, and a pattern of falling short of seven.[^12] We have each heard many ideas about the mark and the number over the years. What were you taught, and how does the picture of a believer turned away from the marketplace for refusing incense sit alongside it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes ask a modern question: "What 'marks' do we carry? What 'beasts' do we serve in exchange for the ability to participate fully in our culture?" They list economic systems, consumer culture, nationalism, career advancement and social acceptance as possible forms of subtle idolatry.[^13] Which of these presses on you most, and what would refusing its mark cost you?

</div>

### Patient Endurance <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#patient-endurance}

*Right in the middle of the beasts, John stops to speak directly to the church.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Revelation 13:9–10 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** John's refrain is "This calls for patient endurance and faithfulness on the part of God's people." The notes say he encourages believers to stand firm, remember they have been here before in Egypt and Babylon, and "know how the story ends."[^14] They also describe the early church's weekly gathering as an act of resistance: every time believers declared "Jesus is Lord," they implicitly denied "Caesar is Lord."[^15] What would it look like for our own gathering to carry that kind of edge, without turning it into a political rally?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes list situations where patient endurance is needed today: job loss for ethical stands, social ostracism, economic disadvantage, family conflict over faith and professional limits for moral boundaries.[^16] Where is patient endurance being asked of you right now, and who in this room could help you stand?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> The woman is Israel, the church, Mary and Eve all at once, and the dragon is the old serpent wearing Rome's crown. She is carried on eagle's wings into the wilderness, as Israel was carried out of Egypt. In Ephesus the beast was something people could see from the harbor, and the mark was the stamp that let you buy bread in the marketplace. John does not promise his readers an escape from that pressure; he calls them to patient endurance and faithfulness, because they have been here before and they know how the story ends.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one "mark" you want to refuse, or one testimony you want to tell, this week?

</div>
