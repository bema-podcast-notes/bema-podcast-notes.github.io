### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Jesus told three parables aimed at the corrupt priestly leadership of the temple, and the episode drew a careful line between his patient years among the Pharisees and his one week confronting the Sadducees, asking whether we are missing the party God is already throwing.

**This week:** Groups that rarely agree on anything join forces to trap Jesus with questions. Each time, Jesus turns the question back on the questioners, and along the way he pushes the meaning of "neighbor" further than anyone expected.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode notes the unusual cooperation of Pharisees, Sadducees, and Herodians in trying to trap Jesus. The first question, about paying tribute to Caesar, is presented as a question about idolatry rather than taxes, because the tribute coin carried Caesar's image and a claim to divinity. The second, from the Sadducees, uses a woman married to seven brothers to mock the resurrection. The third asks for the greatest commandment, which the episode explains as a question about a rabbi's "yoke," the lens through which he reads Torah.

The episode then brings in Luke's parable of the Good Samaritan, where Jesus breaks the expected pattern of a story and echoes an account in 2 Chronicles 28 of Samaritans caring for captured people of Judah. It ends with Jesus' own question about how the Messiah can be both David's son and David's Lord. Some of this touches on allegiance to nation and party, and on what to do when commands seem to conflict, so expect honest differences in the room.

### Caesar's Coin <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#caesars-coin}

*A question about money turns out to be a question about worship.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 22:15–22 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Notice who comes together to ask this question. The notes say the cooperation of Pharisees, Sadducees, and Herodians "demonstrates the intensity of their desire to trap and discredit Jesus."[^1] The episode explains that the Greek word *kensos* ("tribute") refers not to general taxes but to a coin that served as proof of worship to Caesar, bearing his image and an inscription proclaiming his divinity.[^2] What do you notice about the questioners' opening flattery and Jesus' request to see the coin? How does knowing what was on the coin change the question?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes lay out the positions of the day. The Herodians saw worship as external motions; the Sadducees prized political stability; the Essenes withdrew to the desert; the Zealots said anyone who pays deserves death; Shammai's Pharisees called it idolatry; and Hillel's Pharisees argued that God had given authority to Rome, so buying the coin returned to Caesar what God already gave him.[^3] Which of these instincts do you recognize in yourself or in Christians today when faith meets politics?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The episode says Jesus follows Hillel's reasoning but goes further, in effect saying: "Give Caesar his coin, but don't you dare give him your worship."[^4] The notes ask what in our world demands that kind of devotion: "Nation, political party, economic system, social status—all may demand the worship we owe to God alone."[^5] Without pointing at anyone else, what in your own life competes most for the allegiance that belongs to God?

</div>

### Resurrection and the Weightiest Command <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#resurrection-and-the-weightiest-command}

*Jesus answers from his questioners' own Scriptures and names the lens through which he reads them.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 22:23–33 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q4:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The Sadducees, who did not believe in the bodily resurrection the Pharisees taught, tried to show its absurdity. The notes say Jesus reframed the question, since marriage is not a category in the resurrection, and then cited the Torah the Sadducees accepted, where God calls himself the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, the God "of the living."[^6] What do you notice about the way Jesus chooses his evidence?

*Read Matthew 22:34–40 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The episode explains that asking for the greatest commandment was asking for a rabbi's "yoke," his interpretive lens. Shammai paired love of God with keeping Sabbath (shorthand for obedience); Hillel paired love of God with love of neighbor. "Jesus clearly sides with Hillel, making love the weightiest commandment upon which 'all the Law and the Prophets hang.'"[^7] If love is the peg everything hangs on, what difference does that make when you read a hard passage?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes connect this to "weight," *kavod*, which helps when commandments conflict. Rahab lied to protect the Israelite spies, choosing "the weightier command to protect the foreigner," and she is honored in Hebrews 11. The notes compare this to people who hid Jews from the Nazis.[^8] Our traditions may weigh truth-telling and protection differently, and that is a fair thing to explore. Without needing to agree, when have you faced two good commands pulling in opposite directions, and how did you decide which carried more weight?

</div>

### Who Is My Neighbor? <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#who-is-my-neighbor}

*Jesus pushes love past even Hillel's boundary, and Scripture had already shown the way.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Luke 10:25–37 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes say Jewish parables commonly followed a template: a priest (who does wrong), a Levite (stuck in the system, who does wrong), and a Pharisee (who does right). Jesus replaces the Pharisee with a Samaritan, "the most hated group in Jewish society." The neighbor debate had clear lines: for Shammai, only fellow Jews; for Hillel, Jews and even Romans, "but NOT Samaritans."[^9] Who did the original audience expect to walk down the road third? Who would that third person be in a story told to your community today?

*Read 2 Chronicles 28:8–15 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The episode says the parable alludes to this passage, where Samaritans who had defeated Judah clothed the naked prisoners, gave them food and drink, healed them, put the weak on donkeys, and returned them to Jericho. "Scripture already demonstrated Samaritans caring for Jews at Jericho."[^10] List the parallels you notice between the two stories. What does it add that Jesus' shocking story was already in the Scriptures his audience knew?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Jesus ends the day with his own question: if the Messiah is David's son, why does David call him "Lord" in Psalm 110? The notes say this suggests the Messiah must be "more than merely a human descendant of David."[^11] Read Psalm 110:1. What do you make of the question, and why do you think it silenced the opposition?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes offer two possible centers to the structure of 2 Chronicles 28: the three Samaritan names, showing that those who act rightly are the true neighbors, or the question "Aren't you also guilty?", showing our common humanity.[^12] The episode adds that the priest and Levite probably had correct theology but failed in practice, so "Orthopraxy (right practice) matters as much as orthodoxy (right belief)."[^13] Who are the "Samaritans" in your life, the people you are tempted to place beyond the reach of God's love? What would neighborly love toward one of them look like this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Jesus' opponents set three traps, and each time he turned the question back on them. He told them to give Caesar his coin but never their worship, answered the Sadducees from the Torah they accepted, and named love as the weightiest commandment. Then he pushed love past even Hillel's boundary by making a Samaritan the hero, echoing a story where Samaritans had already cared for wounded people of Judah. The question was never only "Who is my neighbor?" but whether we will do what a neighbor does.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one concrete act of neighbor love you will practice this week, and toward whom?

</div>
