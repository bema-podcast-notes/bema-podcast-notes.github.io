### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We explored spiritual disciplines as ways of creating a space in our lives, trusting Marty's conviction that if we create the space, God will fill it.

**This week:** Marty takes a 10,000-foot view of Leviticus and asks what God meant when he called Israel a "kingdom of priests."

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Leviticus is the book many readers abandon. Marty offers a way in: if the Tabernacle is the honeymoon suite from the wedding at Sinai, Leviticus is the owner's manual for what to do with it. Its purpose is to explain the promise of Exodus 19:5–6, that Israel would be a kingdom of priests. That is not a kingdom with priests, but a whole nation of them. Marty names four things priests do: they put God on display, help people navigate their atonement, intercede on behalf of others, and distribute resources to those in need.

He also lays out Leviticus as a chiasm, with atonement for me on the front end, atonement for others on the back end, and the Day of Atonement in the middle. On that day the blood flows out of the Tabernacle instead of in, and the scapegoat carries sin into the wilderness. Marty also stresses that the book commands celebration and care for the oppressed. The episode closes with a set of honest questions about whether these priestly marks are true of us.

### Not a Kingdom With Priests <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#not-a-kingdom-with-priests}

*Leviticus explains what God meant by calling Israel a kingdom of priests.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** Two weeks ago we heard Exodus 19:5–6 as wedding language. Marty returns to it here as the promise Leviticus explains, with this note: "Not a Kingdom WITH Priests..."[^1]

*Read Exodus 19:5–6 aloud.*

What exactly does God say Israel will be? What is the difference between a kingdom with priests and a kingdom of priests?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty calls Leviticus "the Owner's Manual for what to do with the tent of the Tabernacle," which he identifies as the honeymoon suite from "Under the Chuppah."[^2] The book begins with atonement, and Marty summarizes its message this way: "You ARE right with me. You and I are good and what we are about to do is not about making you good."[^3] Why might it matter that the manual begins by settling the relationship before giving any instructions?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes describe the purpose of the holiness laws, such as eating kosher, as making Israel different. Holiness, *kadosh*, means set apart or different. Marty argues that the practical reasons often given for these laws were not the primary purpose; God gives them to set his people apart from those around them.[^4]

*Read Leviticus 19:1–2 aloud.*

How does reading "holy" as "different," rather than as moral perfection, change how you hear this command? What are some ways our traditions have tried to make us visibly different, and what has that communicated well or poorly?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Marty names four roles of a priest: put God on display, help people navigate their atonement, intercede on behalf of others, and distribute resources to those in need.[^5] Most of our traditions affirm in some form that all believers share a priestly calling, though we live it out in different ways. Which of the four do you see most clearly in your own church? Which one do you personally find hardest to live?

</div>

### Blood Flowing Out <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#blood-flowing-out}

*The Day of Atonement sits at the center of Leviticus.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Marty lays out Leviticus as a chiasm: Rituals of Redemption (1–7), Priesthood (8–10), Holiness Code (11–15), then the Day of Atonement (16), then the Holiness Code (17–20), Priesthood (21–22), and Rituals of Redemption (23–27). He notes that this holiday seems "very weird to be found right in the middle of this book."[^6] Throughout the year, blood from daily sacrifices flows into the Tabernacle, so that it "gets pretty full of sin." On the Day of Atonement, the blood flows the opposite direction, out of the Tabernacle, and onto a second goat, the scapegoat (*az-za-zel* in Hebrew), which is taken into the wilderness never to be seen again.[^7]

*Read Leviticus 16:20–22 aloud.*

What happens to the goat, and where does it go? What picture of sin and of God does this ritual paint?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Paul Patterson observes that the front half of the chiasm is about me approaching God as an individual, and the back half is about me helping others find their way back to God.[^8] Why do you think the book is shaped so that my atonement comes before my care for others' atonement?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes sum up the center of Leviticus this way: it is about God taking my sin and getting rid of it, and they go so far as to say "the Law was the gospel."[^9] Many of us were taught to see Leviticus as the clearest example of law in contrast to grace. What does it do to that picture to find the Day of Atonement at its heart? Where are you still living as though your own "tabernacle" is full, waiting for the blood to flow out?

</div>

### Party and Jubilee <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#party-and-jubilee}

*Leviticus commands celebration and ends with care for the oppressed.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Marty's summary of the festival chapters is blunt: "If you don't know how to take a break, I will destroy you." He paraphrases God as saying that you must celebrate, because if you don't, you will lose the plot of the story.[^10] The notes connect these festivals to the Tent of Meeting, the tent of *moed*, the Hebrew word for appointed seasons.[^11]

*Read Leviticus 23:1–4 aloud.*

How does it strike you that celebration is commanded here? What would change if your church treated its parties as seriously as its solemn days?

*Read Leviticus 25:8–10 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The final section covers the Sabbath year and the Year of Jubilee, when slaves are set free.[^12] Some readers are disturbed by passages in these chapters that seem to assign different values to different groups. Marty argues that these groups were often not valued at all in that society, and that God is bringing humanity forward a step further than they were at the time.[^13] How does that perspective help, or not help, as you read the hard parts of Leviticus?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The episode ends with questions drawn from the four priestly roles: Do we look different from those around us? Do we help people navigate their atonement? "Do we intercede on behalf of others or do we do everything we can to stand in the gap and block people from G-d's presence." Do we share what we have in abundance?[^14] Choose one of these questions and answer it honestly for yourself. What is one thing you will do this week in response?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Leviticus is the owner's manual for the Tabernacle, the honeymoon suite of a God who married his people. It begins by telling Israel they are already right with God, then shows them how to live as a kingdom of priests: putting God on display, helping others find their way back, interceding, and sharing. At its very center is a day when the blood flows out of God's house and the scapegoat carries sin into the wilderness. Then it commands a party and a Jubilee, because people who have been forgiven are meant to celebrate and to set others free.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: which of the four priestly roles is God inviting you into this week?

</div>
