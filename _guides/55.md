### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Zephaniah called Judah to *t'shuvah*, a U-turn back to its original design, and showed that idolatry and injustice cannot be separated. The book ends with God rejoicing over his people with singing.[^1]

**This week:** Isaiah 13–39 pronounces "woe" on nation after nation, including God's own people, because no one cares for others. Yet hope keeps breaking through.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode treats Isaiah 13–39 as "2 Isaiah," a voice distinct from the vineyard prophet of chapters 1–12. It explains that prophetic books are compilations of shorter oracles (the episode calls them "prophetic tweets") delivered on different occasions and arranged later, which accounts for their abrupt shifts in tone. Chapters 36–39 switch from poetry to historical prose that closely parallels 2 Kings, which the episode reads as an editor's bridge to the chapters that follow.[^3]

Two moments stand out. First, Isaiah 14:12–15 ("How you have fallen from heaven, O Morning Star") is often applied to Satan, but verse 3 says it is a taunt against the king of Babylon. Second, the woes are not random: God is frustrated that no one, pagan or covenant people, cares about others or works toward restoration. Between the woes, unexpected passages of hope appear, such as a feast for all peoples.[^4] Some of us learned to read Isaiah 14 differently, and some of our traditions talk about how Scripture was formed in different ways, so the questions ask what we notice and how we hold it, not who is right.

### How to Read a Prophet <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#how-to-read-a-prophet}

*Prophetic books work more like a collection of messages than a single essay.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes offer an analogy: imagine compiling someone's most important tweets over several years into a book. You would get topic shifts without transition, repeated themes from different angles, responses to news events without full context, and different audiences for different messages. An oracle about Babylon on a Tuesday and one about Damascus on a Thursday end up side by side.[^5] Using your answer from the opening, how might this change the way you read a prophet who seems to jump from judgment to praise and back again?

*Read Isaiah 35:8–10, then Isaiah 36:1–3 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes observe that chapters 1–35 are poetic oracles, chapters 36–39 are narrative prose closely paralleling 2 Kings, and chapter 40 returns to poetry.[^6] What differences do you hear between the two readings, in rhythm, in detail, or in what each is trying to do?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The episode reads this shift as the work of a redactor, an editor who compiled several prophetic voices and added historical narrative about Sennacherib's invasion and Hezekiah's reign to bridge a gap between the late Assyrian period and the exile. The notes list the questions this raises, including "How should we think about the authority of redacted texts?"[^7] Our traditions describe how Scripture came to us in different ways. How does the idea of inspired editors sit with you, and how, if at all, does it change the way you read your own Bible at home?

</div>

### The Morning Star and the King of Babylon <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-morning-star-and-the-king-of-babylon}

*A passage many of us learned as Satan's backstory has a named subject.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 14:3–15 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Before talking about what you were taught, look closely at the text. Who does verse 3 say this taunt is about? What claims does the speaker in verses 13–14 make about himself, and where does he end up in verse 15?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes explain that the language of ascending to heaven, raising a throne above the stars and sitting on the mount of assembly was standard rhetoric for ancient Near Eastern kings: Egyptian pharaohs claimed to be gods, Mesopotamian kings built ziggurats to ascend to the gods, and Roman Caesars declared themselves divine. The Latin translation of "Morning Star" as "Lucifer" led to a name for Satan being drawn from a passage about a human king, and the same thing happened with Ezekiel 28.[^8] Where did you first hear this passage connected to Satan, and what, if anything, is gained or lost when it is read as a taunt against Babylon's king?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say Babylon appears twice in the list of woes, both as the rising empire God will use and as a power destined to fall to the Medes.[^9] What does it suggest about God that the empire that looks unstoppable is the first one taunted?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes draw a lesson from Isaiah 14 about "the importance of reading scripture in context and not imposing later theological frameworks onto texts that have clear original referents." They also raise the question, "What does responsible biblical interpretation require when popular views conflict with context?"[^10] Think of a verse you have heard used in a way that may not fit its context. What would it cost you, or your church, to read it the way the context suggests?

</div>

### Woe to Everyone, a Feast for All <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#woe-to-everyone-a-feast-for-all}

*The woes fall on every nation, and so does the promise.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Isaiah 31:1–3 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes list the nations that receive woes in 2 Isaiah: Babylon, Assyria, Philistia, Moab, Damascus, Kush, Egypt, Edom, Arabia, Tyre, the whole earth, and in chapter 22, Jerusalem and Judah themselves. Chapters 30–31 condemn God's people for turning to Egypt for help, trusting in empire and military might rather than in covenant relationship.[^11] What does Judah rely on in these verses, and why might it be significant that God's people appear on the same list as the pagan nations?

*Read Isaiah 25:6–9 and Isaiah 32:1–8 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes offer three possible reasons for these sudden passages of hope: moments of temporary reprieve Isaiah witnessed, an intentional "sermon series on hope" to prevent total despair, or a vision of restoration beyond the immediate judgment.[^12] What do you notice about the feast in chapter 25 and the kingdom in chapter 32, and which explanation for their placement helps you most?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes summarize the core message this way: God is frustrated that neither pagan nations nor his people "care about others or work toward restoration," and his vision requires human partnership because he wants people to "help Him put the world back together." Isaiah 32:8 says the noble person makes noble plans.[^13] What is one noble plan, small and specific, that could make you a partner in putting some part of your world back together this month?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> 2 Isaiah is hard to read. Oracle after oracle pronounces woe on the nations, and then on Jerusalem, because no one cares for anyone but themselves and God's own people trust in Egypt instead of in him. Read in context, even the famous fall of the Morning Star is a taunt against an empire's king who tried to climb to heaven. Yet hope keeps breaking in: a feast for all peoples, death swallowed up, tears wiped away, a king who reigns in righteousness. God has not given up on finding partners to put the world back together.[^14]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, where do you feel the pull to trust "Egypt" instead of God right now?

</div>
