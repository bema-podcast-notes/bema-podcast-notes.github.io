### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We closed the Silent Years by asking how we associate with all five Jewish responses to Hellenism, keeping each group's strengths and guarding against each group's weaknesses.

**This week:** Before opening the Gospels, the session steps back to ask four questions of the Bible itself: when it was written, where, by whom, and why.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This episode explores how the Hebrew Scriptures came to be. The notes describe ancient Israel as an oral culture in which stories and teachings were memorized and passed on long before they were written down, and they present the common scholarly view that much of the Hebrew Scripture was written during or after the Babylonian exile.[^1] The episode also introduces the Documentary Hypothesis (the idea that the Torah draws on several distinguishable sources) and describes the Bible as a "prophetic table" where many voices are preserved in conversation.[^2]

The episode's most striking example is the book of Daniel. If, as many scholars think, Daniel was written in the mid-second century BC, it becomes an urgent critique of corrupt priestly leadership, and Jesus' use of "Son of Man" becomes far more provocative.[^3] Throughout, Marty insists that the Bible is God-breathed and that asking honest questions about its origins strengthens rather than weakens faith.[^4] People in the room will hold different convictions about these questions. The aim tonight is to understand what the episode argues and why it matters for reading the Gospels, not to settle every debate.

### An Oral World and a Scroll in the Temple <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#an-oral-world-and-a-scroll-in-the-temple}

*Long before most of Scripture was written down, it lived in memory and voice.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read 2 Kings 22:8–13 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes point out that the text speaks of "the scroll of the law," a single scroll, and suggest that if any book were kept in the temple it would be Leviticus, the priestly manual. They conclude that written texts were rare and that much of Israel's tradition was still oral even during the monarchy.[^5] What details do you notice in how the scroll is found and how the king responds? What do those details suggest about how familiar people were with written Scripture?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes explain that the five books of Moses were attributed to Moses "not because he physically wrote them, but because the teachings originated from his authority and leadership," and that oral transmission does not diminish reliability, since oral cultures developed sophisticated memorization techniques.[^6] How does picturing Scripture living first in memory and community change the way you imagine ancient Israel's relationship with it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> According to the notes, the exile created an urgent need to preserve traditions that had been passed on orally.[^7] What in your own faith was handed to you by voice (songs, sayings, family stories, a grandparent's prayers) that you would want written down before it is lost?

</div>

### Many Voices at One Table <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#many-voices-at-one-table}

*The episode argues that Scripture preserves more than one faithful voice, and that this is a gift rather than a problem.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes summarize the Documentary Hypothesis as four sources in the Torah, identified by vocabulary, names for God, emphases and style. They add that this "doesn't deny divine inspiration," that the final compiling could itself be part of the inspired process, and that chiasms spanning the whole Torah suggest divine orchestration.[^8] They also distinguish what inspiration means (God-breathed, carrying divine authority) from what it does not necessarily mean (written by a single author, or at the exact time of the events).[^9] When you hear the idea of multiple sources in the Torah, what is your first reaction? What in your tradition's teaching about inspiration shapes that reaction, and what in the episode's distinction helps or troubles you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes describe the Bible as a "prophetic table" and give an example from the Persian period: Esther works within the system, Ezra separates from foreign influence, Nehemiah rebuilds while keeping a distinct identity, and Malachi focuses on internal renewal. The notes call these "different faithful responses to the same situation."[^10] How does this compare with last week's five responses to Hellenism? Where else have you noticed Scripture holding more than one faithful answer side by side?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say that in this kind of Bible, "Readers are invited into the conversation rather than simply receiving dictation."[^11] How might your Bible reading this week change if you came to the text as a participant in a conversation rather than as someone receiving answers?

</div>

### Daniel and the Son of Man <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#daniel-and-the-son-of-man}

*The date of a book can change what its words mean, and what Jesus' words meant.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Daniel 7:13–14 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** What does the "son of man" figure receive in this passage, and from whom? What words describe the kingdom he is given? Report what stands out.

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes explain that common scholarly opinion dates Daniel to the mid-second century BC, during the Maccabean period. Read that way, the vision in Daniel 9:25–27 becomes an immediate critique of corrupt Hasmonean high priests, and "When Jesus calls himself 'Son of Man' before the priests, he's invoking a recent book that condemns corrupt religious leadership."[^12] Whatever you conclude about Daniel's date, what does this reading add to your sense of why Jesus' claim provoked such a strong reaction from the priests?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe Marty's journey from angry Bible college student to someone seeking restoration with the schools that trained him, and they sum up the episode's invitation this way: "ask when, where, who, and why about every biblical text you read."[^13] Which of those four questions do you most often skip? What would it look like to ask it of the next passage you read?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> This episode asks us to read the Bible as it actually came to us: remembered and recited by a people, written down in times of crisis, shaped by many hands, and preserved as a conversation rather than a single flat voice. Marty's conviction is that the Bible is God-breathed and authoritative, and that we honor it more by asking honest questions than by defending positions built on incomplete information. As we turn to the Gospels, the same four questions will follow us: when, where, who, and why.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one question about the Bible you are willing to keep holding, even without a final answer yet?

</div>
