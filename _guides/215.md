### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Ramban taught us to see the brothers' sin as a failure to see Joseph as a person, "the poor for a pair of sandals." We ended inside Joseph's head, wondering why his father never came.[^1]

**This week:** The Joseph story and the Jacob narrative come to their end. Judah offers himself in Benjamin's place, Jacob refuses to settle for Egypt, and Joseph has to choose which father and which identity will define him.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Rabbi David Fohrman notices that Joseph's life reverses itself at the midpoint: the pit, the stripped robe and the father who sends him away are answered in Egypt by being pulled out, newly clothed and sent for by Pharaoh.[^3] The episode asks whether that Egyptian rescue is the real fulfillment of Joseph's destiny or a comfortable substitute. Judah's plea for Benjamin, which the notes call "Judah's finest moment," breaks Joseph's resolve.[^4]

The second half follows Jacob's insistence on burial in Canaan and Joseph's risky request to Pharaoh.[^5] It ends with a story Rashi takes from the Talmud: at a place called Goren H'atad, kings who came to attack Jacob's funeral procession laid down their weapons when they saw Joseph's crown on the casket and added their own crowns to it.[^6]

### Pits, Robes and a Second Father <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#pits-robes-and-a-second-father}

*Joseph's story turns around in Egypt, and the episode asks whether the turnaround is the whole story.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes list the first sequence in Joseph's life (a dream told to his father, being sent away, stripped of his robe, cast into a pit) and its reversal in Egypt (pulled out of a prison the Hebrew calls a "pit," given new clothes, sent for by Pharaoh, asked to interpret dreams).[^3] Which pairing strikes you most, and what does the pattern suggest about how God was at work?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes observe that Joseph "effectively has two father figures": Jacob, who sent him away "leading to disaster," and Pharaoh, who "elevates him and treats him with honor."[^7] They add that this is not the Egypt of the Exodus but "an Egypt where Joseph thrives." Why might the second father be so hard to leave?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The notes ask whether Joseph's Egyptian rescue is true redemption "or merely a substitute for what God originally intended."[^3] Where are you tempted to accept a comfortable substitute for something God has called you toward? You can answer in general terms if you prefer.

</div>

### Judah's Finest Moment <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#judahs-finest-moment}

*The brother who once proposed selling Joseph now offers himself in Benjamin's place.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 44:18–34 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Listen to how Judah speaks about his father. The episode paraphrases the heart of his plea this way: "Father loved Rachel more than my mother, and he loves her children more than me. His very soul is bound up with Benjamin's, but I accept that. I'm not battling against that anymore. Please, take me instead."[^4] Which words in the biblical speech carry that meaning for you, and what is different about this Judah compared to the brother of Genesis 37?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes connect this moment to three rash declarations: Jacob's vow that whoever stole Laban's gods would die, Judah's confidence that the cup thief should die, and his earlier sentence on Tamar "before discovering he was the father."[^8] How might Judah's experience with Tamar have prepared him for this moment?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The notes say Judah "no longer fights against his father's favoritism but accepts it while still doing what is right," and that "sometimes reconciliation requires us to accept unfair situations while still doing what is right."[^9] Where is that wise, and where would it be unhealthy? Where in your own life might doing what is right, without bitterness, open a door that fighting has kept shut?

</div>

### A Crown on Jacob's Casket <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#a-crown-on-jacobs-casket}

*Jacob refuses to let the family's story end in Egypt, and Joseph has to choose which father he belongs to.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Genesis 47:7–10 and 47:27–31 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Notice Jacob's words to Pharaoh about his years and his request to Joseph about his burial. The notes read Jacob's insistence as "a theological statement: 'I don't want to settle for Egypt. You have to promise me you won't settle for Egypt.'"[^5] What do you hear in Jacob's description of his life, and why would burial in Canaan matter so much to him?

*Read Genesis 50:1–14 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** Joseph has to ask Pharaoh to let the funeral go elsewhere, and the notes say that "like Esther, Joseph must 'stick his neck out.'"[^10] Pharaoh responds with grace, and the text says "Chariots and horsemen also went up with him. It was a very large company."[^10] Then comes the Talmudic story Rashi uses to explain the name Goren H'atad: the kings of Canaan and the princes of Ishmael, both "dispossessed children," halt their attack when they see Joseph's crown on the casket and hang their own crowns beside it.[^6] Why might Joseph's choice to honor his father have such power over people who were themselves cast out?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes quote the hope this story holds out: "If Joseph can make it back, perhaps there's hope for them too."[^11] Marty also notices the language of "crowns and thorns" and suggests there is something profound in someone who lays down "their crown, comfort, power, and prestige for the sake of others."[^12] What connections do you see, and what do they suggest about how generational patterns of rejection get broken?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes ask "where we might lay down our own crowns, power, and privilege to participate in God's work of reconciliation."[^13] What is one "crown" you hold (a position, comfort, reputation or resource) that you could place at risk this month for the sake of a broken relationship?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Joseph's life reversed itself in Egypt, and Egypt was good to him. But Judah's humble plea broke Joseph's resolve, and Jacob refused to let the family's story end in a comfortable substitute. When Joseph risked his standing with Pharaoh to honor his father, the story says even the dispossessed laid down their weapons. Genesis ends not with another round of vengeance but with the hope that if Joseph can make it back, others can too.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one "Egypt" you sense God asking you not to settle for?

</div>
