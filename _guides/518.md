### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** In a pause from the series, Marty Solomon and Reed Dent described how *The Gospel of Being Human* was written and why they welcomed careful theological critique as "iron sharpening iron."[^1] Before that, Akhsah asked her father plainly for springs of water and received a double portion.[^2]

**This week:** The Women in Joshua & Judges series resumes with the first half of Deborah's story in Judges 4, read through Elle's translation of every Hebrew name.[^3]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Elle Grover Fricks walks Brent and Reed through Judges 4:1–16 using her own translation, which renders the meaning of each name instead of only sounding it out. Once the names are translated, a lot comes to the surface: "Unity" (Ehud) is dead, Israel is handed to a king called "Understanding" (Yavin) of a land called "Bent Low" (Canaan), and Deborah ("Honeybee") sits between "High Up" (Ramah) and "House of God" (Bethel).[^4]

The hosts discuss Deborah's mysterious title, "woman of torches," usually translated "wife of Lappidoth," reconsider whether Barak deserves his reputation as a coward, and look closely at the wording of Deborah's prophecy that Sisera will be sold "into the hand of a woman." The episode ends with Elle's "Biblical Womanhood Corner," a list of what this text shows a woman of God doing.[^5] Church traditions have read Deborah in different ways; the goal tonight is to look closely at what the text itself says and to listen well to one another.

**Opening Question:** Think of a name that carries a meaning or a story, perhaps your own, a child's, a family nickname, or the name of a place you love. What is the story behind it, and how does knowing it change the way you hear the name?

### Names That Tell the Story <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#names-that-tell-the-story}

*Translating the names in Judges 4 turns a list of places and people into commentary.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Judges 4:1–7 aloud. Then read it a second time, saying the meanings in place of these names: Ehud "Unity," Yavin "Understanding," Canaan "Bent Low," Hazor "Trumpeting," Deborah "Honeybee," Ramah "High Up," Bethel "House of God," Barak "Lightning," Kishon "Snare," Tabor "She will make pure."*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes put it simply: if you transliterate names without translating them, you lose much of what the narrative is saying.[^6] What did you hear on the second reading that you missed on the first? Point to one name that changed the meaning of its sentence for you.

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Elle notes that Hebrew has several words for strength. God often describes God's own strength with *oz*, the fierce strength of a she-goat, but the word here is *chazak*, "binding strength," the strength needed to hold someone down. Yavin did not struggle with Israel for 20 years; he held them down.[^7] The notes also say the oppressor's name is a lesson: the oppression helps Israel understand what they are reaping from their own evil.[^8] What do these two details add to your picture of Israel's situation at the start of the chapter?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** Nearly every other character gets a father or a hometown, but Deborah gets none, and the text never explains why she is allowed to lead.[^9] Elle also reads *eshet lappidot* as "woman of torches," a title, rather than "wife of Lappidoth," because the grammar is not the usual Hebrew construction for marriage, the word is plural, and it is feminine.[^10] Without needing to settle every question about women in leadership, what do you make of a narrator who offers no credentials for Deborah at all? In your own life, where do you look for credentials before you will listen to someone, and what might you miss because of it?

</div>

### Rethinking Barak <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#rethinking-barak}

*Barak's request can be heard as cowardice or as a desire for God's presence.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Judges 4:8–10 aloud, then Ruth 1:16–17.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Barak says, "If you walk with me, I will go; if you will not walk with me, I will not go." The notes observe that his words are short and blunt in Hebrew next to Deborah's long sentences, and that they echo Ruth's commitment to Naomi.[^11] Having read both passages, what similarities do you notice? How does hearing Ruth in Barak's words change the way you hear him?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q5:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The hosts offer three readings of Deborah's words that Sisera will be sold "into the hand of a woman." Elle points to word order: the Hebrew ends on *Adonai will sell Sisera*, so the emphasis falls on God acting, not on humiliating Barak. Reed, working from Robert Alter's translation, hears the pattern of God using what is low in the world's eyes to bring down the high. Brent suggests "it won't be your glory" may simply be information, with the woman detail offered out of generosity rather than as a rebuke.[^12] Which reading seems most persuasive to you, and what difference does the choice make for how we see Barak and Deborah?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Communities that oppose women in leadership have often called Barak a whiny coward. The hosts push back: if David had said to Samuel, "I won't go into battle without you," no one would mock him, and Barak may simply recognize that God's presence and voice are with Deborah.[^13] They close hoping to share Barak's wish never to be separated from God's presence.[^14] What are you facing right now where you would want that kind of company before you go? Who in your life helps you hear God's voice? Answer as fully or as briefly as you like.

</div>

### Has Not Adonai Gone Before You? <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#has-not-adonai-gone-before-you}

*God goes ahead of Barak and wins the battle, and Deborah models a direct, generous kind of leadership.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Judges 4:14–16 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** Deborah's command to Barak includes the question "has not Adonai departed before you?" Elle's paraphrase: "He left 30 seconds ago. Are you going to follow him?"[^15] The notes add that God throws both the chariots (the strike force) and the camp (the supplies and support staff) into confusion, so this is a lost war and not just a lost battle.[^16] In these three verses, what do you notice about who acts, who follows, and how complete the outcome is?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Elle's list of what biblical womanhood looks like in this passage includes: offers no qualifications for her authority, places herself near the presence of God, is sought out for wisdom and justice, leaves room for others to do their jobs, is not afraid to admonish, does not have to soften bad news for other people's egos, goes on adventures, and commands men ("Arise!").[^17] Elle stresses that these traits are not exclusive to women, and that the list matters because many Christian communities teach a narrower picture.[^18] Which items fit easily with what your tradition has taught about women in Scripture, and which would take more conversation? What do you think shapes those differences?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** Elle compared how she might deliver Deborah's message (a long voice memo wrapped in apologies and caveats) with Deborah's single direct sentence, noting that you can speak plainly out of a secure identity and still be kind.[^19] She also noted that Deborah lets Barak call up the tribes and lead the army instead of saying, "You asked for A, so I'll do B and C too."[^20] Which do you need more practice in this week: saying the plain thing kindly, or leaving room for someone else to do their part? Where will you practice it?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Read with its names translated, Judges 4 says that unity is dead, Israel is learning the hard way under "Understanding," and the crooked kingdom of "Bent Low" is holding God's people down. Into that situation steps Deborah, a honeybee and a woman of torches, sitting between the high place and the house of God, giving no credentials and needing none. She summons "Lightning," reminds him of God's command and promise, walks with him on a hard march, and tells him the plain truth: God has already gone ahead, so get up. Barak, often mocked, may simply be a man who would not go into battle without the voice of God beside him.[^21]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, name one place this week where you sense God has already gone ahead of you.

</div>
