### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** The parable of the net closed Matthew 13. The fish stood for the nations, the angels do the sorting at the end, and God's people are freed to bless everyone the net catches.

**This week:** We leave the parables for three stories: Jesus rejected in Nazareth, John the Baptist killed by Herod, and five loaves and two fish feeding a crowd of thousands.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode begins in the synagogue at Nazareth, where Jesus reads from Isaiah and gives a one-sentence sermon. His hometown is proud of him until he reminds them that Elijah and Elisha were sent to outsiders, and then admiration turns to rage. Next comes the death of John the Baptist, and the quiet detail that Jesus withdrew to a solitary place when he heard.

The heart of the episode is the feeding of the 5,000. Drawing on the rabbinic idea that everything a rabbi does is intentional, the hosts and Dr. Gambino read the numbers in the story symbolically and present Jesus as a second Moses who gives Torah to his disciples to hand out to the people. The episode closes with a challenge: believing the miracle happened is easy, but do we trust that what Jesus provides is enough?

### A Prophet in His Hometown <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#a-prophet-in-his-hometown}

*Jesus' own town welcomes him warmly and then turns on him.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Luke 4:16–22 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The episode explains that rabbis had no special role in synagogue services; the one synagogue where a rabbi would read was his hometown synagogue, where he was a member. The passage Jesus read (Isaiah 61:1–2) does not appear in any known reading list from the period, and the sermon after the readings (the *dereshah*, from the same root as *drash*) typically lasted only 30 to 90 seconds. Jesus' entire sermon was a single sentence (Luke 4:21).[^1] Walk through what Jesus does, step by step. What do you notice about his one-sentence sermon, and about how the room first responds?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q2:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say the name Nazareth comes from the Hebrew *netzer* ("shoot" or "branch"), recalling Isaiah 11:1, and that other Jews thought it arrogant for a village to call itself "Shootville," as if claiming to be the Messiah's hometown.[^2] How might that town identity have shaped what Nazareth hoped for from one of its own?

*Read Luke 4:23–30 aloud.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q3:** The crowd turns when Jesus mentions Elijah sent to a widow in Zarephath and Elisha healing Naaman the Syrian. The notes say Jesus was declaring that his mission was for outsiders, not "hometown treatment," and they frame this as a challenge to examine "whether they want God's blessing only for their 'in-group' or whether they embrace God's mission to all people."[^3] Why do you think those two stories turned pride into fury so quickly? Where might you be hoping for hometown treatment from God?

</div>

### Grief in a Deserted Place <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#grief-in-a-deserted-place}

*A prophet is killed, and Jesus goes away to be alone.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 14:1–13 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q4:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The episode notes that Herod Antipas enjoyed listening to John even while John confronted him for marrying Herodias, his brother Philip's wife, which Leviticus 18 forbids. Herod was afraid to harm John because of John's popularity and his informal *s'micha* (recognized authority as a teacher).[^4] What do you notice about what Herod wants and what Herod fears in this story?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** When Jesus heard of John's death, he withdrew to a solitary place. The notes call this an often-overlooked sign of Jesus' humanity and his need to grieve: "The humanity of Jesus gives us permission to be fully human in our own emotional lives."[^5] Where do you make room to grieve, and what makes that hard for you?

</div>

### Bread for the Whole Community <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#bread-for-the-whole-community}

*Nothing a rabbi does is random: the feeding of the 5,000 is a lesson for the disciples.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Matthew 14:13–21 aloud, then read John 6:5–6 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The episode stresses that everything a rabbi does is instructional and intentional, pointing to John's comment that Jesus asked Philip where to buy bread to test him, already knowing what he would do.[^6] Using the list you brought (or reading the passage again), name every number in Matthew 14:13–21. What do you notice? What does John 6:6 suggest about who the real audience of this miracle was?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Dr. Gambino points to several echoes of Exodus: Moses going up and down the mountain, receiving the tablets, assembling the whole community of men, women and children, and organizing people into groups of fifty as Jethro advised in Exodus 18.[^7] Which of these echoes can you hear in the feeding story (including the other Gospels' accounts if you know them)?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes offer a symbolic reading of the numbers: 5 loaves for the five Books of Moses, 2 fish for the two tablets of the Law (together 7, the complete Law), 5,000 men as the complete community of Torah people, and 12 baskets left over for the twelve tribes. Jesus takes the Law, gives it to his disciples, and they distribute it to the people, like a second Moses.[^8] What does it mean that the disciples are the ones handing it out? What would this story have taught them about their role?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The episode passes on a challenge from Ray Vander Laan: "All the demons on that hillside believed Jesus did a miracle that day." The real question, the notes say, is whether we trust that Jesus' teaching and provision are sufficient for daily life.[^9] Where are you most tempted to worry that there will not be enough? What would trusting Jesus' provision look like there this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> In Nazareth, Jesus' neighbors wanted a hometown hero, and he pointed them to a Phoenician widow and a Syrian general. When John was killed, Jesus withdrew to grieve, showing us that being fully human is not a failure of faith. Then on a hillside he took five loaves and two fish, gave them to his disciples, and watched them feed a whole community with twelve baskets to spare. The episode suggests Jesus was teaching his disciples that his way of reading Torah is more than enough for everyone. Even the demons believe the miracle; the question is whether we trust it.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one area where you want to trust that there is enough this week?

</div>
