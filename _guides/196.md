### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** The church took up "the handle end of the sword," yet the monks preserved texts, served the sick, and built practices that made space for God.[^1]

**This week:** East and West disagree over how to preserve truth, icons or creeds, and the disagreement helps lead to the Great Schism of 1054.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The episode explores a deep cultural difference behind the conflicts of these centuries. Western Christianity tends to communicate truth through words, definitions and creeds; Eastern Christianity through pictures, images and story.[^3] In the Iconoclastic Controversy, Western eyes saw prayer before icons as idolatry, while the East understood icons as windows to the divine and "books" for people who could not read. Both were trying to solve the same problem: how to preserve biblical truth in a world with no printing press, widespread illiteracy, and worship in languages most people did not speak.[^4]

The episode also introduces Charlemagne, the "Father of the West," and reconsiders the so-called Dark Ages, which the notes say were not entirely dark.[^5] The Great Schism, traditionally dated to 1054, divided the Roman Catholic Church and the Eastern Orthodox Church through a gradual process.[^6] Finally, Marty offers his own critical definition of "Christendom" as Christianity grounded in empire and asks whether it has ever ended.[^7] The notes insist that neither East nor West has a monopoly on truth.[^2]

### Two Ways to Preserve Truth <span class="guide-time">30 min</span> {#two-ways-to-preserve-truth}

*East and West both wanted to keep the biblical story alive; they disagreed about how.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Exodus 20:4–6 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes say the Western critique of icons was that the practice "seemed to violate commandments against graven images."[^4] As you listen to the commandment, what exactly does it forbid, and what reasons does it give? Report what you notice. Then consider: what would a Western Christian need to believe about icons to see them as a violation of this text?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The Eastern position, as the notes describe it, is that icons "are not objects of worship but windows to the divine," that the worshiper "venerates the God behind the story the icon represents," and that icons serve as "books" for the illiterate.[^4] Brent describes his visits to Eastern Orthodox churches as "very moving experiences."[^8] What makes this explanation persuasive, or not, to you?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes list the practical realities of the time: no printing press, widespread illiteracy, worship in Latin or Greek, and the Jewish educational system that had preserved Scripture orally "removed from Christian practice."[^4] If you were a church leader then, responsible for an illiterate farming village, how would you help people remember the story of Jesus?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes suggest that just as Easterners do not worship icons, Westerners do not worship their doctrinal statements, "though the line may be fine," and they name a Western tendency toward "doctrinal idolatry."[^9] Consider this diagnostically for whatever tradition you come from. Where could a beloved statement, creed or way of explaining the faith become something we trust in place of God? How would you notice if it had?

</div>

### Charlemagne, the Schism, and the Dark Ages <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#charlemagne-the-schism-and-the-dark-ages}

*These centuries were neither as dark nor as unified as they are often imagined.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes describe Charlemagne (reigning about 768 to 814) as uniting the Western church "in ways not seen since Constantine" and urging intellectual and spiritual revitalization; they also note his work "can be viewed with either admiration or critical eyes."[^5] Meanwhile ordinary people lived with illiteracy, worship in a language they did not understand, and dependence on clergy for religious knowledge.[^10] What would faith have felt like for an ordinary farmer in that world?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes say the "Dark Ages" were not entirely dark at all, citing James Hannam's *The Genesis of Science* and figures like Boniface, and suggest this era set up the scientific revolution.[^10] Why do you think the label "Dark Ages" has been so durable?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The Great Schism is traditionally dated to 1054, but some historians argue it was not finalized until 1472; it was "an ongoing process" rather than a single moment, and the Western church felt "shock and terror at losing half the Christian empire."[^6] Many of us have lived through a church split or a slow drifting apart. Without naming anyone, what did you learn about how divisions actually happen, and what would you do differently if you saw one beginning now?

</div>

### Has Christendom Ended? <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#has-christendom-ended}

*Marty asks whether the empire mindset ever left the church.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes compare dictionary definitions of Christendom (a worldwide body of Christians, or church and state as two aspects of one society) with Marty's sharper view: Christianity "with imperial undertones," grounded in empire since Constantine, summed up in the attitude "Give me the handle end of the sword. Now we're in charge."[^7] What difference does the definition you choose make for how you tell church history?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes raise the possibility that "even when church and state separated," the imperial mindset remained.[^7] Where do you think Christendom has truly ended, and where might it continue in new forms?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** Where in your own life (your conversations, your hopes for the church, your reactions to culture) do you notice the wish to be "in charge," and what would it look like this week to set that wish down?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> This week we saw the church divide along a line that ran deeper than doctrine: two cultures with two ways of keeping the story alive. The East painted the story so people could read it in images; the West wrote it down so it could be defined and recited. Each saw danger in the other, and each carried its own risk of trusting the tool more than the God it pointed to. Charlemagne united the West for a time, the schism split East from West, and Christendom's imperial mindset raises the question of whether it ever really ended. The invitation is humility: neither half of the church holds all the truth.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one thing you could learn from a Christian tradition very different from your own?

</div>
