### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** On the way to Sinai, God tested Israel's heart at Marah, not to grade them but to know them. The midrash says the lesson was about community: the lame, the elderly, the sick and the marginalized drink first.[^1]

**This week:** The second and third tests examine the soul and the "very," and the episode shows how all three tests line up with the Shema's call to love God with all your heart, soul and might.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

The notes describe three tests drawn from Deuteronomy 8: heart (*levav*, the will), soul (*nephesh*, the whole self), and might (*meod*, literally "very," everything you have). The test of the soul comes through the manna and the water from the rock. The people cross a line from asking God for help into demanding that he prove himself. At Horeb, God tells Moses to strike the rock while God himself stands before him, a picture the notes read as God taking the blow on behalf of his people. The test of *meod* comes when Amalek attacks the weak at the back of the line, and Moses holds up his staff as a banner pointing beyond himself to God.[^3]

Running through all three tests is the same question the notes keep returning to: how does this community treat those who cannot care for themselves?[^4] Empire builds on the backs of the weak; *shalom* circles the wagons around them.

**Opening Question:** When you travel with a group (a family trip, a hike, a tour), who usually ends up at the back, and who, if anyone, goes back for them?

### Manna and the Test of the Soul <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#manna-and-the-test-of-the-soul}

*The question shifts from "Will you wait on God's word?" to "Will you give God your whole self?"*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Exodus 16:13–18 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes say the word *manna* means "What is it?" They also report a rabbinic reading of "some gathered much and some little": it was not about hoarders versus trusters, but about community members gathering for those who could not gather for themselves (the sick, the elderly, the disabled), so that everyone had exactly what they needed.[^5] What do you notice in verses 17–18, and how does the rabbis' reading change the picture?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes describe *nephesh* not as a spirit inside a body but as the whole person: body, spirit, emotions, intellect and consciousness, everything that makes "you" you. They distinguish testing God, which sounds like "God, you don't get me unless you do X," from seeking his help in honest struggle, as Gideon did with his fleece.[^6] Where would you draw the line between asking God for reassurance and putting him to the test?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes suggest modern believers can test God without realizing it: "I'll follow God if He provides X." Putting God to the test, they say, amounts to refusing to give him your soul.[^7] What condition have you been tempted to place on God (perhaps the one you brought tonight), and what would it look like to offer him your whole self instead?

</div>

### The Rock at Horeb <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#the-rock-at-horeb}

*At Horeb, the one who is tested steps into the place of judgment.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Exodus 17:1–7 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes say Moses' cry here (*tsa'aq*) is a cry of literal fear for his life. They also highlight that God stands "before" Moses (*paniym*), between Moses and the rock, while the elders watch.[^8] Where exactly does God say he will be standing, who is watching, and what is Moses told to do?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes explain that the word for "strike" (*nakah*) means to strike to kill, the same action Moses once used against the Egyptian. With God standing between Moses and the rock, the elders see what looks like Moses striking God himself. The notes summarize the message as "Strike me instead of judging the people," and see a God who is consistent from Genesis through the New Testament: "He always provides the sacrifice."[^9] Remember that the people have just demanded proof. What does this scene reveal about how God responds to people who are testing him?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> One set of notes ends by asking whether we will trust God's provision in our own wilderness moments "rather than demanding proof."[^10] Think of a time you were the one demanding proof. How might the picture of God taking the blow at Horeb change the way you come back to him?

</div>

### A Banner and the Back of the Line <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#a-banner-and-the-back-of-the-line}

*The test of "very" asks what we do with our strength when the weak are under attack.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Exodus 17:8–16 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes say Amalek attacked from behind, targeting the sick, the weak and the slow at the back of the group. They explain that Moses' raised staff was not magic but a banner (*nissi*), like Egyptian temple flags that pointed to the god behind them. "The banner isn't the point - it points to what lies beyond."[^11] What do you notice in this passage about what happens when Moses' hands go up and down, about who helps him, and about the name he gives the altar?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q8:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes connect *meod* to Genesis 1:31, where creation is *tov meod* ("very good"), and to the Shema, where we love God with all our *meod*: our talents, time, strength and everything we can produce. They also mention Gideon choosing soldiers who cupped water in their hands to drink quickly and make way for others.[^12] Read Deuteronomy 6:4–5. What would it mean to love God with all your "very," and which of your resources is hardest to hand over?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes contrast two kinds of community. Empire communities put the weak and marginalized on the fringes or at the bottom and build on their backs. *Shalom* communities circle the wagons around their vulnerable members and put them in the protected center. "A community's obedience is measured by where they place the marginalized."[^13] In our church or in this group, where do the vulnerable end up, at the edge or in the center? What is one concrete step that would move someone closer to the middle?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> On the road to Sinai, God tests his people three times, and the tests climb: the heart (will you wait on my word?), the soul (will you give me your whole self?) and the "very" (will you spend your strength on others?). The people fail more than once, yet at Horeb God steps in front of the rock and takes the blow himself. Every test circles back to the same place: the weak at the well, the ones who cannot gather manna, the stragglers Amalek attacks. To love God with heart, soul and might turns out to mean protecting the vulnerable.[^14]
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one part of your "very" (time, money, skill, strength) you could spend on someone at the back of the line this week?

</div>

#### Looking Ahead {#looking-ahead}

**[Ep. 22 — Under the Chuppah](/guides/22)** (44:44): Israel arrives at Mount Sinai, and the episode reads Exodus 19–23 through the steps of an ancient Eastern wedding. The betrothal cup, the groom preparing a room in his father's house, the consecration, the shofar, the chuppah (the cloud covering the mountain), the ketubah (the Ten Commandments) and the wedding gifts (the Law) all find parallels at Sinai. It also reframes the golden calf as unfaithfulness in the middle of the wedding, and God's new tablets as remarkable mercy.[^15]

**What to bring next week:**
- Read Exodus 19:1–19 and mark any detail that sounds like a wedding to you.
- Bring one wedding tradition from your family or culture and what you think it means.
