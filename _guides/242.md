### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** Marty and Becky Solomon read Paul's instruction not to exasperate our children as a call to nourish rather than provoke, and to parent toward who we want to be rather than away from what we fear.[^1]

**This week:** A four-part series on spiritual abuse begins. Marty shares what he learned from campus minister Leah Schrumpf and introduces a Jewish understanding of repentance.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

This week and the next three take up spiritual abuse. Some of us may carry painful experiences from churches, ministries or families, and some of us may not yet have words for what we went through. Sharing is always optional, you may pass on any question, and what is shared here stays here.

Marty begins with the posture older Christians often take toward Generation Z and with Leah Schrumpf's insight that "we are born to see our face reflected in the face of another."[^3] He argues that ministry must move from information to transformation through relationship, and he admits that his own four years of pastoral training included no teaching on trauma.[^4] The heart of the episode is Leah's warning that treating people as commodities and "variables to be manipulated" is a form of violence, even when the goal is evangelism or church growth.[^5] Marty closes with five steps of repentance drawn from Rabbi Danya Ruttenberg and Jewish tradition, and with his own commitment to walk them.[^6]

### Seeing Our Face in Another <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#seeing-our-face-in-another}

*Marty starts with posture: how we look at the people we hope to serve, especially younger generations.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The notes describe a common posture toward young people marked by "disdain, eye-rolling, and dismissiveness," and they record the conviction that "this generation has come to be what it is because of the context that we created for them." Participation trophies, for example, were a reaction by Gen X parents against win-at-all-costs sports culture.[^7] What changes when we ask what we created, instead of asking what is wrong with them?

*Read Romans 12:1–2 aloud, then Romans 12:9–16.*

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes point out that the call to "be transformed by the renewing of your mind" sits in Romans 12–14, which focuses on "loving one another and spiritual family," and conclude that "transformation happens through loving relationships, not through information alone."[^8] Looking at the verses that follow 12:2, what kinds of actions and relationships does Paul describe? What does that context add to how you hear verse 2?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty says that in four years of Bible college (2001–2005) he received "zero training on trauma or being trauma-informed," and the notes call being trauma-informed "not optional for effective ministry in the current cultural context."[^4] Why might well-meaning leaders without that training "inadvertently cause harm"?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Leah says "we are born to see our face reflected in the face of another," and the notes call this reflection "the essence of discipleship."[^3] Who might be looking to you, right now, to see themselves reflected with empathy rather than judgment?

</div>

### Commodities and Variables <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#commodities-and-variables}

*Leah's central warning is that people can be dehumanized by ministries that mean well.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** Leah says, "If we see people as commodities, and if we treat them as variables to be manipulated, we are executing violence on them, because we're not treating them as human beings, we're treating them as something else. We're removing their humanity."[^5] Why do you think she uses a word as strong as "violence"? What does treating a person as a variable actually look like?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q6:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes list ways this happens: when "saving souls becomes more important than loving people," when "the Great Commission supersedes the Greatest Commandment," and when people become "tally marks rather than individuals." The goal might be church growth, doctrinal purity, organizational success or evangelism numbers.[^5] Why might a goal that is genuinely good make it harder, not easier, to notice when people are being used to reach it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q7:** The notes observe that leaders who focus on conversion numbers, attendance figures and "decisions for Christ" may believe they are honoring God's mission while "unconsciously treating people as variables in a success equation."[^9] Sharing the gospel matters deeply in every tradition represented here; the episode's question is about ordering and posture. Without naming any church, where have you seen (or felt) programs or numbers shape the way people were treated? In your own relationships this week, how could you make sure someone knows they are not a project?

</div>

### Repentance in Five Steps <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#repentance-in-five-steps}

*Marty draws on Jewish tradition to show that repentance is a journey, not a sentence.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The five steps are: confess to God and to those affected; acknowledge the negative impact; make restitution (with the Torah principle of adding 20%); outline future behavior changes; and actually change. The notes say step two "is often skipped entirely" because it requires vulnerability and deep empathy, and that this framework "explains why many public apologies feel hollow."[^6] Why do you think the second step is so hard to take? What does an apology sound like when it is missing?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q9:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> *Teshuvah* (repentance) means "to return or make a U-turn," referring to "actual behavioral change, not mere acknowledgment of wrongdoing."[^6] How does picturing repentance as a U-turn differ from the way you were taught to think about it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The episode closes with Marty acknowledging "his own participation in systems that perpetuate spiritual abuse" and committing to the process himself.[^10] Think privately of a harm you have caused, large or small; you do not need to share it. Which of the five steps would be your next one, and what would taking it look like?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> This week we began a hard conversation by looking first at ourselves. We are born to see our face reflected in another, and leaders who look at people with disdain, or count them as tally marks, strip away something essential to their humanity. Transformation happens in loving relationships, not through information alone. When we cause harm, repentance is more than confession: it means naming the impact, making things right, planning a different way, and actually returning.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one person or relationship where you want to practice seeing a face rather than a number this week?

</div>
