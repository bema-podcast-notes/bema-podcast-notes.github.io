### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We saw that God placed Israel at the crossroads of the earth to bless the nations, called them to the *shephelah* rather than the safe mountains, and watched the tribe of Dan flee its hard assignment.

**This week:** Israel settles into the land without a king, and the book of Judges follows a repeating pattern. The episode asks whether that pattern is really about human sin or about God's redemption.

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

Many of us learned Judges as a "sin cycle": peace, sin, punishment, crying out, a judge, deliverance, and around again. The hosts argue that this reading puts the spotlight on human failure and misses the main character. They reframe the pattern as a "redemption cycle" that highlights God's patience, pursuit and compassion as his people learn to follow him without a king.[^1] The whole book flows from one failure at its start: a generation grew up that did not know the Lord or what he had done.[^2]

The episode makes a key distinction. God has endless patience for people genuinely trying to follow him and getting it wrong, but he must act when his people become the "anti-story," working against his purposes and oppressing others.[^3] It also notes a decline across the book, from Othniel and Ehud to Samson, and touches on the hard stories of Jephthah's vow and the Levite's concubine, while insisting that even at the end, God does not abandon his people.[^4]

### A Generation That Forgot <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#a-generation-that-forgot}

*Judges begins not with an enemy but with a memory that was not passed on.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Judges 2:10–19 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** If you did the preparation, share the steps you wrote down. The notes say this passage is the foundational description of the pattern, and that God raised up judges and "had compassion on them as they groaned under those who oppressed and afflicted them."[^5] Look at the verbs in verses 16–18. What does God do, and what moves him to do it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes observe that "The entire Book of Judges flows from this failure to remember and transmit the story," and connect it to earlier BEMA themes: *zachor* ("remember"), passing stories to the next generation, and standing stones.[^6] Why do you think the generation that crossed the Jordan, who saw the miracles, failed to pass the story on?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Two weeks ago we talked about standing stones in Joshua 4. Judges 2:10 is what happens when no one asks, or no one answers. What makes it hard in families and churches today to tell the stories of God's faithfulness?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** Think of the people coming up behind you: children, grandchildren, younger believers, new members. What is one story of God's faithfulness you have never actually told them, and what would it take to tell it?

</div>

### Sin Cycle or Redemption Cycle? <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#sin-cycle-or-redemption-cycle}

*The same pattern can be read with the spotlight on Israel or on God.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Judges 3:7–11 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** This short story of Othniel contains the whole pattern in five verses. The notes list the familiar six steps of the "sin cycle."[^7] Go through the passage and mark each step. Then ask: in how many of these verses is Israel the subject, and in how many is God the subject? What do you notice?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** The hosts argue that traditional teaching reads Judges through a "Genesis 3 lens" (focusing on the fall and sin) when it should be read through a "Genesis 1 lens" (focusing on God's commitment to his good creation). The notes say this shift moves the focus "from human failure to divine faithfulness" and "from wrath to compassion."[^8] Many of us were taught the sin-cycle reading, and it has real value. What does each lens help you see in Othniel's story, and what might each lens miss?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes track the years of bondage and rest for each judge: Othniel 8 and 40, Ehud 18 and 80, Deborah 20 and 40, Gideon 7 and 40, Jephthah 18 and 6, and Samson 40 and 20, "only half deliverance."[^9] What story do these numbers tell on their own?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The episode distinguishes two kinds of failure. God has endless patience for "People genuinely trying to follow Him but making mistakes," but must act against "Becoming the 'anti-story' that works against God's redemptive plan" and "When His people become oppressors rather than crying out as the oppressed."[^10] The notes say this should encourage believers to "Take risks in following God without fear of judgment for honest mistakes."[^11] Which is harder for you to believe: that God is patient with your honest mistakes, or that God takes seriously the moments we work against him? How would living from this distinction change one decision you are facing?

</div>

### Hard Stories Near the End <span class="guide-time">20 min</span> {#hard-stories-near-the-end}

*Judges does not get easier as it goes, and the episode does not pretend it does.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Judges 11:29–35 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes describe Jephthah's vow as a conflict between two commands: keeping vows made to God, and not murdering, loving others.[^12] Look at the vow in verses 30–31 and his words in verse 35. What do you notice about how Jephthah speaks, and what does he seem to believe about God and about his own words?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q10:** The notes call this "a wrestling match about the 'weightier law' - which commandment takes precedence when two conflict?" They say most would argue his daughter's life should come first, and they leave open whether Jephthah carried out the sacrifice or found another way.[^13] When two good commitments collide, how do you decide which one carries more weight?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q11:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> The notes describe Samson as "the nadir of the judges": a Nazirite who did not keep his vow, from the tribe of Dan that failed to settle its land, who "Never lived up to the calling God had for him."[^14] How does knowing Samson came from Dan change the way you hear his story after last week?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q12:** Judges ends with the story of the Levite's concubine (Judges 19–21), which the notes say parallels Sodom and Gomorrah and shows how far Israel had fallen. "Yet even after this depth of degradation, God does not abandon His people."[^15] Where in your life, or in the life of someone you love, do you most need to hear that God does not abandon his people?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> Judges begins with a generation that did not know the Lord or what he had done, and the book that follows could be read as one long record of failure. The hosts invite us to read it another way: as a record of a God who hears his people groan, raises up deliverers again and again, and keeps showing compassion while they learn. That patience has a limit, but the limit is not honest mistakes. It is the moment God's people become the oppressors. Even as the judges decline and the stories grow darker, God does not walk away.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence each, going around the room: what is one place where you want to take a risk in following God, trusting his patience with your mistakes?

</div>
