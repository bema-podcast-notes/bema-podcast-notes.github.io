### Where We Are {#where-we-are}

{% include guide-series.html %}

**Last week:** We began the spiritual abuse series with Leah Schrumpf's warning that treating people as commodities and variables is a form of violence, and with five steps of repentance that go far beyond confession.[^1]

**This week:** Marty and Brent reflect on a conversation between Rachael Clinton Chen and Adam Young from *The Place We Find Ourselves* podcast, asking what spiritual abuse is and why it is so hard to see.[^2]

### About This Session {#about-this-session}

As last week, sharing is optional, you may pass on any question, and what is shared here stays here. None of these questions assumes anything about anyone's experience.

Rachael Clinton Chen defines spiritual abuse as "the use of spiritual power or authority to coerce, shame or bring harm," an exploitation of God's authority to control "bodies, relationships, autonomy and personhood."[^3] The episode stresses that it can happen anywhere power exists, from pulpits to small groups to homes, that fear and shame fuel it, and that people often recognize it only years later.[^4] Marty and Brent look at how Jeremiah 17:9 is misused to teach people not to trust themselves, why theological systems hold such power over us, and why purity culture shows that good intentions do not excuse harm.[^5]

The episode's most practical test is a single question: "Can you question this?" Marty and Brent invite listeners to question them too, and they frame the whole response through Micah 6:8: act justly, love mercy, walk humbly.[^6]

### Naming What Spiritual Abuse Is <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#naming-what-spiritual-abuse-is}

*The episode starts with a definition and then widens the lens beyond senior leaders.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q1:** The definition names four areas of control: bodies (including sexual abuse), relationships (who people may associate with, date or marry), autonomy ("removing people's ability to think for themselves"), and personhood ("robbing people of their full humanity and self-determination").[^3] Which of the four had you not thought of as spiritual before? Why might naming all four matter?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q2:** The notes insist that "power itself is not inherently problematic" and that the critical question is "What do we do with power?" Power can be used to control and strip away, or to "equip, lift up, empower, and give more to others," and "greater power requires greater accountability."[^7] Think of someone who used power over you in the second way. What did that look like?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q3:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty presents purity culture of the 1980s and 1990s as an example of well-intentioned harm: it aimed to protect the sacredness of sexuality and marriage, yet the notes say it "stripped girls and women of autonomy" and "imposed morality without equal accountability for men."[^8] Why can good intentions make harm harder to name, both for the people harmed and for the people who caused it?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q4:** The notes say spiritual abuse can happen even in a small group with no official leader, where someone more educated, articulate or well-read can "lord their education and experience over others."[^9] That includes groups like ours. What habits or agreements would help keep this group a place where anyone can question anyone, including whoever is leading that week?

</div>

### Why It Is So Hard to See <span class="guide-time">25 min</span> {#why-it-is-so-hard-to-see}

*Fear, shame and the need to belong can keep people from seeing harm while they are inside it.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Jeremiah 17:5–10 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q5:** The notes say Jeremiah 17:9 ("The heart is deceitful above all things and beyond cure") is "frequently ripped from context and used to teach people not to trust themselves but to trust the system, institution, or person in authority."[^10] Listening to the whole passage, what is Jeremiah contrasting, and in whom does he say trust should rest? How does the context change the way verse 9 sounds?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q6:** Victims often cannot recognize spiritual abuse "while experiencing it," because it strips away autonomy and self-trust, identity becomes "deeply intertwined with the abusive community," and the system provides belonging. Fear and shame are felt in the body: "a pit in the stomach, recoiling, even nausea."[^4] Rachael adds, "Our gut is a truth-teller. It doesn't mean that it's perfect."[^11] Why might learning to notice that inner sense of "There's something off" be such an important part of healthy faith?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q7:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Adam Young says "a theological system is an ordering of the cosmos and your place in it," which explains its power even when wrong. Rachael remembers thinking, "I could probably do without much of this community but I don't want to lose Jesus."[^12] What happens when a person can no longer tell the difference between Jesus and the community that taught them about him?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q8:** The notes record Rachael Clinton Chen's observation that "Jesus never forces or uses His power to direct people," but typically asks permission or responds to requests.[^10] If someone told you they had only recently recognized that a past church experience had harmed them, how could you respond in a way that respects their personhood the way Jesus did?

</div>

### Justice, Mercy, Humility <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#justice-mercy-humility}

*Marty and Brent frame the whole response with one familiar verse.*
{: .guide-note}

*Read Micah 6:6–8 aloud.*
{: .guide-note}

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q9:** The notes say some people "will need to leave situations to heal, some will need to stay to help change destructive systems, and some leaders will need to repent and fundamentally change how they lead," all guided by acting justly, loving mercy and walking humbly.[^6] Looking at Micah's questions in verses 6–7 and the answer in verse 8, what does the passage suggest God cares about more than religious performance?

</div>

<div class="guide-q guide-q-optional" markdown="1">

**Q10:** <span class="guide-tag">Optional</span> Marty asks leaders, "Have you ever purposely leveled a sermon at people that you knew just had the right amount of sting to it?"[^13] Most of us lead something: a class, a family conversation, a meeting, a Bible study. Where might the temptation to use a little "sting" show up in your own leading?

</div>

<div class="guide-q" markdown="1">

**Q11:** The notes call "Can you question this?" "the most important practical takeaway."[^14] Which of the three postures in Micah 6:8 (acting justly, loving mercy, walking humbly) feels most like your next step in this conversation, and what would it look like this week?

</div>

### Closing <span class="guide-time">15 min</span> {#closing}

> This week we named spiritual abuse plainly: the use of spiritual power or authority to coerce, shame or harm. It can happen anywhere power exists, it is fueled by fear and shame, and it often hides from the very people it is harming. Power is not the problem; what we do with it is. Jesus did not force people, and healthy communities make room for questions. Micah 6:8 gives all of us, whatever our story, a way forward: act justly, love mercy, walk humbly.
{: .guide-summary}

<div class="guide-q guide-q-closing" markdown="1">

**Closing Question:** In one sentence, what is one way you want to help make room for questions in the communities you belong to?

</div>
