### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** BEMA Episode 499: The Pillar of Text  
**Hosts:** Marty Solomon and Brent Billings, with guest Reed  
**Focus:** The BEMA pillar of "Text": the nature of Scripture (2 Timothy 3:16; Genesis 2:7; Isaiah 55:11), categories of truth, and practices for reading the Bible

This episode continues the Session 10 series on BEMA's four pillars: Text, Community, Discipleship, and Wrestling. Reed gives his own take on the pillar of Text without first hearing Marty's thoughts on it. He starts with the word *theopneustos* ("God-breathed") in 2 Timothy 3:16 and asks whether Scripture is "breathed into" or "breathed out by" God, and what each reading implies. From there he argues that the Bible exists mainly to convey the *meaning of human experiences with God*, not to deliver "data packets" of information. He uses newspapers, poetry, satire, fiction, and personal stories to show that there are different categories of truth. He also brings in Meir Sternberg's framework of history, ideology, and aesthetics. He then traces how he has read the Bible at different stages of his life, and closes with practical tips: watch for first-order language, read with Jesus as the lens for interpretation, read in community, follow rabbit trails, and judge your reading by its fruit.

### Key Takeaways

- **"The text" vs. "the Bible":** Reed grew up treating the Bible as a manual for getting saved and living a godly life ("Basic Instructions Before Leaving Earth"). Calling it "the text" invites a different posture.
- ***Theopneustos* has two possible readings:** "Breathed into" (life-giving, like Adam in Genesis 2:7) or "breathed out by" (divine origin, like Isaiah 55:11). Both are true, and holding them together guards against extremes.
- **Scripture is both human and divine:** Adam was shaped from dirt and brought to life by God's breath. In the same way, the Bible is a very human, earthy work that God's breath makes life-giving.
- **The Bible conveys meaning, not just data:** Marty compares "strip-mining" the Bible for truth units to the Kennecott copper mine scarring the landscape outside Salt Lake City.
- **Doctrine starts as experience:** Quoting Frederick Buechner, every doctrine "was a human experience first."
- **There is more than one category of truth:** A newspaper, a poem, satire, and fiction can all be true in different ways. Judging one by another's standards is a category mistake.
- **Transformative truth is often artistic:** The truths that change us at a heart level usually come through story, poetry, and art rather than "spreadsheet" facts.
- **Three "faders" on a soundboard:** History, ideology/theology, and aesthetics are mixed at different levels across different parts of Scripture.
- **Reading the Bible changes over a lifetime:** Paul's imperatives in high school, Ecclesiastes in college, Genesis and Revelation in his 30s, and complex characters at 42, with Jesus running through all of it.
- **Judge a way of reading by its fruit:** If your way of reading produces fear and destruction rather than love, question it. When certainty and love conflict, choose love.

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. Popular Assumptions About "Inspiration"

- Ask most people what "the inspired word of God" means and they will say *the Bible is always right and never wrong*. Inspiration, inerrancy, and infallibility get bundled together.
- Common mental images of how this works:
  - **Dictation:** The authors write down exactly what they hear.
  - **The "glove" image:** God reaches through the authors' hands and uses them like a glove.
- Reed's view: these ideas hold a kernel of truth, but the packaging around them may go "a bridge too far."

#### 2. *Theopneustos*: Breathed Into or Breathed Out?

- **2 Timothy 3:16** ("the other big 3:16") is translated in different ways:
  - NIV: "God-breathed"
  - ESV: "breathed out by God"
  - NRSV: "inspired by God"
  - KJV: "given by inspiration of God"
- **Word parts:** *Theos* (God) + *pneustos* (breathed), related to *pneuma* (breath, wind, spirit).
- **Reading 1: "Breathed into" (inspired)**
  - Echoes Genesis 2:7: God breathes into the man and he becomes a living being.
  - The emphasis falls on the breath's **life-giving, generative function**: "All Scripture is life-giving."
  - Possible misuse: treating the Bible as a mostly human text that God sprinkles with "potential," so readers must pick out which parts are inspired.
- **Reading 2: "Breathed out by" (exhaled)**
  - Echoes Isaiah 55:11: "my word that goes out from my mouth."
  - The emphasis falls on **divine source and origin**: "All Scripture has a divine nature."
  - Possible misuse: imagining the Bible appeared *ex nihilo* and must be flawless, so that any "imperfection" makes the whole thing useless.
- **Marty's "source" view:** Scripture is inspired because of *where it came from*, and that does not push him toward literalism. If God chose to write a poem, it is an inspired poem.
- **Reed's "both/and":** Use the creation of Adam as the model. God gets down in the dirt and shapes something material and human, then breathes divine life into it. The Bible is a human thing and a divine thing at once.

#### 3. What Is the Bible For? Data vs. Meaning

- **The "strip-mining" approach:** Readers extract "truth units" from the text, like the open-pit copper mine near Salt Lake City.
- **The alternative posture:** Engage the Bible the way people engage great stories like Tolkien's *The Lord of the Rings*. The Bible is not the same kind of thing as Tolkien, but the posture is similar.
- **If God dictated it:** Then the Bible is just "information God needs me to know."
- **If humans wrote it out of real encounters:** Then it is real people, in real places and real struggles, trying to convey the *meaning* of their experiences with God.
- **Buechner's insight:** The doctrine of Christ's divinity began "not in the word processor of some fourth-century Greek theologian" but in the experience of untheological people who knew Jesus of Nazareth and found something happening in their lives that had never happened before.

#### 4. Theological Imagination (Marty's Grad Work)

- Marty's current course, *Theological Imagination*, frames theology as **interpreting divine experience**.
- Revelation comes through three sources: **Scripture, tradition, and experience**.
- Finite people conveying an experience of the infinite will always fall short. Even if a source is perfectly inerrant, our ability to understand, communicate, and interpret it is limited.
- This does not reduce Scripture's authority. It shows that the subject is bigger than anything we can say about it.
- Reed adds that beneath every system of theology sits a deeper **religious imagination**, a way of holding things together. People focus on "getting their doctrinal bricks in a row" and neglect it, yet the Bible works mainly by appealing to that imagination.

#### 5. The Bible as a Library: Categories of Truth

- **The 9/11 example:**
  - *Periodicals section:* The Wall Street Journal of September 12, 2001, with flight numbers, times, and souls aboard. If it printed the wrong flight number, it would be in error.
  - *Poetry section:* Scott Cairns's poem "September 11," which opens with an epigraph from Exodus about the pillar of cloud and fire and suggests that "we became the cloud and the fire." Calling a poem "in error" means something completely different.
- **The core problem:** "We only have one category and we call it true/false." The way we usually define "inspired" seems to rule out any other category.
- **Fear:** If the Bible fails in the factual category, we assume it cannot be true in any sense.

#### 6. Meir Sternberg's Three Faders

From *The Poetics of Biblical Narrative*. Sternberg was a contemporary of Robert Alter and also wrote on the Bible as Hebrew literature.

1. **History:** What happened? This is where most readers spend all their attention.
2. **Ideology/Theology:** What does the experience mean? What conviction does it express?
3. **Aesthetics:** What style and presentation best communicate it?

- Picture these as sliders on a soundboard. Different passages mix them at different levels, and none is ever simply at zero.
- This replaces the polarized question "fact or fiction?" with a question about how the three interact.
- Brent's joke: on a real soundboard, "zero" is actually turned up quite a bit.

#### 7. First-Order Language

- This is a term from psychology, which Reed learned through his wife, a professional counselor.
- It refers to words that seem so obviously true to a group that nobody questions them, until they lose their meaning. Reed calls this "Christianese."
- Examples: *faith*, *righteousness*, "by grace you have been saved."
- These words are learned early, repeated constantly, and emotionally loaded, so they become empty vessels unless we examine them on purpose.

#### 8. Jesus as Hermeneutic (Greg Boyd)

- When a passage is theologically or morally hard, go back to the person of Jesus.
- Jesus fulfills Torah by interpreting it correctly and embodying what it truly intends.
- Ask: "How would Jesus interpret this? How would he encourage his disciples to live it out?" This takes imagination, but it is a reliable anchor.
- Marty connects this to John's gospel: if Jesus *is* the Word, it makes sense that Christ meets us in every expression of the word.

### Examples & Applications

1. **The Onion vs. Fox News (2020 stimulus checks):** Fox News reported, factually, that the checks were delayed so the president's signature could appear on them. The Onion ran the headline "President Seeks to Stimulate Economy by Sending Rare Autographed Photo of Himself to Every American." Nothing in the satire is factual, yet it exposes the deeper truth of hubris far more pointedly. It "hits a lot different."
2. **Briggs and *The Return of the King*:** Watching Frodo and Sam say goodbye at the Grey Havens, Reed's son Briggs, then about 10, felt tears coming and dropped to do push-ups to avoid the emotion. He met real truth about loss and friendship through a story that is entirely made up.
3. **Brent becomes a father:** Brent recorded the exact second his son Darius was born on his Apple Watch. But he says he truly became a father when he crouched by the warming table and made eye contact with his son. That is "not factually true in any way" and yet it is the real truth.
4. **The birth-of-a-child test:** Conveying the meaning of a child's birth through time, weight, and heart rate alone falls far short. You have to reach for language that goes beyond facts without contradicting them.
5. **Karl Barth on the serpent:** Asked whether the serpent in Genesis actually spoke, Barth reportedly answered, "Madam, it doesn't matter whether or not the serpent spoke; all that matters is what he said."
6. ***Wake Up Dead Man: A Knives Out Mystery*:** Father Jud concedes to detective Benoit Blanc that religion "is storytelling," then asks: "Do these stories convince us of a lie, or do they resonate with something deep inside us that's profoundly true, that we can't express any other way except storytelling?"
7. **Sports as experience:** Even something as trivial as a playoff win cannot be fully conveyed by the box score.
8. **"Lost at sea" Bible reading:** Reed admits that as a teenager he waded through confusing passages hunting for "dry ground" verses that made sense (for example, "dead in your trespasses but alive in Christ"), then skimmed passages about meat sacrificed to idols.
9. **Practical reading tips:**
   - Notice first-order language and ask what you actually mean by familiar words.
   - Use Jesus as your lens for interpretation when passages are hard.
   - Read in *havruta*: read aloud with friends, where no question is off limits and the conversation itself is the fruit.
   - Follow rabbit trails. If a thinker resonates with you, read the people *they* read. Use footnotes, bibliographies, and suggested readings. Marty's friend Peter traces ideas back to their common sources, "six degrees of Kevin Bacon" style.
   - Judge your reading by its fruit. Does it produce fear or love? Persistent damage to relationships, your mind, or your faithfulness signals that the way of reading is unsound.
   - When certainty conflicts with loving your neighbor or enemy, choose love and re-examine the certainty.

### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

- **The Greek of 2 Timothy 3:16:** Study *theopneustos* and its few parallels in ancient literature, and compare the major translations.
- **Doctrines of Scripture:** The history and differences of inspiration, inerrancy, and infallibility, including the Chicago Statement and its critics.
- **Meir Sternberg, *The Poetics of Biblical Narrative*:** Especially the chapter on history, ideology, and aesthetics. Also Robert Alter, *The Art of Biblical Narrative*.
- **Frederick Buechner:** *Wishful Thinking*, *Telling the Truth*, and his idea that the Bible is "a book about the way things are."
- **Greg Boyd:** Jesus as the lens for interpretation and the "cruciform" reading of hard Old Testament texts.
- **Theological imagination:** How Scripture, tradition, and experience work together, and Garrett Green or Walter Brueggemann on imagination.
- **Genre and truth:** Satire and parody in Scripture (Jonah, for example), Hebrew poetry, and apocalyptic literature.
- **Scott Cairns's poetry** and its use of biblical imagery.
- **J.R.R. Tolkien's "On Fairy-Stories"** and the idea of sub-creation, which Reed mentions jokingly.
- **First and Second Samuel** as a study in the costs of keeping power through violence and deceit (Reed's upcoming BEMA material).
- **Complex biblical characters:** David, Moses, Rahab, Peter, and Jonah.
- **Fear-based vs. love-based hermeneutics:** 1 John 4:18 ("perfect love casts out fear") applied to how we read Scripture.

### Personalized Summary

This episode is an invitation to loosen your grip on a single, factual kind of truth. Reed shows that Scripture is "God-breathed" in two ways: it comes from God, and it gives life. Like Adam, it was shaped from something human and earthy and filled with divine breath. When the Bible is read as a record of real people trying to convey the meaning of their encounters with God, poetry, satire, story, and history can all carry real truth. A moment of eye contact with a newborn, a goodbye at the Grey Havens, or a headline in The Onion each show that the truths that change us rarely fit on a spreadsheet. Sternberg's three faders give you a practical tool for this, and Reed's tips give you habits: question familiar words, read through the lens of Jesus, read with others, follow the rabbit trails, and test your reading by its fruit. If your way of reading leads to fear, re-examine it. If it leads to love of neighbor and enemy, you are reading in the way of Jesus.
