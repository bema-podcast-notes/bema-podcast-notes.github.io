### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** 507: Pillars of Discipleship, as Seen by Reed Dent  
**Hosts:** Brent Billings with Reed Dent (Marty Solomon is mentioned but not present)  
**Focus:** Discipleship as slow, attentive, communal formation. Passages drawn on include Matthew 18:21–35, 1 Corinthians 3:6, Philippians 2:5–11, John 10:10, John 14:27, and John 15:11

Brent sits down with Reed Dent, who has spent 18 years in campus ministry with Campus Christian Fellowship (CCF). They look at discipleship as one of the "pillars" of the faith. Reed doesn't offer a step-by-step program. Instead he reflects on how discipleship actually works in his life with college students. His main claim is that discipleship is slow. It means paying humble attention to who a person really is instead of who we think they should be. It happens within a shared life that builds trust over time. Reed uses two main images: Eugene Peterson's butcher who submits to the grain of the meat, and a blacksmith's forge where the iron must be brought up to heat before it can be struck. He ends by naming the fruit he hopes discipleship produces: following the self-giving way of Jesus, becoming fully alive, and partnering with God to bring shalom into the world.

### Key Takeaways

- **The work of God is a slow work.** Reed borrows this from Pierre Teilhard de Chardin's well-known prayer. Discipleship isn't a conveyor belt that turns out "fully baked" disciples on schedule.
- **Discipleship means bearing with one another** through stages of instability, stumbling, and learning the same lessons again and again.
- **Submit to the material at hand.** Like a butcher working with the grain of the meat, a mentor has to learn who a person actually is instead of forcing their own expectations on them.
- **The goal is not cloning.** Jesus's disciples kept very different personalities and skills. What they shared was his self-giving spirit, offered in love for the world.
- **Friendship, conversation, and spiritual practice** are the three legs of the stool that discipleship stands on at CCF.
- **Discipleship works like a forge.** Shared time and ordinary life bring the relationship "up to temperature." Then moments of vulnerability and receptivity open up, and a word of wisdom, rebuke, encouragement, or call can land.
- **Imitation has value too.** Modeling practices ("this is how we pray, fast, serve, handle conflict, play") is a real part of discipleship, but it should be offered as an invitation, not forced.
- **Discipleship runs on wisdom more than program.** Reading plans and curricula are fine. Care and cultivation outside "spiritual" times matter more.
- **The fruit of discipleship** is following Jesus's self-emptying love, becoming fully alive (joy, peace, abundant life), and partnering with God to bring about shalom, including through our work and careers.

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. Discipleship as a Slow Work

Reed defines discipleship simply: "how we together become more the people that God wants us to be... more in line with the way of Jesus." After nearly two decades in campus ministry, he has seen that this kind of growth can't be hurried.

- **The Teilhard de Chardin prayer:** The BEMA podcast has quoted this Jesuit priest's prayer several times. It urges trust in "the slow work of God" and accepts that growth passes through "stages of instability."
- **The pizza-oven picture:** Reed contrasts real discipleship with a Domino's conveyor belt, where a program goes in at one end and a finished disciple comes out the other.
- **Bearing patiently:** Much of mentoring is "bearing patiently with one another in the progress we're not making." It means being with people as they trip, fall, and relearn.
- **Practical point:** The first job in discipleship is *not to rush the work*. That means not leaning too hard on our own plan for what will happen.

#### 2. Submitting to the Material at Hand (Eugene Peterson)

Reed draws on *The Pastor*, Eugene Peterson's memoir. As a teenager, Peterson learned to cut meat in his father's butcher shop.

- Meat has a **grain**, a natural way it is meant to be cut. Hacking at it by force of will gives messy, wasteful results.
- The butcher still uses skill and a knife. But skill works best when it *yields to the reality* of the material instead of fighting it.
- Peterson calls this **humility**: "to yield to the thing rather than imposing your will on it."
- **Applied to people:** It is hard to let go of expectations about who someone "should" become. Often, Reed admits, the hidden expectation is *"they should be like me."* Fruitful discipleship asks with curiosity:
  - Who is this person in front of me, and how has God made them?
  - What are their passions and desires?
  - What are their weaknesses?
  - What do they do better than me, and what do they do worse?
- **God gives the growth:** Reed points to 1 Corinthians 3:6: "I planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the growth." The mentor's job is to notice where God is already growing something and to tend it wisely, in a way that fits where it is *now*.

#### 3. Essential Likeness, Not Personality Cloning

Brent connects this to Marty's description of first-century discipleship, where a talmid (disciple) followed a rabbi to become like him.

- None of the Twelve became like Jesus in *every* way. Peter, John, James, and Thomas kept very different personalities and skill sets.
- The aim is likeness in the *essential spiritual elements*, not in interests, temperament, or habits.
- Reed: the goal isn't "a bunch of Reed Dents walking around who read out loud to each other." It is to grow **the spirit of Christ, which gives itself for the sake of the world in love**, and then to encourage each disciple to express that spirit in a way true to who they are.

#### 4. The Three-Legged Stool: Friendship, Conversation, Practice

Reed covered this in more depth in an earlier community episode. At CCF, community and discipleship go together: "We are a community of discipleship." The three legs are:

1. **Friendship.** Genuine friendship is "the best context for pursuing God."
2. **Conversation.** Honest talk about all kinds of things, spiritual and otherwise.
3. **Spiritual practice.** Worship, reading the text aloud together, taking part in the sacraments, and observing seasons like Lent and Advent.

Brent observes that he wouldn't have counted a Sunday night gathering of 200 students as "discipleship." Reed's framework explains why it counts: those settings build the friendship that the deeper work depends on.

#### 5. Discipleship by Imitation

Reed acknowledges a model associated with Marty, who is known for strict habits, early mornings, and disciples who lived alongside him:

- **"Do after me."** This is how we pray, read Scripture, memorize Scripture, and what time we get up.
- It reaches beyond spiritual disciplines to **how we ask and explore questions, how we serve, how we handle conflict, and even how we play.**
- The point is that some practices really are good for people, apart from personality. Some students embrace them right away. Others go through healthy discomfort before they adjust. Some never take to them, and "it's not my job to make them want to be there."

#### 6. The Forge: Heat, Then Strike

This is Reed's central metaphor.

**Stage 1: Heating up (building the conditions)**
- The whole of life is the setting: evenings by lamplight, morning coffee, late-night games, ultimate frisbee, pickup basketball, meals, weddings, funerals, parties, and fires.
- These times build **friendship, trust, and understanding**. You come to know someone not just from their answers to Bible questions but from how they react when they're fouled in a basketball game or think someone is cheating at a board game.
- Like heating iron, this can't be rushed.

**Stage 2: The iron is hot (moments of openness)**
- Moments come of **vulnerability, receptivity, and honesty**. Some call these "thin places," where people see each other more deeply and sense that God is present.
- These moments are **mutual**, not just teacher to student. Reed says he benefits from his students as much as they benefit from him.

**Stage 3: Strike (the word spoken)**
- A word or gesture is offered: wisdom, encouragement, rebuke, inspiration, or confession and absolution.
- It can be "a word spoken into the depths of someone" about their life, not just tomorrow's next step.
- **Biblical example:** In Matthew 18:21–35, Peter asks how many times he must forgive his brother. Reed suggests he may literally mean Andrew. Jesus sees "the iron is hot" and tells the parable of the unforgiving servant.

**Timing varies.** Sometimes this happens in one weekend. Sometimes it takes years of regular meetings. When the transformative moment does come, it turns out the transformation had been growing all along.

#### 7. Seeing Things for What They Are (Robert Farrar Capon)

Reed describes *The Supper of the Lamb: A Culinary Reflection*, part cookbook and part theology. Capon is the same author the show discussed for his work on the parables.

- Capon criticizes treating things only by what they *mean* or what they can *do for us*, which is a utilitarian, "how can I use this?" attitude.
- His famous multi-page reflection on simply **sitting with an onion** shows the practice of paying attention to a thing for what it is.
- **Applied to discipleship:** People aren't blank slates waiting for a 12-month curriculum. Good discipleship starts with careful attention to "What is a Brent Billings? What is a Reed Dent?"
- When people truly see each other, "it almost doesn't even matter what the program is," because what comes out of real presence and listening is so holy.
- **Example from Reed's own life:** At a small retreat in his home with Brent, Marty, and others, the evening became "really real" and transforming. It didn't come from a planned agenda. It came from slowing down and lingering long enough to notice.

#### 8. The Fruit of Discipleship

Reed sums up three hoped-for outcomes:

1. **Following the way of Jesus.** This is the mind of Christ from the hymn of self-emptying in Philippians 2:5–11. It means giving ourselves in love for the world, out of real life rather than grim duty.
2. **Becoming fully alive.** Life to the full (John 10:10), joy made complete (John 15:11), and peace given by Jesus (John 14:27) come to live in us.
3. **Partnering with God.** This means going into the world to help bring about shalom: flourishing, justice "rolling down" (Amos 5:24), and being fruitful and multiplying in every sense, not only by having children.

**Discipleship through work:** Christians change the world not only by becoming missionaries or priests. They can also mentor and apprentice others in their own field, showing what kingdom-shaped work looks like there.

### Examples & Applications

- **Parenting.** Raising children shows "what a long con discipleship is." It takes patience, attention, and slowness over many years. Brent adds that children are always watching, whether we mean to disciple them or not.
- **The workplace.** Reed cautions against walking up to a coworker and asking, "Do you want me to disciple you?" Instead, start by paying quiet, intentional attention to who they are and what's going on with them, long before spiritual topics come up.
- **The McDonald's example (Brent).** A new employee watching how you treat customers is already learning from you. Being intentional in ordinary tasks "brings the temperature up on the forge," which can lead to deeper chances to disciple.
- **Church calendar practices.** CCF invites students into Lent and Advent with fasting and prayer. Students who have never done these things are welcomed in, and nobody is pressured.
- **Reading plans with care.** A shared daily Bible reading plan is fine. The real question is whether care and concern for the person extend beyond those "spiritual" times.
- **Know each other through play.** How someone handles losing, being fouled, or suspecting someone of cheating tells you a lot about their character. Recreation is part of real formation.
- **Fitting your rhythms.** Brent and Reed joke that they prefer 11 p.m. conversations to Marty's 4 a.m. routine. Discipleship can take very different forms depending on temperament and season of life. Reed notes his best conversations with students happen late at night.
- **Mentoring at work.** Professionals can disciple younger people in their field by showing how their calling can serve the kingdom.

### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

- **Teilhard de Chardin's "Patient Trust" prayer.** Its theology of slow, unfolding growth and its links to Scripture.
- **Eugene Peterson's *The Pastor*.** His broader ideas on pastoral humility, vocation, and "working with the grain."
- **Robert Farrar Capon's *The Supper of the Lamb*.** Its theology of creation, delight, and seeing things as they are, beyond their use.
- **The talmid–rabbi relationship.** How first-century Jewish discipleship compares with modern mentoring models (see Marty's episode in this series).
- **The parable of the unforgiving servant (Matthew 18:21–35).** Its setting, the Peter–Andrew possibility, and what it teaches about forgiveness in community.
- **The Philippians 2 hymn.** Kenosis (self-emptying) as the pattern for Christian character.
- **"Thin places."** Where the idea comes from in Celtic Christianity and how it applies to relationships.
- **Vocation and discipleship.** How work and professional mentoring can become kingdom work.
- **Program vs. wisdom.** The strengths and limits of structured discipleship curricula compared with relational, attentive approaches.
- **The earlier community episode.** Reed's fuller account of friendship, conversation, and practice.

### Personal Summary

This episode lifts the pressure off discipleship and also deepens it. Reed Dent's main point is that people aren't projects. They are gifts to pay attention to. Discipleship isn't about putting someone through a program or making copies of ourselves. It is the slow, patient, humble work of learning who a person really is and joining in what God is already growing in them.

Shared meals, games, late-night talks, and ordinary days slowly bring a relationship "up to temperature." Then, sometimes after years, a moment opens where a word of truth can land. Whether we're parents, coworkers, friends, or mentors, the call is the same: slow down, linger, notice, and trust that God gives the growth. The hoped-for result is people who live out the self-giving love of Jesus, who are more fully alive, and who join God in putting the world back together.
