### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** BEMA 503: The Pillar of Community, Through Reed Dent's Eyes  
**Hosts:** Marty Solomon and Brent Billings, with guest Reed Dent  
**Focus:** The BEMA pillar of community; 1 John 2:6; Luke 6:46; 1 Corinthians 12 (the body and its many parts); 1 Samuel 18 (Saul's envy of David)

This episode is part of a series where Marty asks his co-hosts how they see each of BEMA's pillars. Here, Reed Dent explains how he thinks about community. Reed has worked at Campus Christian Fellowship (CCF) for about 18 years and now directs it. He starts from a simple idea: following Jesus gets harder, not easier, the further you go, so nobody should try it alone. He compares healthy Christian community to Japanese woodworking joints, where hand-cut pieces lock together into one strong whole without glue or screws. Reed's model has three parts: **friendship** (sharing life together), **conversation** (exploring questions of faith), and **practice** (taking faithfulness seriously). He ends with thoughts on envy, on each person's unique place in the body of Christ, and on why a community has to keep the ability to criticize itself.

### Key Takeaways

- **Following Jesus is costly and complicated.** It raises hard questions about how we read the Bible, who God is, how we treat neighbors and strangers, how we relate to empire, and what we do with our work, money, and things. Community exists so that we don't carry all of this alone.
- **Christian faith that stays private becomes "flimsy" and "impotent."** If faith shrinks down to beliefs, a prayer once prayed, and personal forgiveness, it loses its strength. Jesus's way is formed *by* community and lived out *for* community.
- **Community is a craft.** Like a woodworker's skill, it takes dedication, practice, and patience. You will fumble and have to try again. Leaving after one week, or after the first offense, cuts that learning short.
- **"The appropriate context for theological inquiry is love and mutual friendship."** Reed is paraphrasing John Paul II here. Arguing about doctrine with strangers rarely changes anyone. Talking with people who know and love you does.
- **"All doctrine begins as an experience"** (Frederick Buechner). Beliefs grow out of lived experience. To understand what a friend believes, you need to know their story.
- **Good conversation is rigorous without being rigid.** A community should be able to hold questions without rejecting or condemning people, but it also shouldn't turn into an echo chamber.
- **Questions don't cancel obedience.** Jesus asks for disciples, not admirers (Kierkegaard). "Whoever claims to live in him must live as Jesus did" (1 John 2:6).
- **Practice includes both action and remembrance.** Communities follow Jesus together through obedience. They also remember God together through sacraments, festivals, liturgy, text, and prayer, and they ask what those mean in their own time and place.
- **The Eucharist is Reed's central communal practice.** It "re-members" the community into one body and sends it back out for six more days.
- **The body needs "a real you," not a fake copy of someone else.** Envy and condemnation kill community. Communities also die when they lose the ability to criticize themselves.

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. Why Community? The Cost of Discipleship

Reed's premise is that trying to follow Jesus faithfully raises more hard questions as you go, not fewer. It touches:

- **How we read the Bible** and what it means
- **Theology**, meaning our assumptions about who God is
- **Vocation**: what to do with one's life
- **Ethics and economics**: neighbors, strangers, empire, jobs, time, money, possessions

Reed stresses that this way of life is "full of joy and full of shalom," but it is also tiring. He also admits something personal: "If it's just me, I tend to go awry pretty easily." Community gives us both **encouragement** and **challenge**.

Marty adds that much of this is about rebuilding a whole value system and ethic. Framed that way, it looks silly to treat it as a solo project. You can't overhaul how you live inside your own head.

#### 2. Against Individualized Christianity

Reed names a familiar kind of Christianity that "mostly restricts itself to the realm of the individual." In that version, faith means:

- What you think about God
- The prayer you prayed
- Having your sins forgiven

Reed doesn't deny any of these. His point is that when faith gets *reduced* to them, it becomes weak. He uses "faith" to mean active trust and following, not just belief, and that kind of faith needs community to be strong.

He makes two points:

1. **Formation:** Community shapes us into people who follow Jesus.
2. **Expression:** Jesus's way can only be lived out among other people. Unless you become "a monk in the desert," it is always lived *for* the people around you.

#### 3. The Japanese Joinery Metaphor

Reed describes two ways to join pieces of wood:

| Approach | Method | Analogy for Community |
|---|---|---|
| **Slapdash joinery** | Push two blocks together and drive a screw through, or clamp them with glue | Something outside the people holds them together, like a program, an obligation, or an event |
| **Japanese joinery** | Hand-cut pieces with prongs and angles lock together like a 3D puzzle | Each person adds to the strength of the whole, and nothing else is needed |

In Japanese joinery, each piece stays itself but also becomes part of a whole. Take one piece out and the joint fails. In the same way, CCF's three elements depend on each other.

Brent connects this to the Japanese ideal of giving a whole life to one craft, which matches Reed's 18 years at CCF. Reed builds on that: **community is a crafted thing and a learned skill.**

#### 4. The Three Elements of Community at CCF

Reed's summary: **"Sharing life together and exploring questions of faith while taking faithfulness seriously."**

##### A. Friendship: "Sharing Life Together"

- **Foundation:** "The appropriate context for theological inquiry is love and mutual friendship" (Reed's paraphrase of John Paul II).
- **Why it matters:** Arguments between "talking heads" over fine points of doctrine leave nobody better off, whoever wins.
- **Theological basis:** Frederick Buechner's idea that "all doctrine begins as an experience." The writers of Scripture didn't compose philosophical treatises at a desk. They met God and then tried to express what that meant. Those expressions became Scripture, doctrine, and theology.
- **What follows:** If beliefs grow out of experience, then knowing someone's life (why they named their child what they did, what winning state track meant to them, how they grieved a grandmother) is the best way to understand their faith.
- **In practice:** Games, movies, pickup basketball, and staying up all night watching *Lord of the Rings* are "not superfluous." They are a core part of discipleship.

##### B. Conversation: "Exploring Questions of Faith"

- **What it looks like:** Opening a Bible to a parable, or discussing something Marty said in a BEMA episode on Elijah.
- **Two guardrails:**
  - No **rejection, condemnation, or fracturing** when people disagree
  - No **echo chamber**. People must be free to push back: "I don't think it's like that; I think it's like this."
- **A cautionary story:** Late one night, a student asked Reed, with a tremble in his voice, whether he could still be a Christian if he didn't believe in six literal days of creation. He had never felt safe asking that in his home community.
- **What it requires:** Marty adds that real conversation depends on humility, admitting "there's more to everything than what I see." Anyone might be wrong.
- **Reed's corollary for young adults:** "Sometimes the truth is hard because it's not what you've been taught, but sometimes it's hard because it *is* what you've been taught." Questioning your upbringing doesn't automatically mean you have found the truth.
- **Rigorous without being rigid:** Rigor tends to crowd out curiosity, so the goal is to hold both. Take questions seriously, read and listen widely, and "lift each other up and hold each other to the mat."

##### C. Practice: "Taking Faithfulness Seriously"

Reed warns that people can get so caught up in wondering that they let go of what they're actually convinced of. Practice has two sides:

**1. Obedience and following**

- "Why do you call me 'Lord, Lord,' and do not do what I say?" (Luke 6:46)
- Reed sets this against a "country club" church where people "pat each other on the back" and celebrate their "mutual heaven-boundness."
- Kierkegaard: Jesus never asks for admirers, only disciples. (Reed reads this piece at CCF on Maundy Thursday.)
- 1 John 2:6: "Whoever claims to live in him must live as Jesus did," a verse Marty has groups repeat on study trips.
- You can ask every question you have about the Trinity, Scripture, predestination, or the Fall, and still you must walk as Jesus walked.
- Stanley Hauerwas: "The church doesn't have a social ethic; the church *is* a social ethic." (Reed says "social strategy.")
- God "is in search of a body." He works through the body of Christ as his hands and feet.

**2. Remembrance and worship**

- Sacraments, festivals, rituals, text, and prayer
- The church is not just "a collective action agency."
- Remembrance is **local and contextual**. Each community asks what an ancient practice means *here and now*.

Marty adds that people often treat sacraments as if they happen "in a vacuum." The sacrament has been the same for 2,000 years, but the world around it keeps changing, and each generation has to ask what it means for them.

#### 5. The Eucharist as Re-Membering

At CCF, the Sunday sermon leads into the Eucharist, and Reed has come to see the sermon as "a set-up" for it: "That's where the juice really is."

- **The wordplay:** "Re-member" means to *put the body back together*, to become one body again. (Historically, "remember" comes from the Latin *rememorari*, "to call to mind," but the play on words still teaches something real.)
- **The rhythm:** The community gathers, is fed, and goes back out onto campus as a body "for the next six days."
- **Brent's point:** The Eucharist is "the equalizer." Even if the sermon didn't land, you can still come to the table with your family.
- **Reed's wife's story:** She works as a VA counselor with veterans carrying combat trauma. Asked how she copes, she says: "It's all about Eucharist for me... I take all of that sorrow and I soak it into the bread and I wring it out. And then I can be fed." When a whole community does this together, the burden is shared.

#### 6. Many Members, One Body: Against Envy

Drawing on Paul's picture of the body in 1 Corinthians 12:

- Each person should fully embrace the part they are. A foot shouldn't wish it were a hand.
- **"A singularity that's made up of all this plurality."**
- Envy usually aims at the people closest to us. Reed doesn't envy celebrities. He envies his colleague Derek, because he sees Derek's goodness up close.
- Reed recently preached on Saul's envy of David (1 Samuel 18).
- **"The body of Christ doesn't need a bunch of fake Dereks. It just needs a real you."**
- **The tension:** Be fully yourself, *and* remember you belong to something larger. The body needs every part "firing on all cylinders" to carry God's presence into the world as a temple.

#### 7. Communal Self-Criticism

Reed quotes Richard Rohr, from a podcast: **"Any people group that loses the capacity for self-criticism will become idolatrous."**

- Envy kills community, and so does condemning one another.
- Communities also die when, *as a group*, they stop asking: "Are we doing what is right? Are we following in the way?"
- Marty calls this "a little drop," and Brent notes that the line that makes you say "oof" is usually the true one.

### Examples & Applications

- **Japanese joinery:** Picture a traditional timber frame (*kigumi*) built without nails. Each beam is shaped to fit its neighbors. A community where every member adds structural strength lasts longer than one held together by programs alone.
- **Social media comment threads:** Marty points to these as the opposite of "love and mutual friendship." They are debate, not real theological discussion, and they leave everyone unsatisfied.
- **Public debates (Bill Nye vs. William Lane Craig):** Formal debates mostly make each side more entrenched. Reed admits he does the same thing when he's the one arguing: argue, defend, dig in.
- **Using a podcast well:** Reed applies the point to BEMA itself. Listen to the podcast, then "go to your friends who know and love you and discuss it there." Don't let the podcast be the only voices in your head.
- **The six-days-of-creation student:** A case study in what happens when a community can't hold questions. The student felt he would be rejected "as if he had suddenly caught some kind of super deadly, easily transmissible disease."
- **Derek at morning prayer:** Reed hears Derek's excited voice coming up through the floor as he works through hard questions with students. That is rigorous conversation driven by love.
- **The church-hopper:** Someone tries a church for a week, finds a problem, and leaves. Or they stay a while, then cut ties after one offense. Reed's answer: community is a skill you learn by staying through the awkward parts.
- **The counselor and the bread:** A model for how worship can hold real sorrow. A professional who absorbs other people's trauma finds release and nourishment at the Lord's Table.
- **Envy close to home:** Most people don't envy strangers. They envy a coworker, a sibling, or a fellow church member. Naming that honestly is the first step to resisting it.

### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

1. **Frederick Buechner's theology of experience:** Read *Wishful Thinking* or *The Sacred Journey* and consider how "all doctrine begins as an experience" changes the way you read Scripture.
2. **Stanley Hauerwas on the church as social ethic:** Explore *Resident Aliens* (with William Willimon) or *The Peaceable Kingdom* to see the church itself as God's alternative way of living together.
3. **Kierkegaard's "admirers vs. followers":** Read the relevant section of *Practice in Christianity* and compare it with Bonhoeffer's *The Cost of Discipleship*.
4. **1 Corinthians 12–14:** Study Paul's whole argument about gifts, the body, and love. How does chapter 13 change the meaning of "many members"?
5. **Saul and David (1 Samuel 16–19):** Trace how Saul's envy grows into violence. What does the story teach about comparison inside a community?
6. **Anamnesis and the Eucharist:** Look into the Greek word *anamnesis* ("remembrance") in Luke 22:19 and 1 Corinthians 11:24–25, and the Jewish idea of *zikaron* at Passover, where remembering makes the past present.
7. **Communal self-criticism in the prophets:** Look at how Israel's prophets (Amos, Jeremiah, Hosea) served as the people's ability to criticize themselves, and what happened when that voice was silenced.
8. **Community as a learned skill:** Consider Bonhoeffer's *Life Together*, especially his warning about loving our *ideal* of community more than the actual people in it.
9. **Rigor and curiosity:** What habits help a community stay intellectually serious without hardening into rigidity?

### Comprehension Questions

1. What is Reed's main reason why following Jesus needs community, and why does he say discipleship makes some things "harder, not easier"?
2. Explain the Japanese joinery metaphor. How does the difference between "slapdash" joinery and hand-cut interlocking joints illustrate the relationship between friendship, conversation, and practice?
3. According to Reed, why is "love and mutual friendship" the right setting for theological inquiry? How does Buechner's claim that "all doctrine begins as an experience" support this?
4. What two guardrails does Reed set for healthy conversation in community, and what does it mean to be "rigorous without being rigid"?
5. Reed describes practice as having two sides. What are they, and how does the Eucharist, understood as "re-membering," hold them together?

### Summary

In this short episode (Reed jokes that it may be the shortest he's ever recorded), Reed Dent takes the BEMA pillar of community down to the everyday life of a campus ministry. His main point is that following Jesus is costly and complicated, and nobody is built to do it alone. Faith reduced to private belief turns flimsy. Faith lived among others becomes strong.

His picture of Japanese joinery stays with you. A good community isn't held together by outside fasteners. Each person, shaped by their own story, locks into the others and becomes part of what holds the whole together. At CCF that means **friendship** that makes room for honest questions, **conversation** that is curious but rigorous and neither condemning nor an echo chamber, and **practice** that holds people to the way of Jesus while remembering God together at the table.

The challenge for any of us: stay through the awkward parts, because community is a craft. Be the real you instead of a copy of someone you envy. Bring what you learn back to people who know and love you. And make sure your community keeps asking whether it is really walking as Jesus walked, because a group that stops asking that drifts toward idolatry.
