### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** BEMA 504: The Pillar of Community  
**Hosts:** Marty Solomon and Brent Billings, with co-host Elle Grover Fricks leading the conversation  
**Focus:** Community as one of the four pillars. Main texts: Revelation 19:5–10, Matthew 25:1–13 (the parable of the ten bridesmaids), Matthew 7:21–23, Hebrews 12:1, Philippians 1:15–18, John 14:2–3, Ephesians 1–2 and Isaiah's picture of the scattered being gathered home.

The four-pillars series continues with Elle Grover Fricks offering her view of the pillar of community. She opens with a "found poem" made from news headlines about the loneliness epidemic, then argues that people are made for real, embodied belonging because they bear the image of a relational, Trinitarian God. Her key idea is the Hebrew word *'ud* ("again"), which sits behind the biblical language of witness and testimony. On this reading, God's people are witnesses who tell the stories of God's work over and over. She uses the bride waiting for the bridegroom to describe Christian community as a people sharing "letters from the groom" while they wait for him to return. She then speaks honestly about three things that keep people out of community: hurt done by the church, the intellectual superiority that can come with new learning, and the lone-wolf ideal of self-sufficiency. She ends by urging listeners not to be "too self-sufficient, too good, too busy, or too right" for the wedding feast.

### Key Takeaways

- **The loneliness epidemic is real and widely recognized.** News outlets, governments and the World Health Organization all treat isolation as a serious health threat. AI, parasocial and online relationships cannot replace embodied community.
- **The longing for belonging is part of our spiritual DNA.** Even people who have numbed that longing still love stories of found family and shared mission, like Sam diving into the river after Frodo.
- **To witness is to tell the story "again."** The Hebrew root *'ud* means "again" or "repeat." Bearing witness means retelling encounters with God many times over, the way mourners tell stories about someone they loved.
- **Christian community is a bride longing for her groom.** It is more than accountability, discipline or study. It is a people sharing stories, like "letters from the front," about what the absent bridegroom has been doing in the world.
- **"The testimony of Jesus is the spirit of prophecy" (Revelation 19:10).** When people share stories of God's work, the Spirit can bring something to light in someone else's life.
- **Worship on earth echoes worship in heaven.** The multitudes in Revelation keep giving testimony. When a local community does the same, it joins a "great cloud of witnesses" (Hebrews 12:1).
- **Solitude is not automatically safe.** Fuller healing usually happens with safe people who build us up and bear witness to our lives.
- **Love people more than your theology.** Marty values the people of the church more than his commitment to a particular way of reading the text.
- **Withdrawing in self-sufficiency has real costs.** At best we become like the Essenes, right about corruption but cut off from the people God placed around us. At worst we hear "I never knew you," because the last day is about *knowing* the groom, not about having the best doctrines.
- **If the groom loves someone, his bride should too.** Contempt for the people Jesus died for cannot sit alongside devotion to Jesus.

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. The Loneliness Epidemic as Cultural Diagnosis

Elle's "found poem," in the style of Derek and Reed's Living Poet Society, strings together twelve headlines about loneliness. Together they show:

- How wide the problem is: it touches Black men, women, young people, retirees and whole nations such as Korea.
- How varied the proposed fixes are: the YMCA, convenience stores, neighbors, and even AI companions.
- That the U.S. Surgeon General's own advisory called loneliness an epidemic and said the solution "doesn't come from a doctor."

**Her point:** Everyone senses that real relationships are getting scarcer. Substitutes like AI friends, parasocial attachments and purely online connections can't meet the need. Even good long-distance friendships carry an ache of absence.

#### 2. Image of God and Relational Design

- The Trinity is relational, so beings made in God's image are made for relationship.
- People can become "high on self-sufficiency" and numb to their loneliness, but the longing doesn't go away.
- **Evidence from story:** People are drawn to tales of found families on a shared mission. *The Lord of the Rings* would fall flat if Frodo rode off alone, or if Sam just said, "I'll pray for you."

#### 3. The Vocabulary of Witness

| Term | Language | Meaning | Significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tabernacle | Latin *tabernaculum* | "Hut" or "tent," related to English *tavern* | The English word hides the Hebrew idea |
| Tent of Witness / Testimony | Hebrew (*ohel ha'edut*) | The tent where God's testimony dwells | Ties God's dwelling to witness |
| *'ud* | Hebrew root | "Again," "repeat" | To witness is to tell the story again and again |
| Witness | Old English *witnes* | "Knowledge," "knowingness" | Witness is tied to *knowing* someone |
| Eulogy | Greek *eu* + *logos* | "Good word" | Telling good stories about someone loved |
| Martyr | Greek *martys* | "Witness" | Martyrs testified to a God sufficient to carry them through death |
| Prophecy | Greek *pro* + *phēmi* | "To speak forth," to bring thoughts into the light | Testimony brings hidden things to light |
| *Yada* | Hebrew | Knowing by experience | Elle "knows" church hurt from the inside |

**The funeral picture:** At a wake for a well-loved person, people gather to tell stories. Some are new and make everyone say, "That's so them!" Others are familiar, but people love hearing them again because they love the person. Elle sees this as the shape of Christian community: remembering, laughing and grieving together as we bear witness to someone we know.

#### 4. The Bride Longing for the Bridegroom

- **Matthew 25:1–13:** Ten bridesmaids wait through the night for the groom. Elle reads the oil as the Holy Spirit, a reading she notes BibleProject also takes. Marty and Brent are not convinced.
- **Her key distinction:** Christian community includes accountability, spiritual disciplines and Bible study, but it is more than these. At heart it is the bride sharing news of the absent groom.
- **The "letter from the front" image:** In war films, a family clutches a letter from a soldier far away and then shares it with everyone. The family piling onto each other over Father's letter in *Little Women* (2019) captures it.
- **Longing still matters:** We have the Spirit, but a healthy community still feels sorrow and eagerness for the groom's presence.
- **Why we need each other:** Some people will be in a "dark night of the soul" and unable to believe right now. Others will be full of encounters with God. Both are helped by hearing someone else's story.

#### 5. Revelation 19:5–10: Testimony as the Spirit of Prophecy

Brent reads the passage from the KJV. Its main elements:

- A call to praise from "both small and great."
- A multitude sounding "as the voice of many waters": "the Lord God omnipotent reigneth" (familiar from Handel's *Messiah*).
- "Be glad and rejoice, and give honour." Elle names gladness and honor as themes of her own life.
- The marriage of the Lamb has come, and the bride is dressed in fine white linen, "the righteousness of saints."
- "Blessed are they which are called unto the marriage supper of the Lamb."
- The angel refuses worship: "I am thy fellowservant… worship God: for the testimony of Jesus is the spirit of prophecy."

**Interpretive move:** If heaven is full of repeated testimony, then local communities that tell God's stories are echoing heavenly worship. Marty notes how often Revelation shows multitudes in white robes singing. It is a book full of crowds giving *'ud*.

#### 6. Three Barriers to Community

##### a. Church Hurt and Trauma

- Elle speaks from experience (*yada*) about corruption, trauma and harm in churches.
- She does **not** ask anyone to go back to places where harm goes on without repentance.
- She does gently urge people, in time, to look for **safe places** with different values. That might be another church, another denomination or a BEMA group.
- "Don't let that harm be the end of your story." You need community, and community needs your voice.
- **Marty's point:** Solitude can look like the safest option, but full healing usually comes through safe people who bear witness.
- **Elle on "false solitude":** What we call solitude is often still crowded with news feeds and social media.
- **Pete Davis, *Dedicated*:** Online communities are like a small bonfire in the tundra. They give some warmth, but they are not the full flourishing of shared life, because only selected parts of you are known there.

##### b. Intellectual Superiority ("They Don't Get It")

- People new to BEMA, excited by Hebrew thought and *peshat* (the plain sense of the text), may get frustrated with congregations that "don't know what I know."
- Elle admits she was once "the insufferable spiritual Gestapo," lecturing people about the context of Jeremiah 29:11.
- **The lesson:** God does not need us to be his police force. It feels powerful to be "right," and that rush is a warning sign.
- **Marty's position:** He loves people and community more than his hermeneutic. He would rather join a Bible study to know people than to drop nuggets of insight.
- **Philippians 1:15–18:** Paul rejoices that Christ is preached even when he disagrees with the motives. Elle asks for the same generosity: when someone says "God has a wonderful plan for my life," respond with "Tell me what you've heard!"
- Listening to someone's testimony helps us love them, and the Spirit may stir something in us through their words.

##### c. Self-Sufficiency (The Lone Wolf)

- The Clint Eastwood or John Wayne ideal: "It's just me and Jesus."
- This is often a **reasonable coping strategy** after leaving or being pushed out of a church. Learning that God alone is enough in a dark season is real growth.
- **Best-case outcome: becoming an Essene.** The Essenes withdrew to the desert, critiqued the Temple's corruption (often rightly), and called themselves "sons of light." But they left the people and the roles God had placed them in.
- **"What if?" questions:** Could the Essenes have helped reform the priesthood from inside? If John the Baptist hadn't been the only one immersing people in the Jordan, might his life have been spared? Brent adds: would their work have borne fruit sooner than the Dead Sea Scrolls surfacing 2,000 years later?
- **Worst-case outcome: Matthew 7:23, "I never knew you."** If witness means knowing, then cutting ourselves off from the shared knowing of God puts our relationship with the groom at risk.

#### 7. Loving Whom the Groom Loves

- We can't want the groom's book, gifts, calling and blessing while despising the people he died for.
- Spending free time scoffing at people online contradicts devotion to Jesus.
- **Genesis "one flesh":** A bride takes on her husband's loves. "If Jesus loves this person, obviously I do too."
- Political positions and doctrinal disagreements are "ephemera" when our eyes are fixed on the bridegroom.

#### 8. The Eschatological Home

- **Isaiah:** The kingdom is the scattered being gathered home to each other.
- **Jesus:** Orphans are not left alone, and the prodigal is welcomed back into the household.
- **John 14:2–3:** "I go to prepare a place for *you*." The "you" is plural, and the picture is something like an *insula*, a shared family dwelling of the period.
- **Ephesians:** All believers are adopted into one family.
- **Marty's critique:** We mock "rapture theology" as a disembodied escape, but individualistic Christianity is just another escape. God's dream is a safe family to belong to.
- **Elle's warning:** A Christian life spent "camping out in a little shed," fed by eight favorite celebrity preachers, poorly prepares us for heaven's diversity of "every tribe and tongue."

### Examples & Applications

1. **The found poem:** Search "loneliness epidemic" yourself and notice how many proposed fixes avoid the costly work of real, embodied community.
2. **The *Lord of the Rings* river scene:** Sam can't swim, but he wades in after Frodo. Ask yourself: who would wade into the river for me, and for whom would I?
3. **The wake or funeral:** Think of a memorial where stories were told. Bring that practice to a small group by inviting people to share a "God story," whether new or often told.
4. **Letters from the front (*Little Women*):** Treat corporate worship and testimony as reading the groom's letters together, not as reciting doctrines.
5. **Handel's *Messiah*:** Brent heard Elle sing in a local choral society's performance. Choral singing is a lived picture of the "voice of many waters," many voices becoming one witness.
6. **Practicing generous listening:** When a fellow believer uses a verse "out of context," try "Tell me what God's been doing" before correcting them.
7. **Auditing "solitude":** Notice whether your alone time is really with God or crowded with news feeds and social media.
8. **Finding a safe community:** If you've been hurt, take small steps. Visit a church in a different tradition, join or start a BEMA group, or find one other person to share honestly with.
9. **Watching contempt:** Track how much of your screen time goes to mocking people Jesus loves, and replace some of it with real conversation.
10. **The thorn letter (þ):** Brent's aside about why the KJV says "ye" is a reminder that language carries history, and that biblical words like *'ud* reward a closer look.

### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

- **The Hebrew *'ud* / *'edut* word family:** Trace "testimony," "witness" and "again" through the Tabernacle texts (Exodus 25–40), the "Ark of the Testimony," and Isaiah 43:10 ("You are my witnesses").
- **Matthew 25:1–13 in context:** Compare readings of the oil (the Spirit, good works, readiness) and first-century Jewish wedding customs.
- **Revelation's worship scenes:** Study Revelation 4–5, 7, 15 and 19 together as a running theme of communal testimony.
- **The Essenes and Qumran:** Look into the Dead Sea Scrolls, the "Sons of Light vs. Sons of Darkness" framework, and possible links between the Essenes and John the Baptist.
- **Pete Davis, *Dedicated: The Case for Commitment in an Age of Infinite Browsing*:** Explore its critique of "keeping options open" and its relevance to church life.
- **The U.S. Surgeon General's 2023 advisory on loneliness and the WHO Commission on Social Connection:** Compare the public-health data with biblical anthropology.
- **Church hurt and trauma-informed community:** What makes a church "safe," and how do repentance and accountability work?
- **Philippians 1:15–18:** Look at Paul's generosity toward rival preachers and what it means for unity within the church.
- **The *insula* and John 14:** Look at first-century housing patterns and what "my Father's house has many rooms" might have meant to its first hearers.
- **The four pillars series:** Compare this episode's view of community with Marty's original pillar episode and the other pillars, especially the coming "wrestling" pillar.

### Comprehension Questions

1. What does the Hebrew root *'ud* mean, and how does Elle use it to explain what it means for God's people to "bear witness"?
2. How does Elle's reading of Matthew 25 and the bride-and-groom imagery go beyond seeing Christian community as accountability or study? What does the "letter from the front" image add?
3. In Revelation 19:10, what is "the spirit of prophecy," and how does Elle connect the Greek idea behind prophecy to sharing testimony in community?
4. What three barriers to community does Elle name, and what response does she give to each?
5. Why does Elle call becoming "like the Essenes" the best-case outcome of self-sufficiency, and how does Matthew 7:23 describe the worst case?

### Personalized Summary

This episode answers a lonely culture by recovering an old biblical word, *'ud*: "again." To be a witness is to keep telling the stories of what God has done. Christian community is where those stories are shared, like a bride passing around letters from her absent groom, or mourners at a wake retelling stories of someone they love. Elle is honest that church can hurt people, that learning can make us proud, and that going it alone can feel noble and safe. Her answer is still clear. We are not fine on our own. Solitude is rarely as safe as it looks, and love for the groom has to include love for the people he died for. The image to carry forward is a wedding feast where heaven's multitudes are already giving testimony, and the invitation is to join them now instead of being "too self-sufficient, too good, too busy, or too right" to come home.
