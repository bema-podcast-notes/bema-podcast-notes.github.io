### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** BEMA 513: Women in Joshua and Judges, Introduction: The Context of Joshua and Judges  
**Hosts:** Marty Solomon and Brent Billings, with guest Elle Grover Fricks  
**Focus:** Exodus 23:20–33; Joshua 5:13–15; Joshua 6:1–5, 16–24; Joshua 7:1

This episode starts a new series, *Women in Joshua and Judges*, which carries on the approach of the earlier *Forgotten Women* series. It looks at less familiar women in these two books, such as Rahab. Before the series gets to those stories, Elle Grover Fricks takes on the "elephant in the room": the conquest of Canaan, which many readers find hard to accept morally. She doesn't file the conquest away as a mystery or set it aside as ancient Near Eastern exaggeration. Instead she asks listeners to "lean in" to the text. Using her own translations and a concordance study of the Hebrew word *ḥerem* (usually translated "destroy"), she argues that God's original promise was about liberation and God's own action, not a command to commit violence. She also argues that the tragedy in Joshua comes when Israel layers its own methods and assumptions on top of what God actually said. The episode closes with a challenge about how people today add their own agendas to good things God has given them.

### Key Takeaways

- **Lean in before reaching for "mystery."** Approaches that center on Christ, treat the text as mystery, or read it as cultural retelling are all valid tools. Elle encourages careful study of the text first, before filing hard passages away.
- **Exodus 23 frames the conquest as liberation.** God promises to be "an enemy to your enemies" and to "confine those who confine you." The aim is to free Israel from oppression, not to wipe out "bad people."
- **The action belongs to God.** God sends the angel, God's dread, the confusion, and the hornets, and God does the driving out. Israel is never told in this passage to go and kill everyone.
- **"Drive out" (*garash*) doesn't mean kill.** It's the same verb used when Adam and Eve are sent out of Eden. They were moved to a new place, not killed.
- **God is not a team cheerleader.** Joshua asks the commander of the LORD's army, "Are you for us or for our adversaries?" The answer is "No." We are on God's team, not the other way around.
- **Jericho is full of Jubilee imagery.** The shofar, the Jubilee horn, the priests, and the ark all point to liberation, rest, and returning the land, not to military strategy.
- **The word *ḥerem* means "devoted" or "set apart for God," not "destroyed."** The same word is used for fields given to priests, for sacred items, and for firstfruits, none of which are destroyed.
- **Achan's sin shows what *ḥerem* means.** Taking from what was devoted to God brings trouble on the whole camp.
- **Israel loses when it fights in the ordinary way.** At Ai, conventional warfare leads to defeat. Victory comes through God's supernatural action.
- **Application:** We often take a good thing from God, such as Sabbath, work, or ministry, and "add our own sparkles." Then we pursue it in ways that harm our families and neighbors.

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. Postures toward the conquest problem

Elle names several common ways people handle the violence in Joshua:

1. **Reading through Christ:** "I know who Jesus is." Interpret everything through Christ and don't worry much about the rest.
2. **Mystery:** Hold the passage open-handedly and accept that you don't understand it.
3. **Cultural retelling:** Treat the account as exaggerated ancient Near Eastern war rhetoric that isn't necessarily accurate history.

Marty notes that in an episode early in Season Two he drew on a "salad" of all three. Elle affirms each of them but argues that people press "the red button of mystery" too quickly. Her approach is to study closely and look for clues about "what God might have been up to all along."

#### 2. Exodus 23:20–33: the original promise

Brent reads Elle's translation, and the hosts point out several features:

| Phrase | Hebrew / Cultural Insight | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| "I will be an enemy to your enemies… confine those who confine you" | The enemy is defined narrowly | It sets up liberation as the frame, not a dualism of good people against bad people |
| "I will send out **my** dread" | It is God's dread, not dread of Israel | The nations fear God, not Joshua's army |
| "I will cause a hubbub" | An onomatopoeic word, like English "hubbub" | Confusion and panic: people talking it over and deciding what to do |
| "I will give the back of the neck of all your enemies to you" | Killing someone from behind was dishonorable | It means seeing the backs of enemies as they run away, not lining them up to be killed |
| "Leprosy or hornets" | Similar consonants, vocalized differently; both describe a stinging experience | Whether literal, supernatural, or metaphorical, it's something God sends to keep them from coming back |
| "I will drive them out" | *Garash*, the same verb as the expulsion from Eden | Being removed to a new place, not being killed |
| "Little by little… lest the land be stunned" | A gradual process | It matches the slow historical process. Again, God does it, not a military campaign |
| "If you serve their gods, surely it will be your noose" | God cares about Israel's wellbeing | Idolatry destroys the people who practice it |

Elle notes that Exodus 33 and 34 repeat the same pattern: God sends an angel and God drives the nations out. God never gives the instruction "go and kill everyone."

Marty observes that the list of nations changes partway through. The Perizzites and Jebusites drop out of the second list, and Brent jokes that "they're already gone."

#### 3. Joshua 5: Passover and the commander of the LORD's army

- **Context (Joshua 1–5):** The spies, Rahab, the Jordan crossing, circumcision, and **Passover**. Israel's entry into the land opens with a festival of *liberation*. As Marty puts it, it's "not a story of conquest and triumph" but a story of being liberated.
- **Joshua 5:13–15:** A man with a drawn sword stands opposite Joshua.
  - Joshua asks, "Are you for us or for our adversaries?" The answer is **"No."** Elle compares its bluntness to Dwight Schrute's "False." The NIV's "Neither" reads more softly.
  - **The point:** God isn't a cheerleader for one side at the expense of others. "God is for God" and for love, freedom, justice, and mercy, and humans are invited to be God's co-workers.
  - Marty adds that God is on our side because we're human, "not because we're the right human beings."
  - This prepares the way for Jesus, who loves and heals his enemies.
- **"Remove your sandal… the place is holy":** This deliberately echoes Moses at the burning bush, linking Joshua's mission back to the Exodus liberation. Joshua is following in his mentor's steps.

#### 4. Joshua 6:1–5: Jericho as a Jubilee proclamation

- Jericho is shut up tight. God says, "I have **gifted** Jericho and her **heroic** king." Even the enemy is described with respect.
- The "men of battle" are told only to *walk around* the city. Marty calls the instructions "almost game-show-esque." It's clearly not military strategy, and no modern general would accept it.
- **The shofar of the Jubilee (*yovel*):**
  - The shofar is linked to the ram caught by its horns at the binding of Isaac (the *Akedah*), which became an early image of salvation in Jewish and Christian art.
  - The shofar is blown to announce the **Jubilee**, which meant freeing slaves, rest for the land, returning land to its families, and God's provision.
  - Elle asks whether "tearing down their standing stones" might also be about liberation, since those gods are a noose to the Canaanites as well.
- **The shout (*teruah*):** A great cry, something like "hoorah."
- **"Each man straight ahead" (*neged*):** The same word as in the garden phrase *ezer kenegdo*. The people go up side by side.
- **Land as a grant:** Brent connects this to Sandra Richter's idea of a "land grant." The land belongs to God and is given to Israel to steward, with portions such as field corners set aside for the poor (Leviticus).

#### 5. The key word study: *ḥerem*

**The problem:** Standard translations render many different Hebrew words as "destroy." In Joshua 6:17–19, reading *ḥerem* as "destroy" gives awkward sentences: "Guard yourselves from the destroyed, lest you become destroyed."

**Concordance evidence (from Elle's study):**

1. **Fields given to priests (Leviticus 27):** A *ḥerem* field is not burned to the ground. It now belongs to the priests. Elle's joke: someone sets the offering basket on fire and hands it to the pastor.
2. **Priestly items and garments:** These are set apart, not destroyed. The priests "aren't supposed to run around naked." Marty's joke: dissolving swim trunks.
3. **Firstfruits (Numbers):** These are brought to God and go up to God. They are never described as destroyed.

**The definition:** *Ḥerem* means **devoted, set apart, enclosed, or off-limits for any other use** because it belongs to God.

- Compare *chanak* ("dedicated," as in Hanukkah). A house dedicated to hospitality is open to everyone.
- *Ḥerem* is dedication with a **shield around it**. Nobody else may use it, like a priest's garment you can't lend out the way Torah otherwise encourages lending your cloak.
- Related Semitic languages support this. In Arabic, *haram* means forbidden or off-limits.
- Marty adds that destroying something that has become God's property would itself violate God's ownership.

**Reading Joshua 6 again with this meaning:**

- "The city shall be *ḥerem*": the city is devoted to God and is off-limits.
- "Guard yourselves from the *ḥerem*, lest you become *ḥerem*": don't touch what isn't yours, or you'll be set apart and cut off from the community yourself.
- "All silver and gold… are holy to the LORD; they shall go to His storehouse": this spells out what *ḥerem* means.
- **Joshua does use a separate word for "destroy" later (Joshua 11).** This weakens the argument that word meanings shifted between the time this was written and the priestly texts.

**On verses 21 and 24:** Elle reads "they devoted everything, man and woman, young and old, ox, sheep, and donkey" as consecration to God, not slaughter. She suggests burning the city may have been about removing strong fortifications, which could later be used to oppress others. Burning walls doesn't require killing everyone inside. She also notes that archaeology shows a burn layer, whose cause is uncertain.

**The translations compared (from Brent):** The NIV and NLT first render *ḥerem* as "devoted." Then they add footnotes defining it as "irrevocable giving over… often by totally destroying them," and later render it as destruction. In Brent's words, they "shoot themselves in the foot with their footnotes."

**For further reading:** Michael Heiser's *The Unseen Realm*, chapter 25, works through the same idea.

#### 6. Joshua 7 onward: Achan, Ai, and adding to God's plan

- **Achan (Joshua 7:1):** He takes from the *ḥerem*, and God's "nose burned" (anger) against all Israel. If *ḥerem* meant "kill," the story would amount to God accepting killing but objecting to profit. Read as "devoted," the story makes sense: Achan violated what belonged to God.
- **Ai:** When Israel fights in the ordinary way, it is routed. Elle sums it up as: "You want conventional warfare instead of my supernatural way? I'll let you experience that."
- **The Gibeonites:** Marty notes the next story is about keeping vows and **preserving life**.
- **God's pattern of waiting:** Abraham is promised the land but makes relationships, including with Abimelech, and waits. David is anointed king but waits to rule. Joshua tests whether Israel will trust God's methods.
- **Thesis:** Much of the darkness in Joshua comes from Israel adding its own strategies and assumptions on top of what God actually said.

### Examples & Applications

- **The church and power:** Some voices use the conquest to justify a "God's on our side, destroy whoever" posture toward culture, politics, and civil disputes. Misreading Joshua can lead to a public witness that doesn't follow the way of Jesus.
- **Reading with more than one translation:** Brent compares the NIV and NLT with Elle's translation as an example of not relying on a single version. "If you just do a straightforward simple reading… you can make it say whatever you want."
- **Sabbath at someone else's expense:** One spouse treats Sabbath as personal rest while the other, who stays home, handles the kids and gets no rest. A good practice from God, pursued selfishly, fails to bless everyone in the family.
- **Work and ministry:** "God has given me important work" can turn into an excuse to put work above family, neighbor, and enemy. Elle asks how the families of ministry volunteers are doing, and whether our kids mostly hear criticism from us because "the work is so important."
- **Achan's reasoning, then and now:** "We're victorious, God made us victorious. I'll honor God by keeping a souvenir." This is a model of how people dress up their own desires as faithfulness.
- **Everyday *ḥerem* analogies:** Rooms in a house set aside for one purpose, and Muslim friends who say "that's *haram*, I can't do that."

### Comprehension Questions

1. How does Exodus 23 define the "enemies" God will oppose? How does that definition frame the conquest as liberation rather than as good people versus bad people?
2. What does the phrase "I will give you the back of the neck of your enemies" mean in its cultural context, and why does that change how we picture the conquest?
3. Why is the answer "No" to Joshua's question "Are you for us or for our adversaries?" theologically significant? How does it connect to Jesus's teaching about enemies?
4. Give at least two places outside Joshua where *ḥerem* appears that show it can't simply mean "destroy." What definition does Elle propose instead?

### Summary

This introduction tackles one of the hardest parts of Scripture head-on, and it does so by reading closely rather than explaining the problem away. Elle Grover Fricks shows that God's original promise in Exodus 23 is full of liberation language. God sends dread, confusion, and hornets, and God drives the nations out, the same way Adam and Eve were sent out of Eden, by removal rather than death. When Joshua meets the commander of the LORD's army, he learns that God doesn't take sides. Jericho itself plays out like a Jubilee proclamation, with shofars, priests, and the ark. The center of the episode is the word *ḥerem*. The same word describes priests' fields, sacred items, and firstfruits, which suggests it means "devoted to God and off-limits," not "slaughtered." Read that way, Achan's sin and the defeat at Ai become warnings about what happens when God's people take a gift and pursue it their own way. That's the personal challenge to carry forward: whether the gift is Sabbath, work, ministry, or calling, the question is whether we're walking it out God's way, with fruit that blesses everyone around us, or adding our own sparkles at someone else's expense.
