### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** BEMA Episode 505: The Third Pillar: Discipleship  
**Hosts:** Marty Solomon and Brent Billings  
**Focus:** BEMA's third pillar, being challenged by the historical setting and practice of discipleship. Passages covered: Matthew 4:18–22, Matthew 14:22–33, Matthew 16:13–20, John 15:12–17 and Matthew 28:16–20.

In this episode Marty looks at the third of BEMA's four pillars: discipleship as Jesus practiced it in its Second Temple Jewish setting. He starts with several academic disclaimers. He admits he has spoken too confidently in the past about how rabbinic discipleship worked. He places BEMA's teaching within the scholarly "quests for the historical Jesus." He then describes what he encountered in Israel in 2008 and 2010: discipleship as a full-time commitment, built on imitation, that moves from learning by doing to owning the teaching and then building with it. He traces this pattern through five Gospel passages and compares it with how the modern church usually uses the word "discipleship." He tells the story of his own attempt at intensive discipleship in campus ministry, and admits that he is not doing this kind of discipleship right now. He closes with discipleship principles that any believer can practice, whatever stage of life they are in.

### Key Takeaways

- **Speak with humility about history.** We know less about how first-century discipleship worked than popular teaching suggests. Marty wants to soften claims like "we know this is how it worked."
- **The best answer is usually in the middle.** "Received" history, handed down from earlier scholars and accepted largely on trust, deserves critique. But the critique often goes too far, and archaeology keeps confirming much of what Marty's teachers taught.
- **Rabbinic discipleship had three defining features:**
  1. It was a **24/7/365** commitment: going where the rabbi went, eating and sleeping where he did.
  2. It was based on **imitation and mimicry**, not independent thinking.
  3. It followed a progression of **learning by doing, then possessing, then freely taking and building**.
- **Peter's doubt on the water was doubt in himself, not in Jesus.** Jesus scolds Peter for not believing he could do what his rabbi had called him to do.
- **"The keys of the kingdom"** likely marks the point where a disciple is allowed to interpret and teach on their own, under the rabbi's authority.
- **"I have called you friends"** marks another stage: the disciples move from students to something close to peers.
- **The Great Commission is unusual in two ways.** Jesus keeps his authority instead of passing it on, and he sends out both those who worshiped and those who doubted.
- **Rabbinic discipleship was rare.** It was probably far less than 1% of the population. It was a calling for a few, not a test of whether someone is a true believer.
- **Modern uses of "discipleship" are fine as language but are not the same thing.** These include "believer," "spiritual formation" and "church assimilation." None of them is what Jesus did with Peter, James and John.
- **Not everyone is called to intensive discipleship, Marty included in this season.** Everyone can still practice its principles.
- **There are no shortcuts.** Jesus spent about three years with his disciples. Be patient with yourself and with others.

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. Academic Disclaimers and Historical Humility

Marty says his language about discipleship has "softened" over the years. He is no less passionate, but he is more careful.

- **Received ("receptionist") history:** This is an approach in which scholars weave the available evidence into a coherent picture, and later teachers accept and pass that picture on.
  - An early, foundational figure was **Alfred Edersheim**, author of *The Life and Times of Jesus the Messiah*.
  - Marty's teacher **Ray Vander Laan** studied at Hebrew University in the era of scholars such as **Nahum Sarna**, **Shmuel Safrai** and **David Flusser**. So this tradition came from leading minds, not from rumor passed along.
- **Historical criticism** asks a fair question: "Do we actually know that, or did we just accept what we were handed?"
- **Marty's position:** The critique is valid but often goes too far. The truth is usually "somewhere in the middle."
- **Much depends on definitions.** What do we mean by "rabbi," "disciple" or "the system"? Short-form teaching often has no room for these nuances, but they matter, especially to the scholars in BEMA's audience.
- Brent notes that this kind of humility came up often on the Israel and Turkey trips, in conversations about whether events really happened at a given site.

#### 2. The Quests for the Historical Jesus

This is the scholarly background to BEMA's historical approach.

| Quest | Approximate Era | Key Figure(s) | Central Question / Claim |
|---|---|---|---|
| **First Quest** | 19th century to early 20th century | **Albert Schweitzer** (who effectively closed it) | Was Jesus a real historical person? Schweitzer concluded that he was, but argued that Jesus and his disciples expected the world to end soon, and were wrong. |
| **Second ("New") Quest** | Mid-20th century | **Rudolf Bultmann** (whose skepticism set the stage) | Bultmann argued the historical quest was futile and that theology should be theology, not history. Scholars responding to him tried to sort which Gospel sayings were authentic and which the church had added. |
| **Third Quest** | 1970s to present | **N.T. Wright**, **E.P. Sanders** and others | Draws on new evidence such as the Dead Sea Scrolls and reads Jesus firmly within Second Temple Judaism. It led to developments such as the **New Perspective on Paul**. |

- **Recommended resource:** **Ben Witherington III**, *The Jesus Quest*. It is dated and academic, but it is a strong summary of the Third Quest and its main voices.
- BEMA's emphasis on Jesus's Jewish context grows out of the Third Quest.

#### 3. Second Temple Judaism and the Synagogue

- **Second Temple Judaism** refers to the period after the Babylonian exile and before the Temple was destroyed in 70 AD. This is the world Jesus lived in.
- **The synagogue** changed Jewish culture. It brought:
  - a culture of teachers (**rabbis**) and students (**talmidim**)
  - a renewed commitment to teaching and to studying the text after the return from Babylon
  - a more intense level of practice. This was not a new religion, but Judaism taken to a deeper level of practice.
- That practice included a method of **leadership development**. It resembles later Western scholasticism but came well before it.

#### 4. The Three Features of Rabbinic Discipleship

**a) The 24/7/365 nature** (Matthew 4:18–22)
- This was not "show up on Tuesdays and Thursdays" or "coffee on Friday at Starbucks."
- Disciples went where the rabbi went, slept where he slept, ate where he ate, and traveled with him every day for several years.
- Peter, Andrew, James and John leave their nets, and James and John leave their father Zebedee. This was not just a moment of enthusiasm. They left a whole way of life with no idea when they would return home.

**b) Imitation and mimicry** (Matthew 14:22–33)
- In the modern West we value free, original thinking and avoid conformity. In this world it was the reverse: you **were not allowed to think for yourself until your rabbi said you could.**
- **The context of the story:** Jesus sends the disciples by boat, probably along the shoreline toward Bethsaida rather than across the lake, which is frustrating because they could have walked. He prays on the hillside, where he is probably visible to them. No supernatural sight is needed.
- **The disciples are not foolish** for thinking they see a ghost. Someone walking on water is not normal, and they are trying to make sense of it.
- **Marty's reading of Peter:** Peter reasons, "If it's really you, you'd want me to do this too, because a rabbi doesn't do anything he doesn't want his disciples to copy." Jesus takes him up on it: "Come."
- **The point:** It works. Peter really does walk on water. When he sinks, he has lost faith **in himself**, not in Jesus. "Why did you doubt?" means "Why don't you believe you can do what I've called you to do?"
- **Application:** We often say, "I can't be like Jesus." Jesus's answer seems to be, "Why would I call you to imitate me if I didn't believe you could?"

**c) Learning by doing, then possessing, then freely taking and building**

Marty uses three passages to show the stages of growth:

1. **The keys (Matthew 16:13–20):** In Second Temple rabbinic thought, giving someone "the keys" to the kingdom or to Torah likely meant permission to **interpret and teach independently**, while staying aligned with the rabbi. Before this, a disciple could only repeat what the rabbi said ("I've heard my rabbi say..."). The "you" in Jesus's words is plural, so Peter speaks for the whole group (the *havurah*).
2. **Friends (John 15:12–17):** "I no longer call you servants... I have called you friends." The disciples have gone from students, to interpreters, to near-peers. Marty compares this to commissioning his own campus disciples: "It's not so much about being my equal as it is that you're not my student anymore."
   - **"You did not choose me, but I chose you."** This reverses the normal pattern. Usually a student approached a rabbi and asked to follow him, and the answer was usually no. Jesus took the initiative and went to the Twelve, and to several women.
3. **The commission (Matthew 28:16–20):** A rabbi would normally send out his disciples. Two things stand out here:
   - **Jesus keeps his authority.** Rabbis passed their authority on when their ministry ended or when they died. The risen Jesus is alive, so his followers live under his authority rather than inheriting it.
   - **"But some doubted."** Jesus sends them **all** out, believers and doubters alike.

#### 5. What We Usually Mean by "Discipleship"

Marty names three common modern meanings. He considers each one fine as a matter of language, but none of them is rabbinic discipleship.

1. **Disciple = believer.** This is common in Marty's own tradition, the Christian Church/Church of Christ. In Jesus's day, nearly all of Bethsaida loved and followed the God of Israel and recited the **Shema**, but very few were *talmidim*.
2. **Discipleship = spiritual formation.** This means growing spiritually through a rule of life and spiritual practices.
3. **Discipleship = church assimilation.** This is the megachurch model of getting people into small groups, sometimes marketed as "doing discipleship the way Jesus did." It has good principles, but Jesus was not running a small-groups program.

**The key correction:** Rabbinic discipleship was **rare and for a select few**. God called them to it not because they were better, but for a particular kind of leadership development. **Not being in this kind of discipleship says nothing about anyone's standing before God.**

#### 6. Marty's Experiment in Campus Ministry

- **Why campus ministry?** College students can make their own decisions, as Jesus's young followers could. High schoolers need permission slips, and older adults have careers, mortgages, spouses and children.
- **Living close together:** He rented the house next door for students, rented a large house where everyone lived together, and gave students space in his basement.
- **Shared rhythms:** They got up at the same time and memorized Scripture, read the Bible, prayed and ate breakfast together. They also shared chores, rest, fun, football games, Netflix and kids' activities.
- **One-on-one time:** He took walks of two to three miles with each disciple at least twice a week.
- **Mentoring by example:** Students saw his marriage, his parenting and family decisions such as buying cars or making changes to the house.
- **Guarding against danger:** Marty admits it "could get real culty real quick." To prevent that, the group stayed tied into the wider church through worship, small groups, service projects, staff meetings and peer accountability.
- **The change:** A new job that required travel ended this way of life. In the end that job led to the podcast. Marty believes God called him to this season, but he still says the campus work was "way more meaningful than anything that I've done on the podcast."

#### 7. Living in the Tension

- Marty admits: "I'm not doing discipleship right now." He wrestles with how to teach a pillar he isn't currently practicing.
- He hopes to return to it someday. He may try **virtual discipleship**, a "digital havurah" that shares disciplines, conversation and breakfast over Zoom. He stresses that this is only a dream and he is not taking applications.
- He reminds himself: "I'm not letting Jesus down." Intensive discipleship is not for everyone, and right now it is not for him. **Someone should be pursuing it, but not every believer is required to.**

#### 8. Discipleship Principles for Everyone

Even without 24/7/365 discipleship, believers can build its principles into their lives:

1. **Intentional pursuits and relationships.** There is always someone a step or two ahead of you to follow, and someone a step or two behind you to reach back to: "If you want to chase me while I chase Jesus, we can all get to Jesus together."
2. **Ask who you are around.** Look at family, spouse, children, parents, coworkers, friends, church and other groups. Is there someone to form an intentional "let's follow Jesus together" relationship with?
3. **Imitation and mimicry:**
   - **Spiritual practices:** keep up an active, vibrant daily pursuit of Jesus.
   - **Work:** what would it look like to be a barista, or an accountant, "like Jesus"?
   - **Accountability:** "It's one thing to follow Jesus. It's another thing to know that somebody is watching you follow Jesus."
   - **Family flourishing:** raise your children, partner with your spouse and honor your parents in ways that help everyone flourish.
4. **A "two-degree shift."** Small, steady commitments make a big difference over time.

### Examples & Applications

- **Brent's kids as accountability:** Brent hears his oldest child scold the youngest using Brent's own exact words. It shows that imitation happens whether we intend it or not.
- **Teaching the Lord's Prayer at home:** Brent's family has moved from repeat-after-me to praying together in unison. This is a small cultural shift, since their church tradition doesn't do much unison speaking.
- **Brent's personality:** Brent likes to be competent before acting, so the idea of watching and imitating before taking on authority "is music to my ears." Many learners feel the same way.
- **The study-tour experience:** Walking the actual distances in Israel and Turkey shows how much of discipleship happened along the road, in conversation. This mirrors Marty's twice-weekly walks with students.
- **A workplace example:** A nurse, teacher or software engineer can ask, "What does it look like to fold the life of Jesus into my daily work?" This is about the character of the work itself, not about evangelizing coworkers.
- **Mentoring in other fields:** Medical residencies, trade apprenticeships and graduate research labs follow a similar path. You watch, then do under supervision, then practice independently, then train others.
- **Patience in ministry:** If a small-group leader feels like nothing has changed after three months, remember that Jesus took about three years. "His yoke is easy and his burden is light." If it feels heavier than that, step back and reconsider.

### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

- **The quests for the historical Jesus:** Read Ben Witherington III's *The Jesus Quest*, Albert Schweitzer's *The Quest of the Historical Jesus*, and Rudolf Bultmann's work on demythologizing.
- **Third Quest scholarship:** Explore N.T. Wright's *Jesus and the Victory of God*, E.P. Sanders's *Jesus and Judaism*, and the New Perspective on Paul.
- **Received history and its critics:** Assess Alfred Edersheim's *The Life and Times of Jesus the Messiah* against modern historical criticism. Which claims have archaeology and later study confirmed or overturned?
- **The Jerusalem School:** Study the work of David Flusser, Shmuel Safrai and Nahum Sarna on Jesus's Jewish context.
- **Synagogue origins and archaeology:** Look at recent finds such as the first-century synagogues at Magdala and Gamla, and what they suggest about teaching in Jesus's day.
- **Rabbinic discipleship in later sources:** Compare the Mishnah, especially *Pirkei Avot* ("raise up many disciples"; "cover yourself in the dust of their feet"), with the Gospel accounts, while being careful about reading later sources back into the first century.
- **"Binding and loosing":** Research how this rabbinic phrase relates to authority to interpret Torah.
- **Other rabbis and their disciples:** Look at Hillel and Shammai and their schools as comparisons.
- **Virtual and hybrid discipleship:** Can digital tools support real imitation and closeness, or only parts of it?
- **Healthy versus controlling discipleship:** What safeguards keep intensive mentoring from becoming cult-like?
- **The next pillar:** Connect this episode to BEMA's fourth pillar, being challenged to wrestle.

### Personalized Summary

This episode is honest and humble. Marty begins by admitting that he has sometimes claimed more certainty about first-century discipleship than the evidence supports. He places BEMA's teaching within the long scholarly search for the historical Jesus. He then shows what still holds up: in Jesus's world, a disciple gave the rabbi all of their life and learned by imitating him. Over time they moved from copying, to interpreting, to friendship, to being sent out. Peter's walk on the water says something to us: when Jesus calls us to imitate him, he believes we can do it, even when we don't.

The most freeing part may be Marty's insistence that this intensive kind of discipleship was always rare. Not being in it does not make anyone less faithful, and he admits he isn't in it right now. The principles are still open to everyone: follow someone ahead of you, reach back to someone behind you, choose people to walk with on purpose, practice following Jesus where others can see you, and help your family flourish. As Brent says, there are no shortcuts. Even Jesus took three years, so go easy on yourself, and remember that his yoke is easy and his burden is light.
