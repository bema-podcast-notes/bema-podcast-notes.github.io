### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** 500: Passover, Easter, and the Council of Nicaea (500th Episode Celebration)  
**Hosts:** Marty Solomon and Brent Billings, with guest Rabbi Dr. David Rudolph  
**Focus:** The Quartodeciman controversy over dating Pascha/Easter, from Polycarp to the Council of Nicaea (325 CE), Acts 15, 1 Corinthians 9:19–23, John 10:18, and Ephesians 2

The hosts mark the podcast's 500th episode and nearly ten years of BEMA by announcing their first live recording event. They then talk with Rabbi Dr. David Rudolph, a Messianic Jewish New Testament scholar, about how the early church dated *Pascha* (Passover), its central yearly celebration. The conversation follows a shift over three centuries. At first, Gentile believers depended on the Jewish community to know when Nisan 14 fell. By 325, Constantine's Council of Nicaea was deliberately cutting the Christian calendar loose from Jewish reckoning, and doing it in openly anti-Jewish language. Rudolph presents this history as a small window into the "parting of the ways" between church and synagogue. He asks today's believers to face it honestly, push back on antisemitism, and recover a model of Jew–Gentile unity in the Messiah.

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. The Parting of the Ways

- This is the scholarly term for how Christianity and Judaism grew into two separate religions that often see each other as strangers or enemies.
- Rudolph's question is how the church got from a **Jewish Jesus** (Yeshua HaMashiach) and Jewish apostles to Gentile Christians who feel no closeness to the Jewish people.
- The Easter dating story works as a **case study**. It follows one practice over time and shows where the two communities split and why.

#### 2. Passover in the Earliest Church

- **Jewish believers**, for example in Jerusalem, most likely kept Passover like other Jews, adding new theological meaning in light of the gospel.
- **Gentile believers** were not required to keep Passover. The Jerusalem Council (Acts 15) decided they did not have to become Jews or take on the full "yoke of the Torah."
- Even so, Gentiles worshiped and shared fellowship with Jewish believers. Many probably joined in or timed their own observance to match.
- **"Pascha"** comes from the Hebrew *Pesach* by way of Greek and Latin. Early Christians kept calling their main yearly celebration "Passover."

#### 3. The Gentile Pascha Was Distinct from the Jewish Seder

| Feature | Jewish Passover | Early Gentile Christian Pascha |
|---|---|---|
| Central act | Seder meal remembering the Exodus | Eucharist (Lord's Supper) |
| Bread | Unleavened (matzah) | Leavened |
| Week-long avoidance of leaven | Yes | No |
| Covenantal framework | Sinai covenant obligation | Not part of that covenant obligation |
| Date (Quartodecimans) | Nisan 14 | Nisan 14 (same as the Jews) |

- Brent's summary: the *practice* was different, but the *date* was shared. That shared date was a deliberate sign of unity.

#### 4. Polycarp vs. Anicetus: A Peaceful Disagreement

- **Polycarp** of Smyrna (born c. 69/70 CE, died c. 156) was a Gentile disciple of the Apostle John. He kept Pascha on **Nisan 14** because John had done so. To him this was *apostolic tradition*.
- **Anicetus**, bishop of Rome, kept Pascha on a **Sunday**. He did not claim this came from the apostles, only that it was what earlier Roman leaders had done.
- Polycarp went to Rome to discuss it. Neither persuaded the other, and they **agreed to disagree**.
- **The dependence point:** To keep Nisan 14, Polycarp had to *ask the Jewish community* when it fell. His worship calendar tied him to them.
- Epiphanius quotes an early church regulation along these lines: *don't calculate it yourselves. Keep it with your Jewish brothers, and don't worry even if they get it wrong.*

#### 5. The Bar Kokhba Revolt as a Turning Point (c. 132–135 CE)

- Jews revolted against Rome under Simon bar Kokhba, whom Rabbi Akiva hailed as the Messiah.
- The revolt failed and hundreds of thousands of Jews died.
- Rome **expelled all Jews from Jerusalem**, including the Jewish believers and their bishops.
- Rome renamed the province from *Judea* to *Palaestina*.
- **What this meant for the church:** Jerusalem was no longer the central "mother congregation" (compare Acts 15). The model Jewish believers had provided, including when to keep Pascha, disappeared. Rome increasingly took Jerusalem's place as the center.

#### 6. Victor vs. Polycrates: The Disagreement Hardens (c. 190 CE)

- **Polycrates**, bishop of Ephesus, wrote to **Victor**, bishop of Rome, defending the Nisan 14 practice of the churches in Asia Minor. He appealed to Scripture and to a line of witnesses: John, Philip, Polycarp, Melito, and others.
- Victor reacted strongly and tried to **excommunicate the churches of Asia Minor**.
- The result was about 150 years of division between East and West.
- **Why a date mattered so much:** This was the most important day in the church's year. Sharing it was a large part of what held the church together. Rudolph compares it to how upset people would be today if someone moved Easter.

#### 7. The Quartodeciman Controversy

- The name comes from the Latin *quartodecimani*, "fourteeners": the people who kept Pascha on Nisan 14.
- It was the "14ers" against the Sunday observers within the Gentile church.

#### 8. The Council of Arles (314) and the Council of Nicaea (325)

- **Arles (314 CE):** Tried to unify the church by requiring everyone to follow Rome's practice. **Constantine attended.**
- **Rome's method:** Pascha falls on the Sunday after the first full moon after the vernal equinox, which was eventually fixed at March 21.
  - This method **needs no input from the Jewish people.**
  - Rome also made sure Pascha always came *after* Jewish Passover so the two would not overlap. The Eastern Orthodox Church still keeps Easter after Passover today.
- **Nicaea (325 CE):** Constantine made the dating question one of the council's **two main issues**. The other was the **Arian controversy**: whether Jesus is fully divine and eternally shares in the divine identity of the God of Israel, or is a created being.
- Constantine's attendance at Arles probably explains why he put the dating question so high on Nicaea's agenda.

#### 9. Constantine's Letter (as preserved by Eusebius)

Key phrases from the letter:

- It is "an unworthy thing" to "follow the practice of the Jews."
- The Jews "have impiously defiled their hands with enormous sin" and are "afflicted with blindness of soul."
- "Let us then have nothing in common with the detestable Jewish crowd, for we have received from our Savior a different way."
- It describes the Jews as those who "slain their Lord," which is the **charge of deicide**.

Rudolph's main point is that Nicaea's *positive* decision on Christ's divinity has echoed for 1,700 years, and **so has its antisemitically motivated decision on the calendar.**

#### 10. Lessons for Today

1. **Put the deicide charge to rest.**
   - Vatican II's *Nostra Aetate* rejected the idea that all Jews are guilty of Jesus' death.
   - Romans and others took part, and those involved did not represent all Jews for all time.
   - Jesus said, "No one takes it from me, but I lay it down of my own accord" (John 10:18). He died for everyone's sins.
2. **Fight antisemitism actively.** Real repentance for this history means working to protect Jewish people.
3. **Reject the "nothing in common" mindset.** It shows up in ideas like these: Christians shouldn't visit synagogues, Messianic Jews should assimilate into the church, or the "Christian God" is different from the God of Israel.
4. **"Do Jewish things with Jewish people."** Before 135, the principle was celebrating "with them." That principle can still guide the church.

#### 11. A Critique of Gentile-Only Seders in Some Hebrew Roots Circles

- Many Gentile Christians now hold Passover Seders with no Jewish people present.
- Rudolph sees this as repeating, in a new form, the problem that led up to Nicaea: **worship cut off from any dependence on the Jewish community.**
- The Seder is a **covenantal meal**, and some of its language needs careful thought. For example, can a Gentile say "He brought *us* out of Egypt"?
- **His recommendation:** If you feel led to celebrate Passover, join a Messianic Jewish congregation's community Seder or a Jewish friend's Seder. Having guests is a traditional part of the Seder.

### Examples & Applications

- **A shared calendar as relationship:** Think of an immigrant family that relies on relatives back home to know when a festival falls. The calendar becomes a reason to stay in touch. Polycarp's need to ask the Jewish community worked the same way, binding the two groups together.
- **Moving Easter today:** Picture a denomination announcing that Easter will move to a different month. The outcry gives a sense of why the second-century dispute was so heated.
- **Remembering honestly:** Nations that observe days of remembrance for their own historical wrongs, such as Holocaust remembrance in Germany, show what Rudolph is asking for. The aim is to honor what was good while still naming what was wrong.
- **Easter and Passover in your own calendar:** Notice each year how the Western, Orthodox, and Jewish dates line up or don't. Use the differences as a chance to teach this history.
- **Practical Passover participation:** Look for community Seders at a local Messianic synagogue instead of holding a Gentile-only Seder at home.
- **Responding online:** When you see deicide accusations or antisemitic tropes on social media, correct them clearly and with good information.
- **Choosing where to study:** Marty's advice is to find a teacher whose work speaks to you and go study under them. Rudolph teaches at The King's University in Southlake, Texas.

### Summary

For its 500th episode, BEMA paired a celebration with a sobering history lesson. Rabbi Dr. David Rudolph showed how something that looks like a technical question, when to celebrate Easter, carries the story of the church's relationship with the Jewish people. At first, Gentile believers like Polycarp gladly relied on their Jewish brothers and sisters to know when to celebrate, and Epiphanius describes that shared timing as a deliberate sign of unity. After Jerusalem fell in 135, Rome took over as the center and the disagreement grew bitter. At Nicaea, Constantine cut the calendar loose from Jewish reckoning in language that has fed antisemitism ever since. Rudolph's response is to face this history honestly, without condemning the church as a whole. That means rejecting the deicide charge, protecting Jewish neighbors, and, for Gentiles who want to share in Israel's feasts, celebrating *with* Jewish people.
