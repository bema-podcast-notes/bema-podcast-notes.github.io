### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** BEMA 506: Discipleship (*Talmidim*), Spiritual Parenting and the Third Pillar  
**Hosts:** Marty Solomon and Brent Billings, with guest Elle Grover Fricks  
**Focus:** The Hebrew, Greek and Latin words for "disciple"; Deuteronomy 4:10, 11:19, 31:12; Psalm 34:11; Jeremiah 9:14; Isaiah 30:20–21; Isaiah 54:13; 1 Corinthians 4:15; 1 Timothy 1:2; Titus 1:4; Philemon 10; 1 John 2:1; 1 Peter 5:13; the sending of the seventy (or seventy-two) in Luke 10; the Great Commission in Matthew 28

This episode is part of a series on "nurturing the roots" of the BEMA pillars, and it focuses on the third pillar, discipleship. Elle Grover Fricks starts with the origins of the words for "disciple" in Latin, Hebrew and Greek. She then traces a pattern through the Old Testament and the New Testament letters: the language of discipling keeps appearing next to the language of children. From that pattern she argues that making disciples often means becoming a **spiritual parent**. That role differs from a professor or a coffee-chat mentor because it means taking responsibility for someone while protecting their independence. The conversation covers how this role gets abused and why it is still worth restoring. It describes discipling as "the teacher flowing like water," explains the "I do, we do, you do" model seen in Jesus' ministry, and stresses praising what people do well. It ends with concrete practices from the hosts' own homes that can be adapted for discipling adults.

### Key Takeaways

- **A disciple is a learner.** The Latin *discipulus*, the Hebrew *talmid* (from the root *lamad*, "to learn/teach") and the Greek *manthanō* ("to learn") all mean a pupil or student, someone who receives teaching.
- **Discipleship carries authority.** The Hebrew letter *lamed* is pictured as a shepherd's or teacher's staff, which stood for authority more than punishment.
- **Disciple language and child language go together.** In the Torah, the Prophets and the Writings, verses about discipling usually mention children, sometimes literally and sometimes as a metaphor.
- **The apostles saw themselves as spiritual parents.** Paul, John and Peter call the people they invested in "my child," "my little children" and "my son."
- **Spiritual parenting holds two things in tension:** taking real responsibility for someone while keeping their independence and agency intact. Unhealthy discipleship flips this, taking away independence and neglecting responsibility.
- **Abuse doesn't cancel the good thing.** Spiritual authority can be twisted, but that is a reason to restore it with humility and care, not to throw it out.
- **People grow into who God made them to be.** A hydrangea can't be turned into a tulip, but it still needs weeding, watering and protection from frost.
- **A good teacher knows what to send downstream, and when.** The Hebrew *moreh* ("teacher") pictures teaching that flows like water and fits what the learner needs right now.
- **I do, we do, you do.** Jesus modeled this progression and moved back and forth between the stages, with debriefs and correction along the way.
- **Name the good.** Disciple-makers tend to assume good behavior and only point out mistakes, which crushes people's spirit. Say out loud what people are doing right.
- **Consistency matters more than perfection.** Keep showing up even when a day "was a bust," because over time those patterns build a culture that blesses the whole household.

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. The Etymology of "Disciple"

| Language | Word | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Latin | *discipulus* | pupil, student |
| Hebrew | *talmid* (plural *talmidim*), from the root *lamad* | one who receives teaching; the root means "to learn/teach" (the same root gives us *Talmud*) |
| Greek | *manthanō* (the noun is *mathētēs*) | to learn; a learner |

- Elle admits these meanings aren't surprising, but she finds it useful to start from something concrete.
- **The *lamed* staff:** The ancient picture behind the Hebrew letter *lamed* is a staff. In Hebrew thought a staff stands mainly for **authority**. When God tells Moses at the burning bush to pick his staff back up, the point is that Moses must accept the authority he has been given, even though he is unsure of himself. Authority is built into the discipleship relationship, and it is meant to be used well.

#### 2. The Disciple-and-Child Pattern in the Tanakh

Elle reads several verses, translating *lamad* as "disciple":

1. **Deuteronomy 4:10:** "…that they may disciple to fear me… and that they may disciple their children."
2. **Deuteronomy 11:19:** "You shall disciple your children… when you sit in your house, when you walk by the way…"
3. **Deuteronomy 31:12:** "Gather the people together, men, women, and children, and the stranger… that they may be discipled."
4. **Psalm 34:11:** "Come, you children, listen to me; I will disciple you in the fear of the Lord."
5. **Jeremiah 9:14:** Israel followed the false gods "which their fathers discipled them."
6. **Isaiah 54:13:** "All your children shall be discipled of the Lord, and great shall be the peace of your children."

What the hosts noticed:
- **Marty:** The verses keep mentioning **children**, they include **foreigners**, and there is often a **gathering** of people.
- **Brent:** The verses come from all three sections of the Hebrew Bible, the **Torah, the Prophets and the Writings**. The pattern runs through the whole Tanakh.
- **Elle's main point:** Sometimes "children" means actual children, as in the Shema passage. Sometimes it is a metaphor; even *Bnei Yisrael* ("sons/children of Israel") puts the whole nation in a child's position. **Learning from God means coming as a child.**

#### 3. Jesus' Young Disciples and the Childlike Learner

- Jesus' disciples were probably very young, some of them perhaps teenagers. Elle jokes that Jesus was "a youth minister."
- Jesus teaches that you must become like a child to enter the kingdom (Matthew 18:3).
- **Children are eager learners.** They come with humility, openness and excitement. Elle's five-year-old son Lewis shouted "Bioluminescence!" during a lesson on nocturnal animals because he knew something about the topic. That is the energy we should bring to the things of God.
- **Marty's connection (Matthew 13:52):** A teacher who becomes a learner again is like a homeowner who brings new and old treasures out of a storehouse. Humility, curiosity and wonder keep us finding more treasure.

#### 4. The Teacher as Parent in Early Jewish Tradition

- **Talmud (Sanhedrin 19b):** Whoever teaches another person's child Torah is regarded by Scripture as if they had fathered that child.
- **Philo of Alexandria:** Teachers are "fathers of the soul."
- So the Tanakh's link between teaching and parenting carries into the Jewish world of the first century.

#### 5. The Spiritual Parent in the New Testament Letters

| Passage | Language |
|---|---|
| 1 Corinthians 4:15 | "You have countless guides in Christ, but not many fathers; I became your father in Christ Jesus through the gospel." |
| 1 Timothy 1:2 | "To Timothy, my true child in the faith" |
| Titus 1:4 | "To Titus, my true child in a common faith" |
| Philemon 10 | "My child, Onesimus, whose father I became in my imprisonment" |
| 1 John 2:1 | "My little children, I am writing these things to you…" |
| 1 Peter 5:13 | "…and so does Mark, my son." |

- **Conclusion:** Everyone's ultimate Father is God, and everyone is a disciple of Jesus (Matthew 23:8–9: one Father, one Rabbi). Still, **living out the Great Commission includes becoming a spiritual parent to others.**
- Spiritual parenthood can grow out of years of ministry, as with Timothy, or form quickly in a crisis, as with Onesimus, whom Paul met in prison.

#### 6. Spiritual Parent vs. Professor vs. Coffee Mentor

- **Professor:** focused on content and tasks.
- **Coffee mentor:** listens, reflects back, offers wisdom when invited, but has no authority. You advise someone to leave an unhealthy relationship, and two weeks later they say "We're engaged!"
- **Spiritual parent:** has the same limits (you can't control someone's destiny) but carries **extra responsibility.** "This is my kid. If they're not doing well, it's on me to find out where they are and what's going on."
- **Elle's illustration:** Giving someone directions home is different from walking them home. Calling an Uber for someone at a party is different from saying "You're not safe to drive. I'll go with you."
- **Why discipleship is rare:** Taking responsibility for someone is scary and takes a lot more work than a monthly coffee.

#### 7. The Paradox of Responsibility and Independence

- Healthy discipleship means **taking responsibility while protecting independence.**
- **Marty's warning:** Unhealthy spiritual leaders reverse this. They take away people's independence, neglect responsibility, and still call it discipleship. Turn a good approach "a couple of degrees" and it quickly becomes manipulation, control and forced conformity to a program, house or set of rules, all justified as "being like first-century rabbis."
- **Cultural pushback:** Individualism, especially in white American Protestant culture ("It's just me and Jesus"), resists any human authority. Some of that resistance comes from real harm. Elle asks whether the good version can be restored. The early church called its leaders *abba* and *amma* (father and mother), and Orthodox and Catholic traditions still use those titles to express care and relationship.

#### 8. The Hydrangea Metaphor

- A child (or disciple) who will grow into a hydrangea can't be made into a tulip.
- The gardener still has work to do: weed, remove pests, water, protect from frost.
- **The two ways to fail:**
  - *Authoritarian:* forcing people into a copy of ourselves.
  - *Laissez-faire:* "Depression is just your personality; nothing I can do," which leaves people to fend for themselves.
- **Marty's application:** Each plant needs different care. A disciple-maker asks, "What are you? How do I care for you? When do I prune?"
- **Parenting has two sides:** Help people discover their own gifts, *and* hold to shared values ("In this house we speak with integrity" or "We seek Jesus' glory, not our own"). Discipleship should be **consensual**: the relationship is named and agreed on, not done "secretly."

#### 9. *Moreh*: The Teacher Who Flows Like Water

- **Isaiah 30:20–21:** "Your Teacher will no longer hide… your eyes will see your Teacher, and your ears will hear a word behind you: 'This is the way; walk in it,' when you turn to the right or to the left."
- *Moreh* ("teacher") is linked to the root *yarah*, which is also connected to the early rain. Elle explains it as "to flow like water into me."
- **What this means in practice:** A disciple-maker has a reservoir of knowledge and relationship with God. The skill is knowing **what to send downstream, and when.** Unloading trivia (such as the etymology of "Sadducee") on the wrong person has no effect. But if you notice someone finds their worth in performing spiritual disciplines, or doesn't know what "relationship with God" means, that is the moment to talk about grace or about spiritual practices.
- **First, figure out who they are, where they are, and what they're struggling with.**
- **Brent's caveat:** Jesus' disciples were very different from one another, and at some point Jesus let Judas go his own way. Some personalities like clean frameworks, and those help for a season, but discipleship eventually has to deal with complexity and nuance.

#### 10. I Do, We Do, You Do

| Stage | Dishwasher example | Jesus' ministry |
|---|---|---|
| **I do** | "Watch me put the silverware away." | Disciples watch Jesus teach, heal, rest and pray |
| **We do** | "Help me! You remembered where the spoons go, nice work!" | Teaching them to pray (the Lord's Prayer, which some compare to an abbreviated *Amidah*) |
| **You do** | "This is your chore now. I'm off to work." | Sending out the seventy (or seventy-two) two by two, and finally the Great Commission |

- **The process isn't a straight line.** When the disciples come back unable to heal someone, Jesus is honest with them ("You faithless generation…"), debriefs, gives pointers, shows them again, and sends them back out.
- **Affirmation runs through every stage.** The goal isn't just a well-unloaded dishwasher. It is a person growing into who they are.
- **A common gap:** Many discipleship relationships run only one way and never move through these stages.
- **Note on the number:** Marty explains that Luke 10 says seventy in some manuscripts and seventy-two in others, which may reflect different Jewish traditions about the number of nations.

#### 11. Name the Good

- **Marty's confession:** He assumes that 80% done right is just what's expected and only comments on the 20% done wrong, so the disciple hears only criticism. He says this "killed the spirit" of disciples early in his ministry, and he still struggles with it. Brent says he does the same thing.
- **Elle's insight from working with children:** Everyone, child or adult, craves connection. If attention only comes after misbehavior, the misbehavior continues (the child keeps jumping on the couch to get a parent to look up from the computer).
- **The biblical basis:** 1 Thessalonians 5:11, "Encourage one another and build each other up, just as you are doing." Our default should be speaking life.
- **Marty:** When his face lights up with delight at his kids, it has far more effect than criticism. Spiritually, saying "That's it! That moment was full of Jesus" shapes people more than any critique.
- **God as the delighted parent:** To God we are toddlers proud of tying our shoes. A loving parent celebrates anyway, and it "doesn't even feel like work because it's joy."

### Examples & Applications

#### The Solomon Household (Marty)

- **Holidays and the biblical calendar:** Recurring holidays teach the family's story year after year.
- **Preparing for bar and bat mitzvah:** For three years each child followed a discipline modeled on Marty's college discipleship program. They got up early, memorized texts, followed Dad during the school year and Mom in the summer, and then "graduated" into choosing their own spiritual practices.
- **Pulling back on purpose:** They chose not to "drown" their kids in discipline. They showed the value of the practices and then gave their kids room to choose.
- **Name and legacy documents:** On the day each child was born, Marty wrote out the meaning of the name and the legacy he hoped for. It hangs on the wall and he reads it aloud every birthday.
- **Public but not performative:** Blessings over the children at Sabbath candles happen even when friends are over. They don't seek an audience and don't avoid one.

#### The Billings Household (Brent, kids ages 7 and 5)

- **Text writing plan:** His seven-year-old is learning cursive (partly to read letters from grandparents), so Brent plans to write out Scripture by hand together with him.
- **Evening prayers:** The Lord's Prayer and others, first repeated after a parent and now said together.
- **Scripture memorization:** Led mostly by his wife Maggie. Their son memorized Genesis 1 at a young age.
- **Goal:** Lay foundations now and adjust as attention spans and interests grow.

#### The Grover Fricks Household (Elle, kids ages 5, 3 and 8 months), "the Drowning Them Era"

1. **Early-morning modeling (I do):** Elle does her text writing and reflective prayer around 5:15 a.m. with a sermon playing, so that if her mind wanders, it wanders toward God. Kids who sneak downstairs snuggle up and listen. Her son is sure one deep-voiced preacher is "Doc Hudson teaching about Jesus." Recently the kids found a dramatized audio Bible and were hooked.
2. **"God music" in the morning:** Worship music plays after her devotions. Her three-year-old protested at first, but the habit stuck, and now the kids ask for God music later in the day too.
3. **Children's Bibles at breakfast:** Around twelve children's Bibles and five catechism-style books, rotated for variety. Elle changes or skips lines she disagrees with as she reads. Her three-year-old looks for Jesus on every page. The stories spark real theological questions, though some mornings everyone is watching the baby smear banana instead, and that's fine.
4. **Memory verses in the car:** Her husband George and the older kids recite, call-and-response, the prophetic passages chosen at their baby dedications while they wait for school to open, so the last thing they hear before entering the world is a word about who they are in God.
5. **After-school conversations:** Every story about classmates, struggles or praise from a teacher becomes a chance to talk about the way of Jesus and each child's calling.
6. **Shabbat practices:** God music all day, the week's only movie (with ice cream), and a small treat if they have to run an errand.

#### Carrying These Practices Over to Adult Discipleship

- **Intentional conversations** about calling.
- **Personality assessments** (a big part of Elle's family culture), reviewed together to see which way they point.
- **Paying attention to dreams.**
- **Verses chosen for a person** as the Spirit leads.
- **Reading the text together** and working through hard questions together.

#### The Whole Household Benefits

- Elle learns from her kids' Bibles herself. For example, she noticed that God scattering languages at Babel instead of destroying people was unusual for the ancient Near East.
- Worship music blesses everyone in the house.
- **Brent's response:** He realized he already does many of these things and now wants to do them **with more intention**.
- **Marty:** "Quality over quantity." **Elle's revision:** Consistency matters even more than quality. Don't quit after one bad day.

### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

1. **The Hebrew letter pictures:** Look into ancient pictographic meanings of letters like *lamed*, what they can tell us, and their limits as evidence.
2. ***Moreh*, *yarah* and *Torah*:** Study the shared root behind "teacher," "early rain," "to shoot or aim" and "Torah," and what it suggests about teaching as direction.
3. **Sanhedrin 19b and Philo:** Read rabbinic and Hellenistic Jewish texts that describe teachers as parents.
4. **Paul as father and mother:** Compare 1 Corinthians 4:14–21 with 1 Thessalonians 2:7–12, where Paul uses both maternal and paternal images.
5. **Matthew 23:8–12:** How do "call no one father" and "you have one Rabbi" fit with Paul's "I became your father"?
6. **Luke 10 and the seventy or seventy-two:** Study the manuscript variant and its link to the Table of Nations in Genesis 10.
7. **Spiritual abuse and healthy authority:** Read about recovering from spiritual abuse and about models of accountable, consent-based mentoring.
8. **The *abbas* and *ammas* of the desert:** Explore the Desert Fathers and Mothers and the tradition of spiritual direction.
9. **The gradual release model in education:** Compare "I do, we do, you do" in teaching research with Jesus' approach.
10. **Positive reinforcement research:** Look at studies on praise-to-criticism ratios in parenting and leadership.
11. **Childlikeness in the Gospels:** Do a word study on children and "little ones" in Matthew 18–19.

### Personal Summary

This episode recasts discipleship as **spiritual parenting**. Elle starts with the plain meaning of *talmid*, a learner, and then shows how Scripture keeps pairing discipling with children. That pattern runs from Deuteronomy through Paul's "my true child" letters. The conversation is useful because it is honest about the risks. Spiritual authority has been badly misused, and Western individualism pushes back against it. Even so, Elle, Marty and Brent argue that the answer is to recover the good version: **take real responsibility for people while protecting who God made them to be.** Disciple-makers are gardeners, not sculptors. They tend hydrangeas without trying to turn them into tulips, let teaching flow like water when it is needed, and move people from "I do" to "we do" to "you do." Above all, they **name the good**, because delight shapes people more than criticism. The family practices in the second half of the episode, like early-morning sermons, God music, children's Bibles over cereal and memory verses in the car, show that discipleship is mostly faithful, consistent habits that bless everyone involved. The episode closes with a blessing: may we find spiritual parents, may we be good spiritual parents, and may we go and make disciples, knowing that Jesus is with us always, to the very end of the age.
