### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** BEMA 517: Behind the Book: *The Gospel of Being Human*  
**Hosts:** Brent Billings with Marty Solomon and Reed Dent  
**Focus:** How the book *The Gospel of Being Human: How Asking Better Questions of the Bible Reveals Who We Are* (NavPress) was written and published: writing habits, collaboration, editing, theological pushback, audience, titles and the book tour

This episode is a behind-the-scenes conversation, not a teaching episode on a biblical text. Marty Solomon has already given dozens of interviews about what the book says, so Brent Billings asks him and Reed Dent how it came to be. The three talk about how each author writes, the book's start in a conversation about Barbara Brown Taylor's *An Altar in the World*, and how co-authors kept one voice. They also cover their editor Caitlin Carlson's role and the heavy revision that theological writing goes through, especially the chapters on Eve and the "Romans Road." The talk then moves to who the book is for, how the book and chapters got their titles, and stories from the book tour. It closes with a tribute to Wendell Berry and a light-hearted lightning round. Along the way it shows that writing theology together works like "iron sharpening iron": careful critique tests and improves ideas about God and about being human.

### Key Takeaways

- **People write in different ways, and each way can work.** Reed writes in bits spread across his normal week, much like writing sermons. Marty goes away for eight days of "sleep, eat, write" and drafts a whole chapter in one sitting.
- **The book started as a friendship and a shared idea.** It grew out of reading *An Altar in the World* together and a walk spent talking about curiosity, attention and wonder. Marty had quoted Reed so often in his first book that the publisher asked him to stop. So he invited Reed to co-write the next one.
- **Good editors help you, not just correct you.** Caitlin Carlson cared about the authors and about the project. She helped bring out the best version of the book and handled the back-and-forth with the parent publisher.
- **Theology gets more scrutiny than method.** Their first book, on Jewish hermeneutics (ways of reading the Bible), got through easily. Theological claims drew much more review, especially the Eve chapter and the Romans Road chapters.
- **Pushback can be healthy.** Reed describes the long revision threads as "iron sharpening iron." He says there was no bad blood, only people checking whether the claims were true.
- **Cutting is part of the craft.** The first draft ran to about 70,000+ words, against a 55,000-word contract. Whole chunks had to go, including material both authors loved.
- **One voice was chosen on purpose.** Chapters aren't labeled by author, so the book reads as one piece. Marty and Reed teach in such similar ways that this worked.
- **Being bold without being harsh is hard.** Marty wanted to challenge the simplified evangelical "Romans Road" while respecting how sacred it is to many people. He wanted his statements to have "just enough teeth" to make readers stop and think again.
- **The book is for people who sense there is more.** Marty pictures someone holding a Bible and sensing "there's so much more going on here." Reed doesn't write for any particular audience. He writes "something universal, something human."
- **Books are also products.** Editors, marketers and publishers all shape the titles, length and packaging. The book's title, *The Gospel of Being Human*, came from Caitlin.

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. Two Approaches to the Writing Process

| | **Reed Dent** | **Marty Solomon** |
|---|---|---|
| **Setting** | Woodworking shop, home, office, local copy shop | A remote retreat where no one knows him (Scotland, for this book) |
| **Rhythm** | Spread through daily life; long stretches of staring, then bursts of typing | About 8 days devoted to sleep, eat, write; one chapter in the morning, one in the afternoon |
| **Goal** | Finish a set portion of a chapter, however long it takes | Get a whole chapter out in one stream of thought |
| **Source material** | About 3.5–4 of his 5 chapters began as sermons | Researched and drafted fresh |
| **Self-editing** | Usually edits as he goes; for this book he drafted freely and found the drafts "unintelligible" a month later | Gets everything out first, then cuts it up later |

- **Main idea:** No single method is right. What matters is knowing how you work best creatively.
- **"Write first, edit later":** Many writers say not to edit while drafting. Reed's chapters on Adam and Eve show both sides. Free drafting gets ideas onto the page fast, but those drafts need a lot of revision afterward.

#### 2. The Book's Origin Story

- **Where it started:** Marty and Reed sat in a driveway reading Barbara Brown Taylor's *An Altar in the World* aloud. (They jokingly argue over who read to whom.)
- **The conversation:** On a walk afterward, Marty talked through ideas about curiosity, attention and wonder. Reed kept putting into words exactly what Marty was reaching for.
- **The practical reason:** In Marty's first book, *Asking Better Questions of the Bible*, Reed had essentially written chapter five, on Proverbs and wisdom, through all the quotes Marty used. Making Reed a co-author solved the "too much quoting" problem.
- **How it changed:** Over time the publisher shaped the original idea into **ten stories**, each one built around an assumption we hold about ourselves.

#### 3. The Editor's Role and the Revision Process

- **Common myth:** An editor is a critic with a red pen.
- **What they found:** Caitlin Carlson was "a very loving editor." Her job was "to bring out the best thing that can be from the project."
- **How they worked:** They traded video messages on **Marco Polo**, a video walkie-talkie app, instead of long email threads.
- **Mediating with the publisher:** Caitlin helped work through the "policing" from the larger parent publisher, which happens to theological content more than to books about method.
- **Revision fatigue:** Marty says revision "sucks the life out of the thing," leaving it over-dissected. Then, reading the finished book aloud for the audiobook, he fell in love with it all over again. In his words, revision is "this beautiful part of the thing becoming a thing."
- **The irony Reed points out:** Hermeneutics leads somewhere. Once you learn to read the Bible differently, theological questions follow. "What did you think a hermeneutic was for?"

#### 4. Theological Negotiation and "Iron Sharpening Iron"

- **The Adam chapter (hardest for Reed personally):** The BEMA audience already knows this ground well. The challenge was to make it worth reading without just adding "more noise," because it still had to come first as the foundation.
- **The Eve chapter (Reed's favorite, and the most debated):** Its theology challenged some reviewers, and the comment thread on the Word document got very long. Reed treats this as healthy: people with something at stake asking, "Is this legit? Is this actually true?"
- **Proverbs 27:17 ("iron sharpens iron"):** Brent uses this picture, and Reed agrees it is the right one. Theological disagreement, done in good faith, sharpens ideas.

#### 5. The "Romans Road" as a Bundle of Assumptions

- **What the Romans Road is:** A common evangelical way of sharing the gospel. It strings verses from Romans (for example 3:23, 6:23, 5:8 and 10:9–10) into a simple step-by-step path to salvation.
- **Marty's balancing act:**
  - He is **not** trying to "undo every truth" the Romans Road points to.
  - He **is** trying to say something "with just enough teeth" to move readers "from point A to point B."
  - The danger: if you remove all the teeth, a statement becomes "safe," but it no longer says anything.
- **Reed's view:** Every chapter in the book deals with one assumption. The Romans Road chapter is different because it is "a package of assumptions": one big assumption made of many smaller ones. Reed wishes more of it had stayed in and thinks it "could be a book by itself."
- **Brent's view:** The ruts in how people read Romans run deep. Romans is reduced to "six simple steps" when it is "a deeper and richer thing." He says even regular BEMA listeners need to be reminded of this.

#### 6. Co-Authoring and a Unified Voice

- **How they divided the work:** Most of the collaboration happened while planning and whiteboarding the book. Then each wrote his own chapters, handed them off, and the other edited them.
- **What Marty values in Reed:** Reed is willing to say "move this, change that, get rid of that." Once Marty sees something that makes sense to him, he finds it hard to reimagine it.
- **"One project, not two bags":** They didn't try to stuff "Marty's bag of things" and "Reed's bag of things" into one book. They designed a single project and then divided it up.
- **Why chapters aren't labeled:** They decided with the publisher to keep the reading unbroken. In some co-authored books, such as Brad Gray and Brad Nelson's recent book, each chapter names its author, which makes sense when the illustrations are very personal. Marty dislikes the alternative of writing "I (Marty)" in parentheses all through the text.
- **A funny result:** On tour, almost all the questions Marty got were about Reed's chapters.

#### 7. Audience: "Featherless Bipeds"

- **The publisher's answer:** Adults aged 25–65 who are interested in Bible study. Marty jokes that, as a college minister, he hopes it's more like 18–65, and plenty of readers over 65 are on board too.
- **Rob Bell's line:** Asked who his audience was, Bell answered, "humans, featherless bipeds." Marty thinks of this whenever the question comes up.
- **Marty's picture:** Someone standing in a spiritual space with a look of awareness, sensing a "doorway" between something bigger and their everyday life. He wants to say to them: "Don't look away, don't run away, don't give up. It is here."
- **Reed's approach:** He doesn't picture any audience while writing; he just follows the ideas. Targeting an audience is marketing's job. The book is "something universal, something human," not "a technical manual for diesel engines."

#### 8. Titles, Tables of Contents and Structure

- **Why the table of contents matters:** Brent and Marty both read it first to see the book's "stream of consciousness" and to know where they are as they read.
- **The temptation to be clever:** Both authors tend to write clever titles that hint at obscure references. Clearer titles like "When Failure Isn't Failure" and "When Winning Isn't Winning" do more to orient readers.
- **How the book got its title:**
  - Working title: *Asking Better Questions of Ourselves*.
  - Caitlin suggested *The Gospel of Being Human*, and Reed loved it immediately.
  - About 20 emails followed over the subtitle's prepositions and articles before it settled as *How Asking Better Questions of the Bible Reveals Who We Are*.
- **Section headings:** Marty added headings inside chapters to break up Reed's continuous prose. This shaped Reed's preaching too: his sermons now have "chapter" slides.

#### 9. Publishing as an Industry

- Books are **products** that must be packaged, marketed and sold, so many people have a hand in the final result.
- Platforms now count for a lot. Publishers often sign authors who already have a large audience that will buy the book, not mainly for the quality of their writing.
- Reed calls himself lucky. Many talented writers never get into publishing, and for him the chance "fell into [his] lap."

### Examples & Applications

1. **Writing retreats for creative work:** Marty's eight days alone in Scotland show the value of time with nothing else to do. A pastor drafting a sermon series, or a student writing a thesis, might set aside a protected weekend instead of squeezing work into scattered hours.
2. **Letting a draft rest:** Reed's discovery that his "gold" drafts read badly a month later is a common experience. Waiting a while before rereading lets you see your work with fresh eyes.
3. **Reading aloud:** Marty fell back in love with the book while recording the audiobook, and Reed calls reading aloud "almost a sacred spiritual act." Small groups can do the same by reading Scripture passages aloud together, not just silently on their own.
4. **Welcoming critique in community:** The long comment thread on the Eve chapter is a model for church life. Disagreeing about theology can be loving and useful when everyone shares the goal of finding what is true.
5. **Rethinking simple gospel summaries:** Leaders who use the Romans Road might teach the full letter of Romans first, so the "steps" sit inside Paul's larger story instead of standing alone.
6. **Using structure to help listeners:** Reed's new sermon "chapter" slides show how headings help people follow a long argument. The same works for teaching outlines, podcasts and presentations.
7. **Putting down roots, as Wendell Berry advised:** Berry's advice was to "stop some place and stay there, learn the history of it and the names of the plants and animals and the neighbors." Reed's contented life in Kirksville, Missouri, and the thriving local book-tour gatherings show the value of being deeply rooted in one place.
8. **Faith communities as hosts:** Book-tour stops such as Bridgeway Christian Church in Sacramento and the long-running launch party in Indiana show how local churches become hubs for learning together.

### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

- **Barbara Brown Taylor's *An Altar in the World*:** The book that sparked this one. How do its practices of attention and wonder show up in *The Gospel of Being Human*?
- **The "three Bs": Wendell Berry, Walter Brueggemann and Frederick Buechner.** These are writers Marty and Reed consider deeply formative. One chapter of the book takes its title from them. Buechner's collections of sermons, reflections and his "dictionary of terms" also show the format Reed would like to try.
- **The Romans Road and Romans as a whole:** Compare the step-by-step method with readings of Romans that focus on Jews and Gentiles becoming one people and on Paul's larger story.
- **Theology of Adam and Eve:** Revisit BEMA's teaching on Genesis 1–3, especially readings of Eve and the Hebrew ideas behind *ezer kenegdo* (often translated "helper suitable").
- **Proverbs and wisdom literature:** Reed essentially wrote this chapter in Marty's first book.
- **Illustrated sermons and picture-book theology:** Reed's idea that the images and the text carry the message together.
- **The ethics and economics of Christian publishing:** How the importance of an author's platform shapes which theological voices get heard.
- **Marty's next book:** It covers relationships, empathy and other people, building on this book's theological groundwork.

### Personalized Summary

This episode shows the human side of making a book about being human. Reed writes in bursts woven through ordinary life, while Marty writes in solitary marathons, and together they turned a driveway reading and a walk into a full theological book. Their editor, Caitlin Carlson, guided it through cuts of about 15,000 words and long theological debates. The hardest parts were the most important ones: the familiar Adam chapter, the contested Eve chapter, and the Romans Road chapters, which needed both reverence and teeth. Both authors see that scrutiny as iron sharpening iron, not conflict. For you as a reader, the lesson matches the book's message: honest questions, careful critique and community make our understanding of God and ourselves richer. Like Wendell Berry's call to stay put and know your place, the book asks you to slow down, pay attention, and step through the doorway between the bigger reality you sense and the life you are living.
