### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** 515: The Daughters of Zelophehad (the Bible's first "class action lawsuit")  
**Hosts:** Marty Solomon and Brent Billings, with guest Elle Grover Fricks  
**Focus:** Numbers 27:1–11 and Joshua 17:3–4

This episode is part of Elle Grover's series on the women who appear in short stories scattered through Joshua and Judges. It looks at the five daughters of Zelophehad. Their father died in the wilderness without sons, so under the existing system his family's land would have gone to his brothers. The sisters bring their case publicly before Moses, Eleazar the priest, the leaders, and the whole assembly. God's reply is immediate: "The daughters of Zelophehad speak rightly." God then rewrites the inheritance law at every level. Years later, in Joshua, the sisters return to make sure the promise is kept, and Joshua honors it right away. The hosts look at what the names mean, the Hebrew vocabulary, and a rabbinic midrash about how the case climbed the chain of leadership. Elle closes with her "biblical womanhood corner," a list of what these women actually do in the story. She contrasts that with cultural ideas of what "biblical womanhood" means.

### Key Takeaways

- The daughters are named twice, in Numbers and again in Joshua. In a Bible where many women go unnamed, that is an honor from both God and the narrator.
- God's answer opens with the verdict: "rightly." There is no suspense and no delay.
- God doesn't make a one-time exception. God changes the whole inheritance system, setting the order as sons, then daughters, then brothers, then uncles, then the nearest relative.
- The sisters come prepared. They expect the objection that their father died with Korah's rebellion, and they answer it before anyone raises it.
- They act together as a group. Their case is about policy, not personal attacks, and they bring it in public.
- They ask plainly for what they need. They don't hedge, drop hints, or manage the leaders' egos.
- Nobody tells them to "just get married." The text expects that answer and pointedly doesn't give it.
- Moses, Joshua, and God all listen, and justice is both decided and carried out without delay.
- They follow up with a new leader (Joshua) after the old one (Moses) is gone.
- Many of these traits are not specifically "feminine." They are what a faithful follower of God looks like, and here they show up in women.

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. The Story the Names Tell

Elle translates each name in the family line. Read in order, the names sketch a story of trauma slowly giving way to delight:

| Name | Meaning (per Elle) |
|---|---|
| Joseph (Yoseph) | "He will add / gather" |
| Manasseh (Menashe) | "Forgetting," from Joseph's attempt to move past his pain |
| Machir | "Seller" |
| Gilead | "Witness heap" |
| Hepher | "Dug / humiliated" |
| Zelophehad | "First rupture" or "shadow of fear" |
| Mahlah | "Diseased" |
| Noa | "Wanderer" |
| Hoglah | "Partridge" |
| Milcah | "Queen" |
| Tirzah | "She will delight" |

- Brent points out that things seem to "get better" with each daughter. The line runs from sickness and wandering to royalty and delight.
- Manasseh's name comes from Joseph trying to forget his suffering (Genesis 41:51). The family comes from a line of people trying to heal from deep wounds.
- Reading names this way is common in the Torah and the wider Tanakh, where a name often comments on the story.

#### 2. Key Hebrew Terms

- **Eleazar (El-Azar).** Elle translates it "God is our ally." The root *ezer* is the same word used for the woman in Genesis 2. In war stories it describes a foreign king who comes to rescue Israel, the kind of help America gave France in World War II, not a "sweet little helper." Marty notes how fitting the name is for a priest: his role is help and allyship, not gatekeeping.
- **Mishpat.** This is a long-running friendly disagreement between Elle and Marty:
  - Marty usually translates *mishpat* as **justice**.
  - Elle translates it as **judicial matter** or **judicial decree**. The *mem* prefix marks place or product, and the root is *shaphat*, "to judge," so a *mishpat* is what a judge says. She saves "justice" for *tsedek*, partly because of a Canaanite deity named Tsedek, a god of justice.
  - In practice they mostly agree: a judicial decree should embody justice.
- **The tent of witnesses or appointed meeting.** "The witnesses" here means the whole assembled community. The sisters spoke in front of everyone.
- **"Shaved down."** This is their phrase for their father's name being erased from the clan. It reflects real concern for family honor and legacy.
- **"Kin-flesh."** Elle's rendering of the word for nearest relative, a nod to Robert Alter's translation.

#### 3. Getting Ahead of the Korah Objection

- The sisters say their father was **not** part of Korah's rebellion, the group the earth swallowed (Numbers 16).
- They know someone will argue, "Your father died under God's judgment, so accept it." They deal with that argument before it comes up.
- They also own the truth: "he died in his own mistakes." They don't claim he was perfect. They argue that his failings shouldn't erase his legacy. (Elle notes the Hebrew could also be read as describing Korah's sin rather than their father's.)
- Marty says the midrash repeatedly stresses that these women **knew their scripture**: the promises, the curses, and what happened to Korah. Elle adds that they also knew God's character, a God who cares about justice and about everyone having enough.

#### 4. A Thorough Fix, Not a Band-Aid

- God's ruling starts with the verdict: "The daughters of Zelophehad speak rightly." Elle compares this to Ryan Seacrest on *American Idol* dragging out results across commercial breaks. God does the opposite.
- God goes further than the request. Brent: "We were just asking for this one contingency and you give us like ten contingencies."
- The new inheritance order is sons, then daughters, then the father's brothers, then the grandfather's brothers, then the nearest relative.
- The result is a "carved law of judicial decree," a permanent statute that fixes the whole system rather than granting one exception.

#### 5. The Midrash: Climbing the Chain of Leadership

Marty shares one of his favorite midrashim (from the Aggadah). It tries to explain why Moses had to "bring their case before the Lord":

- One rabbi says Moses simply didn't know the answer.
- **Rabbi Simeon ben Lakish** disagrees. In his telling, the women first went to the chiefs of tens, who said, "This is above our jurisdiction." The chiefs of fifties, hundreds, and thousands, and then the princes, all deferred upward in turn. Eleazar sent them to Moses. Moses saw that everyone below him had shown humility, and he said, "If I rule, I'll be taking honor that isn't mine. I too have a superior." So he took the case to God.
- **Marty's generous reading:** humility runs from the bottom to the top, and that leaves room for **God** to declare, "This is what Torah is designed to do." If any leader had ruled along the way, God's affirmation would never have happened.
- **Elle's reading:** it highlights the women's persistence, in the spirit of the parable of the persistent widow. They kept going, and they never arrived with a list of complaints about the leaders who passed them along.
- An affirmation straight from the mouth of the Lord carries far more weight than one from a chief of tens.

#### 6. The Follow-Up in Joshua 17

- Years later, after Moses has died and Israel is in the land, the sisters go to Eleazar, Joshua, and the leaders and remind them what God commanded.
- Joshua doesn't say "things have changed" or "we'll form a committee." He gives them the land immediately, "according to the mouth of the LORD."
- Brent: this is "not like a modern class action lawsuit." There is no committee, no theological review, and no "these things take time."

#### 7. Elle's "Biblical Womanhood Corner"

Elle lists what biblical womanhood looks like **in this story**. She is clear that she isn't shaming any community or leader. She wants to separate what is biblical from what is cultural.

1. **Communal, not individualist.** All five sisters stand together. Brent notes that this fits the communal outlook of the whole ancient society.
2. **Bold and persistent.** They go to the top of the hierarchy and keep going until the injustice is fixed. Marty connects this to the women of Exodus, who refuse to settle, like the midwives Shiphrah and Puah.
3. **Focused on policy change.** They criticize no individual and spread no stories about leaders. They ask for a change to the rule.
4. **Willing to address injustice publicly.** They don't take Moses aside to protect his ego, and they don't try to manage how he'll react. That is his job and God's.
5. **Unafraid to name grief and loss.** They speak about their father's death without worrying about being called whiny, dramatic, or too emotional.
6. **Strategic.** They plan their argument instead of wailing at someone's feet, the way women plead in Greco-Roman stories. Marty calls it "Esther vibes."
7. **Family-minded.** They care about their father's honor and name, as Tamar did. They aren't throwing off family for independence.
8. **Willing to oppose their own family.** Their uncles benefit from the old rule. The sisters don't attack the uncles, but they don't back down either.
9. **Heard by those at the top.** Moses doesn't wave it off as "some woman's issue."
10. **Not told to "go get married."** The women appear to be unmarried, and nobody suggests marriage as the fix.
11. **Heard by God, who works through the existing structure.** God doesn't remove Moses. God listens and makes the change.
12. **Asking directly, without hedging or hinting.** They don't say, "What do you think would be right, Moses?" They say, "Give us a holding."
13. **Following up.** They don't just "trust the process." They go to Joshua to make sure the promise is kept.

#### 8. Public vs. Private: The Sisters and Tamar Side by Side

- Marty asks how this public boldness fits with Tamar (Genesis 38), who confronts Judah privately.
- Elle's answer: deal with private sins privately and public problems publicly. She loosely paraphrases a principle she attributes to 1 Timothy; 1 Timothy 5:20 is the closest passage, though she hedges on the reference.
  - Judah's sin was personal and private, so Tamar sent a discreet message.
  - The inheritance rule affected every woman in Israel, so it belonged in public, where fixing it would help everyone.
- Brent adds that going public carried **risk**. If the ruling had gone against them, they would have been embarrassed in front of everyone. Their willingness shows how sure they were of their case.

### Examples & Applications

- **Workplace or ministry transitions:** Elle compares Joshua 17 to being promised something by a boss who then leaves, and having to raise it with the new person. Joshua honoring Moses' word is the model for incoming leaders.
- **The *American Idol* comparison:** God doesn't make people wait for a dramatic reveal. Leaders can deliver good news and fairness clearly and quickly.
- **America helping France in World War II:** this illustrates *ezer* as strong, rescuing help, which changes how we read Eleazar's name and the woman as "helper" in Genesis 2.
- **"Just go get married":** Elle says churches have often offered marriage as the answer to women's problems. This text is a counterexample.
- **The habit of hinting:** Elle admits she often asks for things indirectly ("Have you thought about…?") to avoid a negative reaction. The sisters show a more direct way.
- **Protest vs. direct appeal:** protest is "the tool of people who aren't listened to." Where leaders are reachable, the sisters start with a direct, honest request.
- **For leaders:** Marty calls the list "a helpful list for me as a leader who's not a woman." It is a guide for hearing and acting on justice concerns.
- **Tribute to "BEMA Josh":** Marty and Brent remember a former team member who raised justice issues with persistence and strategy, without personal attacks. He followed up, as the daughters did.

### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

- **Numbers 36:** the sequel, where Manasseh's clan leaders raise a counter-concern and the daughters are told to marry within their tribe so the land stays put. How does this complicate or complete the story?
- **East or west Manasseh?** Marty's open question. Joshua 17:5–6 places the daughters' portions in western Manasseh, separate from Gilead and Bashan east of the Jordan. What does that mean given Ray Vander Laan's emphasis on the "fields of Manasseh"?
- **Achsah (Joshua 15; Judges 1):** the next episode's subject, a woman who also asks for land, with some notable differences.
- **Mishpat vs. tsedek:** a word study on how Hebrew separates judicial rulings from righteousness and justice.
- **The *ezer* word study:** every use of *ezer* in the Tanakh, especially in war stories and in Genesis 2.
- **Rabbinic sources:** the Talmud's discussion of the daughters (Bava Batra 119) and the Sifrei on Numbers, which praise the sisters as wise and learned.
- **Women who refuse to settle in the Torah:** Shiphrah and Puah, Moses' mother and sister, Pharaoh's daughter, Tamar, and how they shape the Torah's picture of faithfulness.
- **Prescriptive vs. descriptive:** how should communities decide which of these traits are meant as patterns to follow?
- **Moses' possible errors:** Elle's example of "first born who breaks the womb" versus "first male" (Exodus 13:2 vs. 13:12) and how Torah handles leaders' interpretive mistakes.

### Personalized Summary

This episode shows that justice doesn't have to be slow, grudging, or a one-time exception. Five sisters, whose names move from "diseased" to "she will delight," stood in front of the whole community and asked plainly for what was right. They prepared their argument, owned their father's flaws, avoided attacks on anyone, and didn't soften their request to protect the leaders' feelings. Moses was humble enough to take the case to God instead of defending the old rule. God answered "rightly" at once and rewrote the whole system, and Joshua later kept that promise without hesitation. Elle's three takeaways sum it up: be like the daughters, bold, persistent, and unafraid of how you're perceived; be like Moses, willing to look "wrong" in order to listen to outsiders and do what's right; and be like Joshua, quick to follow through on God's commands. For anyone in leadership, the story offers a practical picture of what it looks like to hear a cry for justice and act on it.
