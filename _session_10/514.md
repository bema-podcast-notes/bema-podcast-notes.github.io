### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** 514: Rahab, the Surprise Matriarch in Jesus' Family Tree  
**Hosts:** Brent Billings, with guest teacher Elle Grover Fricks and guest Reed Dent  
**Focus:** Joshua (Yehoshua) 2:1–17, the story of Rahab (Rachav) in Jericho (Yericho)

In this episode Elle Grover Fricks leads Brent Billings and Reed Dent through her own translation of Joshua 2, the first of several stories about "un-underlined" women in Joshua and Judges. The previous episode asked whether the conquest, as it is usually imagined, really reflects the heart of God. This episode builds on that question by looking closely at Rahab, a Canaanite sex worker who hides two Israelite spies, deceives the king of Jericho, and negotiates rescue for her family. The group points out how much of the Exodus story echoes through the passage. They also look at the meanings of names and key Hebrew words, and at the comedy and cinematic pacing of the account. Elle closes by asking what Rahab's story adds to the popular idea of "biblical womanhood," and uses Rahab's layered faith as a model for having the courage to join a new spiritual family.

### Key Takeaways

- The names in Joshua 2:1 carry meaning. Yehoshua means "Yah delivers," Nun means "resprout," Shittim means "acacia grove," Yericho means "his moon" or "fragrant," and Rachav means "spacious." Together they show that rescue is God's story from the very first verse.
- The passage is full of **Exodus echoes**: the "melting" of hearts, the drying of the Sea of Reeds, spies hidden among flax stalks (like Moses hidden among the reeds), a red cord at the window, and blood "upon their own heads." These all recall Passover and the escape from Egypt.
- Rahab is called a *zonah*, and Elle reads the word plainly as a prostitute rather than an innkeeper. Even so, she becomes a hero of faith and appears in Hebrews 11 and in Jesus' genealogy (Matthew 1:5).
- Rahab uses Israel's own story, and its God, to argue for compassion. This fits a recurring biblical pattern in which the "wrong people," such as the sailors and Ninevites in Jonah, grasp God's mercy better than the insiders do.
- The spies answer with honor, not condemnation: "Our souls under yours unto death." They promise to "do *chesed* and *emet*," meaning active compassion and firm faithfulness.
- The cord Rahab lowers the spies on is linked to the **measuring line** used later for dividing the land. That makes her rescue a small picture of how the land was meant to be shared: through unexpected kindness and trust in God.
- Rahab's story widens narrow cultural pictures of "biblical womanhood." She is financially independent, unmarried, takes charge, gives orders, is hospitable at great cost to herself, and has no allegiance to king and country.
- Her faith has four parts: she is **willing to believe** what she heard, she gives **intellectual assent** to who God is, she **trusts God's hospitality** to make room for her, and she has the **courage to join a new people**.

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. Names as Theology

Elle's translation renders the meaning of each proper name, which shows the themes the narrator has built in:

1. **Yehoshua (Joshua):** "Yah delivers." Rescue is built into the leader's identity.
2. **Bin-Nun:** "Son of resprouting." This fits a people whose identity is growing back after slavery.
3. **Shittim:** "Acacia grove." Most of the tabernacle was made from acacia wood. It recalls Joshua's first appearances in Exodus, where he stays near the tent of meeting just to be close to God's presence.
4. **Yericho (Jericho):** "His moon" or "fragrant."
5. **Rachav (Rahab):** "Spacious." Elle offers three readings:
    - A crude pun on her profession.
    - A reference to her **hospitality** toward outsiders.
    - A callback to God's **promise of a "spacious" land** with room to flourish, the opposite of *Mitzrayim* (Egypt), which suggests narrowness, cramped space, and oppression. On this reading Rahab embodies the promise: someone with no reason to cheer for Israel who ends up rescuing them.

#### 2. Exodus Echoes Throughout the Narrative

Reed noticed that the word "melt" sent him back to Exodus, and from there more parallels appeared:

- **Melting hearts:** This language comes from the Song of the Sea (Exodus 15:15), where the inhabitants of Canaan "melt away."
- **Drying the Sea of Reeds:** Rahab names this event directly.
- **Hidden in plant material:** The spies hidden under flax stalks recall baby Moses hidden among the reeds.
- **Scarlet cord and blood on heads:** Both recall the blood on the doorposts at Passover and the warning to stay inside.

**Why it matters:** A Canaanite woman is the one telling Israel's rescue story back to them. The outsider knows the insiders' story, and uses it to ask for the same kind of rescue.

#### 3. Key Hebrew Words and Their Significance

| Hebrew Term | Common Translation | Insight from the Episode |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Zonah* | Prostitute / harlot | Elle sees no basis for the softer "innkeeper" reading. Rahab really is a sex worker. |
| *Shakhav* | "Lay" | This is not the ordinary word for sleeping. The sexual meaning adds comedy to where the spies go first. |
| *Charam* (Herem) | "Devoted" | Discussed in the previous episode. Applied here to Sihon ("sweeper") and Og ("baker"). |
| *Tikvah* | Cord / hope | Means both a woven cord and "hope." Elle says hope weaves two realities together, such as "in the world you will have trouble" and "I have overcome the world" (John 16:33). |
| Measuring line | Rope / cord | The same kind of word used for measuring land allocations, which turns Rahab's rescue into a small picture of how the land is distributed. |
| *Tachat* | "Under" | Often used for succession of authority. "Our souls under yours" puts Rahab in the lead role of rescuer. |
| *Chesed* | Compassion / kindness | "Do *chesed*": an active, concrete good, not just behaving nicely while still doing harm. |
| *Emet* | Truth | Something firm and strong enough to support a life. It is related to *emunah* (faithfulness), but the emphasis is on truth. |
| *Nagad* | Proclaim / tell | Related to *neged*, meaning "across from" or "corresponding to" (as in *ezer kenegdo*). It plays on the king assuming Rahab is on his side. |

#### 4. Literary Comedy and Cinematic Structure

- **The comedy:** Joshua sends two spies with the order to "walk, see the land," and they go straight to a prostitute's house.
- **The cutting:** The narrative moves between the spies, the king, and Rahab like a film edit, and the timing is sometimes unclear (did she hide them before or after the king's messengers came?).
- **Cartoon-like action:** Men stuffed under flax on a roof, soldiers running off in the wrong direction, spies lowered from a window and sent off to the hills.

#### 5. Historical and Archaeological Context of Jericho

- Jericho has one of the largest archaeological footprints in the Levant, including some of the world's earliest **monumental buildings**.
- Monumental building implies **centralized authority**: a king, a hierarchy, and the organization to build large walls and to hunt down perceived threats.
- **Casemate walls:** Double walls with rooms or apartments built between them. In a siege, residents emptied these rooms and filled them with rubble (and possibly flax or other plant material), thickening the wall from a couple of feet to as much as 12–15 feet.
- Possible reasons the flax was on the roof: craft material, insulation (Jericho gets cold), roofing, or siege preparation. Plant material doesn't survive in the archaeological record, so we can't know for sure.

#### 6. Rahab's Social Position

- The king comes to Rahab directly and *asks* her to send the men out instead of simply storming her house. This suggests some **deference or respect** on his part.
- In the ancient world, as sometimes today, sex workers were not always at the bottom of society. They could have access to elites and be present at rituals or celebrations.
- Elle reads the relationship as client-based and possibly exploitative rather than friendly. Rahab is close to the king but not loyal to him, and she deliberately sends him off in the wrong direction.
- Brent wonders whether Rahab is unusually observant (she watches the city from her window) or has inside knowledge of how searches are run, since she knows the spies need to hide for exactly three days.

#### 7. The Outsider Motif: Rahab and Jonah

Reed connects Rahab to the book of Jonah:

- In Jonah, the pagan sailors and the king of Nineveh appeal to a God who might relent and show mercy, while the prophet himself wants destruction.
- Rahab also appeals to compassion, which may be something the spies had not expected of their own God.
- **The principle:** The "wrong people" are often the ones who see something true about God.
- **Elle's application:** In polarized times, believers can ask of their opponents, "What element of God might you be keying into that I've neglected?" Doing so avoids Jonah's mistake of sulking on the hillside because God showed mercy to people we thought deserved judgment.

#### 8. Rahab and "Biblical Womanhood"

Elle is careful **not to be prescriptive**. Narrative describes rather than commands. But because Hebrews 11 holds Rahab up as a model of faith, her qualities belong at least somewhere within what "biblical" womanhood can look like. She contrasts this with narrow cultural stereotypes, from the 1950s "June Cleaver" homemaker to today's homesteading ideal, while honoring stay-at-home motherhood as a real gift. Her concern is with **mandating** one pattern, which harms single women, women without children, empty-nesters, and widows.

Observations from Rahab's story:

1. **Her past (and present) doesn't disqualify her.** Reed adds that she is not a *reformed* prostitute. She is still in that life when God uses her. This is not an endorsement of sex work, but it does challenge the idea that a person must "clean up" before they can serve God.
2. **She is financially independent**, with her own property and income, in a culture that offered women few paths to stability.
3. **She provides for her family.** She has a father, mother, brothers, and sisters, yet she is the one who negotiates for them. The king goes to her, not to her father.
4. **She has no spouse.** Being unmarried does not bar anyone from taking part in God's work.
5. **She is hospitable.** She welcomes foreigners without xenophobia, at enormous personal risk, since harboring spies is treason.
6. **She has no allegiance to king and country.** She tells an elaborate, deliberate lie to protect the spies.
7. **Her ethics allow deception.** She was not at Sinai, and her sense of integrity doesn't treat lying as wrong.
8. **She tells men what to do.** The Hebrew imperative is unusual in the Bible, and Rahab uses it again and again ("walk," "be hidden," she *made* them go up). She doesn't soften her words to be more palatable.
9. **She is confident and competent.** She comes up with the plan herself, based on her knowledge of the city, the king, and the land.
10. **She uses emotional and social intelligence.** She plays on the king's ego ("pursue quickly, you will overtake them") and appeals to the spies' sense of their own people's honor.
11. **She is persuasive.** Everyone does exactly what she says, without argument.
12. **Her faith holds even when the news is bad.** The message she has heard is essentially "you are going to die," and she still believes.

#### 9. The Four Elements of Rahab's Faith

1. **Willingness to believe the testimony.** She accepts the reports she has heard, which are reports of devastating violence.
2. **Intellectual assent.** "Adonai is Elohim in the heavens above and on the earth beneath." Faithfulness and belief go together.
3. **Trust in God's hospitality.** Unlike Lot, who only asks to escape to a small town, Rahab asks to be *included* in God's people.
4. **Courage to join a new tribe and family.** She takes on a new culture and identity, and probably lives the rest of her life known as "the Canaanite" and "the woman from Jericho."

#### 10. Rahab's World Literally Crumbles

- Later in the chapter (beyond verse 17), the spies tell Rahab to stay inside her house to be safe. Her house is **in the wall**, which is about to fall.
- Either God miraculously protects her section of the wall, or she waits among the rubble and is pulled out by her new community. Either way, her entry into God's people comes as her old world collapses.
- Reed points out that the story's scale (falling walls, a city conquered) can hide the human cost and the real courage Rahab needed.

#### 11. Rahab's Faith in the Spies

Brent observes that Rahab trusts two unnamed men with no real authority, who could easily forget her. Perhaps she knows something of Deuteronomy's call to remember the outsider, and trusts that if God is who He says He is, His people will act faithfully.

### Examples & Applications

- **Waiting until you're "good enough":** Many people believe they must sort out their lives before God will use them. Rahab, still living in the middle of her circumstances, contradicts that belief. Someone in recovery, a new believer with a complicated past, or anyone who feels unworthy can take courage from her.
- **Learning from the "wrong people":** In a polarized culture, a churchgoer might listen to a political opponent or someone from another faith tradition and ask what that person sees about justice, mercy, or compassion that they have missed.
- **Joining a faith community after hurt:** Elle names real obstacles: church trauma, busy schedules, sports, screens, and fear that a community won't match one's views exactly. Rahab joined an entirely foreign people, which makes her a model for anyone afraid to commit to a church or small group again.
- **Expanding church roles for women:** Congregations that limit women to children's ministry or hospitality could look at Rahab's leadership, strategic thinking, and direct speech as a sign of how varied women's gifts are.
- **Single and childless believers:** Rahab is unmarried, with no mention of a husband or children, and is central to God's story. That affirms the place of single adults, widows, and women without children in the church.
- **Costly hospitality:** Rahab's protection of the spies resembles the way people throughout history have sheltered refugees or the persecuted at great personal risk, which is hospitality as an act of courage.
- **Brent's gravel driveway:** His joke that the neighbors notice any delivery to his house raises a real question: why didn't Rahab's neighbors on the wall report the pile of flax? Perhaps she had built trust in her community.
- **A "female Han Solo":** Elle compares Rahab to a morally gray but lovable hero: clever, self-interested in saving her own family, and still the protagonist. That is a kind of woman rarely shown in Christian literature.

### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

1. **Rahab in Jesus' genealogy:** Read Matthew 1:5 and look at why Matthew includes four unexpected women (Tamar, Rahab, Ruth, and "the wife of Uriah").
2. **Rahab in the New Testament:** Compare Hebrews 11:31 (faith) with James 2:25 (works). What does each writer value in her story?
3. **Rabbinic midrash on Rahab:** Look into traditions that she married Joshua and became an ancestor of prophets such as Jeremiah and Huldah.
4. **The rest of Joshua 2 and Joshua 6:** Follow the scarlet cord (*tikvah*), the instruction to stay inside, and how Rahab's family is rescued when Jericho falls.
5. **Herem and the conquest:** Go back to the previous episode's question about whether the conquest reflects God's heart, using Rahab as a test case for how Canaanites could join Israel.
6. **Jericho archaeology:** Study the casemate walls, Kathleen Kenyon's and later excavations, and the debates about dating the city's destruction.
7. **The Jonah parallel:** Compare Rahab's appeal for *chesed* with the sailors' and Ninevites' hopes for God's mercy (Jonah 1, 3–4).
8. **Lying in biblical narrative:** Compare Rahab with the Hebrew midwives (Exodus 1), Michal (1 Samuel 19), and others who deceive the powerful to protect life.
9. ***Chesed* and *emet* as a pair:** Trace the phrase from Exodus 34:6 through Ruth and the Psalms.
10. **Prescriptive vs. descriptive reading:** Develop a framework for drawing guidance from biblical narratives without turning every character into a rule.

### Personalized Summary

You've probably been taught to think of Rahab as "the prostitute who hid the spies." This episode shows a much fuller character. She is shrewd, independent, hospitable at great risk, unafraid to give orders, and ready to deceive a king to protect strangers. In her mouth, Israel's own story of rescue becomes an argument for compassion toward her, a Canaanite outsider. The names, the Hebrew wordplay, and the Exodus echoes all point the same way: God's story has always been about rescue and about making "spacious" room for people no one expected. Rahab didn't clean up her life first. She believed what she heard, accepted who God is, trusted that there would be a place for her, and found the courage to join a new family while her old world fell apart around her. If you have been holding back from belonging somewhere, whether because of past hurt or plain inconvenience, Rahab's step of faith invites you to take your own.
