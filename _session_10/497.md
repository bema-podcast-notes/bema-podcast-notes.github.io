### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** 497: The Lord's Prayer, with Brad Gray and Brad Nelson  
**Hosts:** Marty Solomon and Brent Billings, with guests Brad Gray and Brad Nelson of Walking the Text  
**Focus:** The Lord's Prayer (Matthew 6:9–13), especially "Our Father in the heavens," along with the Hebrew view of the cosmos and the prayer's place in early Christian practice

This interview episode features Brad Gray and Brad Nelson, two former pastors and longtime friends. They studied at the same schools: Cornerstone University, Western Theological Seminary, and Jerusalem University College. The conversation covers their organization, Walking the Text, and their multi-format project on the Lord's Prayer: a feature film distributed by Angel Studios, the co-written book *Bringing Heaven Here*, and an upcoming seven-episode television series called *The Sacred Thread*. They describe filming in six countries during COVID and after October 7, and they share stories of God opening doors they could not have opened themselves. Their main argument is that the Lord's Prayer is "the most powerful thing Jesus ever gave us that is the most underutilized." They see it as a summary of who God is, why Jesus came, and what his followers are meant to do, and they say it should be prayed and lived out every day.

### Key Takeaways

- **The Lord's Prayer is familiar, and that makes it easy to overlook.** Because most believers know it so well, they assume they understand it and set it aside. The guests argue it is the most powerful and most underused tool Jesus gave his followers.
- **"This, then, is how you should pray" is meant literally.** Brad Gray says reading Matthew 6:9 again made him realize Jesus meant this to be prayed regularly, not just admired.
- **The prayer summarizes the whole biblical story.** It shows who God is, why Jesus came, and what humanity's purpose on earth is. It was the prayer that shaped Jesus' own daily life.
- **"Heavens" is plural.** In Greek, and in the Hebrew word *shamayim*, "heavens" is plural. That points to a layered ancient picture of the cosmos in which God rules from the highest heaven, yet the heavens reach all the way down to where people live.
- **God is above everything and close at hand.** Scripture repeatedly shows God coming near when his children cry out, as with Abel's blood, the outcry against Sodom and Gomorrah, and Hagar and Ishmael in the wilderness.
- **Praying "Our Father in the heavens" changes your perspective.** It asks you to "zoom out" from your immediate troubles and see them from God's point of view, much like the "Overview Effect" astronauts describe.
- **The early church prayed it three times a day.** The *Didache*, an early church discipleship manual from the late first or early second century AD, contains the earliest known text of the prayer outside the Bible and tells believers to pray it three times daily.
- **God's people become the answer to the prayer.** Followers of Jesus represent the Father, "crashing into" hurting people's lives the way God does.
- **Context and application need each other.** The two Brads describe themselves as complementary: one is strong on historical and cultural context, the other on story and pastoral application.

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. Walking the Text and the "Original Context" Approach

Walking the Text was founded about nine years ago to focus entirely on the original context of the Bible. Brad Gray lists the parts of that context as:

1. History
2. Geography
3. Cultural background
4. Literary design
5. Language
6. The visual settings of the places themselves

The organization started on a "four-legged stool": study trips, a teaching series (200+ short, TED-style video teachings with discussion questions), conferences, and speaking engagements. It is crowdfunded and gives its content away for free.

#### 2. The Lord's Prayer Content Ecosystem

#### 3. "Our Father in the Heavens": The Three-Tiered Cosmos

Brad Nelson's biggest surprise from the project was the plural word "heavens."

- **Hebrew:** *Shamayim* ("heavens") is always plural in the Hebrew Scriptures.
- **Greek:** Matthew's version of the prayer uses the plural as well, which is often lost in the traditional "who art in heaven."

**The ancient Israelite picture of the cosmos:**

1. **The earth:** where people stand.
2. **The atmosphere:** the air around us, which was also considered "the heavens."
3. **The firmament:** a dome-like structure with the sun, moon, and stars fixed to it, holding back the "waters above."
4. **The heaven of heavens:** far above everything, the throne room where God lives.

**Why it matters:** This picture holds two truths together:

- **Transcendence:** God reigns above everything, sees everything, and is fully in control.
- **Immanence:** The "heavens" reach all the way down to where we are, so God can come near. Scripture shows God hearing his children's cries:
  - Abel's blood crying out from the ground (Genesis 4)
  - The outcry against Sodom and Gomorrah (Genesis 18–19)
  - Hagar and Ishmael crying in the wilderness (Genesis 16, 21)

#### 4. Perspective Through Prayer

Nelson points out that people in crisis tend to become "hyper-focused on what's wrong." Praying "Our Father in the heavens" lifts our attention to God's view of things.

- **The Overview Effect:** Author Frank White studied how astronauts who saw Earth from space went through a deep shift in perspective, often a spiritual one.
- **The Book of Revelation:** In the judgment section (roughly chapters 6–19), John repeatedly interrupts the narrative to take readers up to the heavenly throne room. In effect he is saying: *when things are hard down here, look at them from up there.* That heavenly view makes it possible to keep responding faithfully.

#### 5. The Prayer as Jesus' Blueprint

Brad Gray's main claim is that the Lord's Prayer is "the greatest distillation in the entire Bible" of:

1. **Who God is:** a loving Father who reigns in the heavens.
2. **Why Jesus came:** to bring the kingdom, so that God's will is done on earth as it is in heaven.
3. **What our purpose is:** to take part in the kingdom advancing "on earth as it is in heaven."

The prayer was not just something Jesus taught. It "animated his daily life." When he gave it to his disciples, he was saying, in effect, *this is what the story is about, what I am about, and what you are to be about.*

#### 6. The *Didache* and Praying Three Times a Day

- The *Didache* ("Teaching of the Twelve Apostles") is an early Christian discipleship manual, usually dated to the late first or early second century AD.
- It contains the earliest known copy of the Lord's Prayer outside the New Testament.
- It tells believers to pray the prayer **three times a day**, which echoes the Jewish rhythm of morning, afternoon, and evening prayer.
- For that community, everything centered on the Lord's Prayer.

#### 7. Connection to the Kaddish

Marty mentions a chapter in which Nelson connects the Lord's Prayer historically to the **Kaddish**, an ancient Jewish prayer that sanctifies God's name and asks for his kingdom to come. A form of it is recited as the Mourner's Kaddish. Marty read the chapter shortly after his father died and found it especially moving. The parallels, such as "hallowed be your name" and "your kingdom come," suggest Jesus was drawing on familiar Jewish prayer traditions.

#### 8. Living Out the Prayer: Becoming the Father's Hands

Nelson's wife kept asking "so what?" while he was writing, and one question stood out: *"How can this prayer help me pray for our daughter?"* Later she was moved by chapter one's picture of God as a loving Father who comes "crashing in, knocking things over," when he hears his children cry. She realized: *"I am one of the ways God does that in the world."* The community of Jesus represents the Father to people who are hurting.

#### 9. Collaborative Authorship: Context Meets Application

| Brad Gray | Brad Nelson |
|---|---|
| "A walking, talking Bible encyclopedia" (roughly 400 BC–AD 200) | Storyteller, poet, pastor |
| Strong on context; works harder on application | Strong on application and stories; works harder on context |
| Host and executive producer of the film and series | Co-writer of the film and series; ended up writing the larger share of the book |

At first the book was planned as "Brad Gray *with* Brad Nelson," with one long chapter per phrase of the prayer. Their editor suggested a better approach: each author writes his own chapters in his own voice, after the two work out the main contextual insights and stories together. The process strained their friendship, but it gave them a working model for future projects. Marty says he had the same experience writing a book with Reed.

### Examples & Applications

- **Texting a picture of Earth:** When his wife is having a hard day, Nelson texts her a photo of Earth from space as a reminder to "zoom out," remember that new creation is coming, and remember that she is called to bear witness to it now.
- **Praying the prayer line by line for children:** Nelson started applying each line of the prayer to situations in his daughter's life. It changed how he and his wife bring their children before the Father and how he prays with his youngest child.
- **Responding to someone who is hurting:** After getting a text from someone who was hurting and skipping an event, Nelson's wife saw herself as "a representative of the Father," called to show up the way God does.
- **A daily rhythm:** Following the *Didache*, a believer could pray the Lord's Prayer morning, midday, and evening as a regular anchor for the day.
- **Mentoring and hermeneutics:** Marty recalls Nelson showing him a rabbi's assignment to write as many questions as possible about a single biblical story. Nelson had written a page and a half; the rabbi's own list ran many pages. Marty says this planted early seeds of his approach to reading the Bible. It's a practical model for slow, question-driven study.
- **Walking away from the prayer, and coming back:** Brent describes setting the Lord's Prayer aside after leaving the Catholic Church. Walking the Text's teaching series on the prayer (around 2020) helped him take it up again, which shows how studying context can restore a neglected practice.
- **Filming as an act of faith:** During the April 2024 Iranian drone and missile attack on Israel, the team prayed and sensed God saying, "I didn't fly you halfway around the world to fly you back after two days." They stayed, and found empty sites and permits granted on the spot: Capernaum, the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, three hours in Gethsemane's oldest olive grove, and a border lookout near Mount Hermon that was hit by a rocket 45 minutes after they left. Their Jewish guides asked what was going on, and the team answered, "Jesus is real. He wants a story told."
- **Bringing the land to people who can't travel:** Almost none of the world's professing Christians will ever visit the biblical lands. High-quality cinematography gives them some of that experience and can show even past travelers things they missed.
- **Reaching people through streaming:** Americans streamed an estimated 15 million years of content in 2020. Cinema-quality biblical content can reach "very choosy audiences" where they already spend their attention.

### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

1. **The ancient Near Eastern cosmos:** Study *shamayim*, the firmament (*raqia*), and the "waters above" in Genesis 1, Psalm 148:4, and 1 Kings 8:27 ("heaven, even the highest heaven").
2. **The Kaddish and the Lord's Prayer:** Compare the two prayers phrase by phrase, and look at other Jewish liturgy such as the Amidah (Eighteen Benedictions).
3. **The *Didache*:** Read the whole text (especially chapter 8) and compare its version of the prayer with Matthew 6 and Luke 11.
4. **Matthew's and Luke's versions:** Look at why the two Gospels record different forms of the prayer and what each setting adds.
5. **The throne-room interludes in Revelation:** Trace how John moves between earthly judgment scenes and heavenly worship scenes (Revelation 4–5, 7, 11, 14, 15, 19).
6. **God hearing cries:** Follow the theme of the "outcry" (*tsa'aqah* / *ze'aqah*) from Abel through Exodus 2:23–25 and the prophets.
7. **The Overview Effect:** Read Frank White's work and astronaut accounts, and think about how they relate to the biblical idea of seeing from God's perspective.
8. **"On earth as it is in heaven":** Explore kingdom theology and how the prayer frames the Christian mission.
9. **Fixed-hour prayer:** Look at the Jewish three-times-daily pattern (Psalm 55:17; Daniel 6:10) and how it carried into Christian practice.
10. **Further resources:** *Bringing Heaven Here*, the Lord's Prayer film, *The Sacred Thread*, Walking the Text's teaching series, and Brad Gray's earlier book *Make Your Mark* (recommended back in BEMA Session 2).

### Personalized Summary

This episode asks you to stop treating the Lord's Prayer as a familiar recitation and start using it as Jesus' blueprint for daily life. Brad Gray and Brad Nelson show that even the opening words, "Our Father in the heavens," hold a whole picture of the world: a God enthroned above everything whose presence still reaches down to the ground where his children cry out. Praying those words is a daily chance to step back from whatever feels overwhelming and see it from the throne room, the same move John makes in Revelation and the same shift astronauts describe when they see Earth from space. The early church prayed this prayer three times a day and organized its life around it. The invitation goes beyond praying it, though. When you hear someone hurting, you can be the way the Father comes to them. Watch the film, read *Bringing Heaven Here*, and try praying the prayer line by line over the real situations in your own life.
