### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** 509: Three Ways to Wrestle  
**Hosts:** Brent Billings with Elle Grover Fricks  
**Focus:** Wrestling as the final "pillar" of BEMA. The transfiguration (Matthew 17, Mark 9, Luke 9), Jacob wrestling at the Jabbok (Genesis 32), Moses at the burning bush (Exodus 3), Paul on the Damascus road (Acts 9), the "descended into hell" line in the Apostles' Creed, and "daily bread" (Matthew 6:11; John 6:35; Lamentations 3:22–23)

In this episode, Elle Grover Fricks covers the last pillar in BEMA's series on its core values: **wrestling**. She offers three ways to practice it, each one building on the last. First, wrestle with the **text** by approaching a passage through many lenses. Her example is eight readings of the transfiguration. Second, wrestle with your **community and tradition** by turning frustration into curious, patient study instead of cynicism. Third, wrestle with **God himself** through daily spiritual practice and by paying attention to what the Spirit keeps bringing up. Across all three, Elle and Brent argue that wrestling done well builds humility, a learner's posture, and belonging. It also brings blessing, as it did for Jacob, who refused to let go until he was blessed.

### Key Takeaways

- **Wrestling is the last BEMA pillar.** Like Jacob at the Jabbok, the person who wrestles comes away blessed.
- **Every text has many facets.** Jewish tradition speaks of the "70 faces" of Torah. A passage like the transfiguration can yield meaning through many legitimate lenses, not just one "right" teaching.
- **Many lenses produce humility.** Practicing multiple readings keeps us from getting bored or arrogant when a pastor offers a traditional reading. Different hermeneutics produce different fruit, and a traditional reading is not a "wrong" one.
- **Repetition is a faithful practice.** Coming back to the same text, prayer, or route day after day creates space to notice something new, like spotting a tree on your usual drive to the grocery store.
- **Wrestling with community doesn't mean fighting.** It means digging into the history and complexity behind practices that bother you, such as creeds, liturgy, communion, or hymns.
- **Cynicism is the main alternative to wrestling.** When we disengage, we can end up alienated either from ourselves (saying words we don't believe) or from our community (sitting out), and bitterness fills the gap.
- **Anger often comes from not seeing the complexity.** Studying reception history and the background of a creed usually shows that a grievance applies to a small slice of the global church, not "all of Christianity."
- **Wrestling with community is really seeking God.** Paul thought he was fighting a sect, but Jesus asked, "Why are you persecuting *me*?"
- **Wrestling with God is daily.** Jesus is our *daily* bread, and God's mercies are new *every morning*. Weekly church, the latest book, or a podcast cannot replace an ongoing encounter.
- **God brings things up for us to work through.** These can be reconciliation, integrity, parenting, or patience. Filling every moment with content can drown out his voice.
- **Wrestling belongs in community.** Other people's perspectives, even on something as ordinary as how they buy bread, open new angles on Scripture.

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. Wrestling with the Text: Many Facets of the Gemstone

Jewish tradition describes Torah as a gem with 70 facets, so each passage can be turned and seen in new light. That matters because most of us will spend our lives reading the same set of stories. Churches that follow a **lectionary** cycle back through the same readings every few years, and some, like Transfiguration Sunday, come every year. Without a habit of wrestling, a familiar story turns into "the one lesson I grew up with," and repetition becomes boring.

Elle shows the practice with eight readings of the transfiguration, which she nicknames the "T-Fig":

1. **Jesus as fulfillment of the Law and the Prophets.** Moses stands for the Law and Elijah for the Prophets. Jesus is the fulfillment of both.
2. **A Trinitarian reading.** This is the common popular reading: the Father's voice declares Jesus the beloved Son. Elle notes it is valid but leaves questions open. Why is he glowing? Why are his clothes like lightning? Why the specific count of days?
3. **Jesus as the bridge between death and life.** Moses died without entering the Promised Land. Elijah never died and was taken up in a fiery chariot. Jesus connects the two.
4. **Jesus as access to the Holy of Holies.** Moses' face glowed after he met God face to face, and he wore a veil because the people were afraid (Exodus 34). Jesus *is* God face to face and needs no veil.
5. **Jesus as atonement (Yom Kippur).** The high priest wore white linen on the Day of Atonement. Jesus glows in public, a picture of the torn veil.
6. **Jesus as better than the angels and as our bread** (drawing on Hebrews). In 1 Kings 19, an angel wakes the exhausted Elijah under the broom tree and says, "Get up and eat." On the mountain, Jesus wakes the collapsed disciples and says, "Get up and don't be afraid," which is the angels' usual greeting. He doesn't offer physical bread. He *is* the bread.
7. **Jesus as the new circumcision.** Luke says the event happened "about eight days after" Jesus taught about taking up the cross, and circumcision happens on the eighth day. Elle points to a tradition that in Egypt, circumcision marked the end of priestly training and readiness to enter the gods' presence. Israel's God reverses this: a named, living baby is already qualified to be with him. The disciples committed to costly discipleship, and eight days later they are "qualified" to see God's presence in Jesus.
8. **Jesus as embodied wisdom (a Greco-Roman lens).** Interpretation should check intertextuality, the Jewish conversation, and the **Greco-Roman culture** in the land (theaters in Caesarea Maritima, Beit Guvrin, Gadara, Sebaste, and the Decapolis). In Homer, Athena, goddess of wisdom, has clothes that "flash like lightning." Luke uses similar language for Jesus, and that image is hard to find in the Tanakh. In the *Odyssey*, Athena tells Telemachus to stop being childish, get up, call the assembly, confront the self-serving suitors, and travel abroad to seek and spread news of his father. That parallels Jesus raising the disciples, confronting corrupt leadership (the Sadducees benefiting from Rome), and sending them out with good news.

**Why it matters:** The point isn't to show off. Building many lenses produces a **humble, curious learner's posture**. When a pastor preaches a traditional reading, a person who has done this work can respond with compassion rather than contempt. Pastors carry meetings, hospital visits, and funerals, not just study time.

#### 2. Repetition as Faithful Practice

Brent adds that BEMA endorses doing the *same* thing over and over. Marty prays the same daily prayers. Many days nothing stands out, but over time the same words speak in new ways. Elle compares it to the same drive to the grocery store: one day you notice a beautiful tree that was always there.

#### 3. Wrestling with Community and Tradition

**The parable:** Someone leaves an unhealthy fundamentalist or non-denominational setting, deconstructs and reconstructs, and lands in a liturgical church. It starts to feel life-giving. Then they hit something in the liturgy that bothers them, such as the Apostles' Creed line "he descended into hell."

**Two dead ends:**
- They keep saying the line and feel **alienated from themselves**, as if they're being inauthentic.
- They stop saying it and feel **alienated from their community**.

Either way, **cynicism** can move in. Maybe there's no place to belong. Maybe they're better off with their books and a few "heretic friends." In the worst case, they end up criticizing the church on social media or surrounding themselves with bitter voices.

**The wrestling path:** Become an expert in the issue, even though it takes time:
1. Find the scripture behind the line. For "descended into hell," that's usually 1 Peter 3:18–20 and Ephesians 4:8–10.
2. Read commentaries and trusted authors. Use tools like intertextual.bible to trace connections to contemporary literature.
3. Study **reception history**: how different denominations have read the verse over time.
4. Learn the history of the creed itself: who wrote it, over how long, and what controversies it was answering. Every creed is a response to something.
5. Find out why other creeds, such as the Nicene, leave the line out, and what their authors said.

**What you get:** Humility, an appreciation of complexity, and a learner's posture. Anger often comes from not seeing the complexity, whether in people, institutions, or creeds. Even if a disagreement remains, you'll know it concerns a small part of the global body of Christ, not the whole church.

**The burning bush and the Damascus road:** Moses thought he was looking at a bush. He was actually meeting God, his calling, his identity, and God's promise to be with him. Walking away in cynicism is walking away from the bush. Saul thought he was fighting a community, but Jesus said, "Why are you persecuting *me*?" When we wrestle with community, we are really pursuing God, not trying to prove ourselves right.

#### 4. Wrestling with God Himself

Walking by the Spirit amounts to wrestling: seeking God's face and pursuing life in his presence. It happens on several levels:

- **Getting there.** Waking at 5:15 a.m. without feeling grateful is the first match.
- **Inside the discipline.** The Spirit doesn't always show up as soon as the Bible opens. Most mornings sound like Psalm 63: "Earnestly I seek you… I thirst for you."
- **On a bigger stage.** Elle recounts a colleague's story, shared at a CCF event, of hearing a partial word from God on a car ride, quitting his job, wrestling for three months, and hearing the rest of the sentence on a personal retreat day.
- **The uncomfortable nudges.** The Spirit may raise reconciliation with a former friend, integrity at work, how we parent, or how we spend money and time. It's easier to drown that out with sermons, audiobooks, videos, and podcasts.

**Control vs. relationship:** We can feel in control of the text (we open and close the Bible) and of community (we choose when to show up). God wants to wrestle with us at all times. Jacob's encounter came in the middle of the night, after he had moved his family across a river and while he feared an army was coming. Brent imagines the burning bush as something God may have shown Moses day after day until he finally turned aside. That's like running into the same person three times in one week: are you open to what God is doing?

**God sets the agenda:** We plan to work on kindness, and God says the lesson is patience, then sends a long delayed train ride.

#### 5. Daily Bread

Wrestling with God isn't only for big decisions like careers, marriage, or purpose. Jesus calls himself the **bread of life**, and the Lord's Prayer asks for **daily** bread. Modern preservatives, refrigerators, and bulk two-packs hide how fragile bread was in the ancient world. Homemade bread goes stale or moldy in about three days, sooner in humid places. Only artificial supports let us "live off" one encounter with Jesus for a week. Lamentations 3:22–23 says God's mercies are new **every morning**, not every Sunday or with every new book. If our practices produce no "glorious friction," it may be time to change them.

### Examples & Applications

- **Lectionary repetition:** Before the next Transfiguration Sunday or Christmas Eve, list five or ten possible lenses on the passage. Look for intertextual echoes, the Jewish conversation, and the Greco-Roman background.
- **Listening to a traditional sermon:** Instead of thinking "they're missing the Shammai–Hillel debate," ask what fruit the traditional reading produces, and remember how much else a pastor carries.
- **Eight minutes a day:** Use a short commute to study one thing that bugs you, such as a line in the creed, how your church does communion, or the history of a hymn and its writer.
- **Joining a liturgical church:** If a creedal line bothers you, study its sources and reception history before you decide whether to say it, sit it out, or leave.
- **Recurring coincidences:** If you keep running into the same person, ask whether God is opening a door, whether that's something for you to do or something they need to tell you.
- **Daily rhythms:** In parts of Europe, many people shop for food each day. Talking with someone who lives that way can deepen how you pray "give us this day our daily bread."
- **Refreshing a stale practice:** If spiritual disciplines feel dry, try a new practice (writing out Scripture, praying a written blessing, journaling) that leads to a real encounter.
- **Wrestling together:** Study texts in discipleship relationships so other people's perspectives can add to yours.

### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

- **The "70 faces of Torah"** and rabbinic interpretation. Browse how Sefaria and the Talmud preserve many opinions side by side.
- **The transfiguration across the Synoptics:** Why do Matthew and Mark say "six days" while Luke says "about eight days"? What does each gospel emphasize?
- **The Greco-Roman background of the Gospels:** Homer's *Odyssey* (especially Books 1–2), Athena imagery, and Hellenistic theaters in first-century Judea and the Decapolis.
- **Circumcision in the ancient Near East:** Egyptian priestly practice compared with the covenant sign in Genesis 17.
- **"He descended into hell":** 1 Peter 3:18–20, Ephesians 4:8–10, the *Harrowing of Hell* tradition, and why the Nicene Creed omits the line.
- **Reception history** as a method: how readings of a text change across centuries and traditions.
- **Genesis 32:** What does Jacob's wrestling and renaming as "Israel" (one who struggles with God) mean for spiritual formation?
- **Lectionary traditions:** The Revised Common Lectionary and how annual feasts shape what congregations read.
- **Daily bread in the ancient world:** Ancient food economies and the Greek word *epiousios* in the Lord's Prayer.
- **Spiritual disciplines and "dry seasons":** Psalm 42, Psalm 63, and writers on spiritual dryness.

### Personalized Summary

This episode closes BEMA's pillars by presenting wrestling as a way of life, not a single event. Elle gives three related arenas for it. With the **text**, we keep turning the gem: the transfiguration alone yields readings about fulfillment, atonement, access to God's presence, angels and bread, circumcision, and embodied wisdom, and that variety keeps us humble toward people who read differently. With our **community and tradition**, we choose curiosity over cynicism and dig into the history of a creed, a liturgy, or a hymn until we see the complexity, often finding God and belonging there, as Moses did when he turned aside to the bush. With **God himself**, we wrestle every day, because his mercies are new every morning and Jesus is daily bread that goes stale if we try to live off last week's encounter. The episode ends with a blessing: that we grip the word, our community, and the Spirit and don't let go, trusting that God already has a grip on us and wants to bless us.
