### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** 508: Wrestling (The Four Pillars, Part 4)  
**Hosts:** Marty Solomon and Brent Billings  
**Focus:** The fourth BEMA pillar, wrestling; Exodus 3:1–6 (the burning bush); Acts 10 (Peter and Cornelius); Acts 15 (the Jerusalem Council)

This episode finishes the series on the four pillars of the BEMA experiment: text, community, discipleship, and wrestling. Marty starts from the claim that Western Christianity is rooted in Greek philosophy and has become fixated on certainty. He says that this fixation often leads believers to trust their theological systems more than God. He uses the restoration of Dunkeld Cathedral in Scotland as a picture of healthy "deconstruction for the sake of preservation." Brent adds a counterpoint from the rebuilding of Notre-Dame, where restoration meant returning to older traditions. The hosts also discuss Leonardo da Vinci's cave, the difference between puzzles and mystery, and hypocognition. They then read two passages. Moses at the burning bush shows curiosity, attention, and wonder. Peter's vision in Acts 10 shows how scripture, tradition, and experience can collide. Marty ends with the "critique, retrieval, construction" method from constructive theology and with how the Holy Spirit and prayer run through all four pillars.

### Key Takeaways

- **Wrestling is the "universal pillar."** It can happen anywhere, at any time, in any setting. Marty sees it as one of the most important things the BEMA experiment offers, because it treats questions as legitimate.
- **Western Christianity's obsession with certainty can cut the legs out from under good theology.** Creeds, orthodoxy, and systematic theology have real value. The trouble starts when certainty becomes the goal and questions get discouraged or punished.
- **Theology should not replace God.** Our confidence often rests on a system of beliefs instead of on the living, personal God.
- **Theology is meaning-making.** It connects revelation to faithfulness, and orthodoxy (right belief) to orthopraxy (right practice). The aim is a life lived faithfully, not a test passed after death.
- **"We'll never find a God that's any bigger than our theology"** if we refuse to stay open to wonder and mystery.
- **Deconstruction can serve preservation.** Like Dunkeld Cathedral, faith sometimes needs scaffolding and careful dismantling so it can be saved.
- **Wrestling can also mean going back.** The Notre-Dame example shows that sometimes the answer is to recover traditions and practices we had dropped.
- **Puzzles have solutions. Mystery opens more doors.** Western culture likes puzzle-and-solution thinking. Much of God is mystery, where walking through one door shows you four more.
- **Moses models curiosity, attention, and wonder.** He noticed that the bush was not being consumed, and that small detail opened a holy encounter.
- **Peter faced a collision of scripture, tradition, and experience,** and he followed God rather than his belief system.
- **Critique, retrieval, construction** is a constructive (not deconstructive) way to wrestle faithfully.
- **The Holy Spirit and prayer run through all four pillars.** They are not missing pillars.

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. The Four Pillars in Review

The series has covered BEMA's four commitments:

1. **Text:** keeping the scriptures central as one of the main ways the Spirit works.
2. **Community:** never doing faith alone.
3. **Discipleship:** shaping and developing particular people God calls. In BEMA's framing, this is not necessarily a call for every believer.
4. **Wrestling:** asking, "What if everything we've set up needs to be reexamined?"

Marty stresses that the fourth pillar is "not a throwaway." Over years of meeting listeners, he has seen that permission to ask questions is one of the most significant things BEMA gives people.

#### 2. Western Christianity and the "Sin of Certainty"

- **Historical roots:** From roughly the second and third centuries on, Gentile Western Christianity has rested heavily on philosophy. That produced creeds, belief statements, systematic theology, and apologetics.
- **Not a knock on orthodoxy:** Marty affirms the creeds and the importance of knowing why early Christians cared about certain debates. He calls Western Christianity the "left hand" that contributes to "the larger song of God's people."
- **The danger:** Without a tie to its Jewish roots, this tradition drifts toward an addiction to certainty, proofs, complete explanations, and analysis. Marty cites Pete Enns's book *The Sin of Certainty*.
- **The core problem:** We say our faith is in God, but under pressure it often turns out to rest in a system rather than a person.

#### 3. Theology as Meaning-Making

Marty borrows the phrase "meaning-making," which he first met in the work of Karen Swallow Prior, author of *The Evangelical Imagination*. He later learned the phrase is older and used more widely.

- Theology **bridges divine revelation and faithfulness.**
- Theology **bridges orthodoxy (right belief) and orthopraxy (right practice).**
- "Practice" here means a whole life of faithfulness, not only spiritual disciplines.
- **"It's not a test that we're going to pass. It's a life that we're going to live."**

Theology is not the problem. *Bad* theology is the problem, and so is an obsession with certainty that shuts down critical thinking, questions, and openness to mystery. Marty's illustration is the recent Artemis II mission: humanity has "been out there before," yet one more step farther still brings a whole new perspective on how much we don't know.

#### 4. The Dunkeld Cathedral Metaphor

- Dunkeld Cathedral, about 800 years old, was once the seat of Christianity in Scotland. That role later moved to St Giles' in Edinburgh, John Knox's church.
- The stone building was reinforced with iron. Over centuries the iron has aged and expanded, and it is **pushing the building apart.**
- To save the cathedral, restorers have to take it apart stone by stone, remove parts that once helped but now cause harm, add new supports, and rebuild.
- **The application:** Some parts of our faith were helpful centuries ago. They were not bad, but the world has changed. Wrestling puts scaffolding around faith so we can keep what needs keeping and replace or reuse what doesn't. The goal is a faith that stays "vibrant" and standing as "a beautiful cathedral of faithfulness."

#### 5. The Notre-Dame Counterpoint (Brent)

After the 2019 fire, Notre-Dame was restored with wood from the original sources and with traditional building methods. Sometimes a crisis of faith shows us that we need to **return** to traditions such as liturgy and older church practices that modern Christianity dropped too quickly. Wrestling asks both questions: *What should we return to?* and *What should we rebuild differently?* Because it holds both, wrestling **always involves tension** and is rarely fully resolved.

#### 6. Da Vinci's Cave: Fear and Desire

Marty quotes Leonardo da Vinci standing at the mouth of a dark cavern, caught between **fear** of what might be inside and **desire** to see any "marvelous thing" there. Ian Leslie, in *Curious*, uses the image to describe being human: we spend our lives "at the entrance of a cave," torn between the safety of the familiar and the pull of the new. Healthy humanity requires wrestling.

#### 7. Puzzles vs. Mystery

| Puzzles | Mystery |
|---|---|
| A problem paired with a solution | Has no final solution |
| Episodic, like a TV episode arc | Ongoing and keeps expanding |
| Common in marketing, advertising, sales | Behind and beneath everything |
| Often how theology gets packaged ("here's the human problem; we have the answer") | One door leads to four more |

Puzzles are not bad, and some problems really do need solving. Marty's question is: *Where do I settle for puzzles when the greater reward is found in mystery?*

#### 8. Hypocognition

This is another idea from Karen Swallow Prior. Hypocognition means assumptions, metaphors, and symbols so deeply built in that we no longer even have words for them. We hold them without knowing it. If hypocognition is real, it is a strong reason for curiosity, because wrestling helps us notice what we assume without seeing.

#### 9. Curiosity, Attention, and Wonder: Exodus 3

- A burning bush in the wilderness was not remarkable in itself. What caught Moses's attention, and what the text repeats, was that **the bush was not consumed.**
- The rabbis read this as revealing Moses's character. He needed:
  1. **Curiosity:** wanting to know more.
  2. **Attention:** watching long enough to notice something unusual.
  3. **Wonder:** being open to something beyond his understanding.
- Brent adds that a shepherd leaving his flock shows how compelling the sight must have been.

#### 10. Scripture, Tradition, and Experience Collide: Acts 10

- **Cornelius** is a Gentile God-fearer. He worships the God of Israel but is not circumcised or Torah-observant. God still meets him in that space.
- **Peter's vision:** A sheet full of unclean animals comes down three times with the command, "Kill and eat." Peter refuses. He is not abandoning Torah.
- **Peter's tradition:** Jews avoided table fellowship with Gentiles because they could not know whether Gentiles were ritually clean. Marty stresses that this was a **biblically based, logical tradition,** not arbitrary exclusion. Orthodox Jews today still often avoid sharing meals with Gentiles.
- **The realization:** "The Bible called pork unclean; it never called the Gentile unclean." Peter concludes, "God has shown me that I should not call *anyone* impure or unclean." The vision was about people, not food.
- Brent notes further echoes of Peter's own story. The Spirit calls him "Simon," and the vision comes three times, which recalls Peter's earlier experiences with Jesus.

#### 11. Models for Holding Scripture, Tradition, and Experience

- **Triangle:** Marty's graduate program treats the three as equal sources of divine revelation.
- **Tricycle:** This is Richard Rohr's image. One large front wheel steers, and two smaller back wheels support it. Rohr puts experience in front. Marty prefers **scripture as the front wheel,** with tradition and experience behind.
- **World, Word, Work:** This is Marty's own framing: the world we live in, the word we live by, and the work we're called to.

#### 12. Critique, Retrieval, Construction

This method comes from constructive theology, which Marty is studying in a course on "theological imagination."

1. **Critique:** Hold something up and think critically about it.
2. **Retrieval:** Go back in and recover what is right and good, as at Dunkeld.
3. **Construction:** Build something new from what was recovered.

Peter does this. He critiques his tradition without throwing out Judaism, retrieves what scripture actually says, and constructs the worldview of the gospel.

#### 13. Are There Missing Pillars?

- **The Holy Spirit** runs through all four pillars:
  - Text: the sword of the Spirit is the word of God.
  - Community: the Spirit dwells in the gathered people as God's temple.
  - Discipleship: Jesus breathes on his disciples and says, "Receive the Holy Spirit."
  - Wrestling: at the Jerusalem Council in Acts 15, "It seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us."
- **Prayer** also runs through all four:
  - Praying the text.
  - Praying in community.
  - Teaching disciples to pray, as Jesus did.
  - Wrestling with a strong commitment to prayer and discernment. Marty plans to write more on Ignatian discernment from his Jesuit graduate program.

### Examples & Applications

- **Home renovation:** An old house with failing wiring or a cracked foundation may need walls opened up, not because the house is bad but because the old systems can't carry modern loads. Questioning inherited beliefs can work the same way and make the whole structure safer.
- **Rediscovering liturgy:** Many evangelicals today are drawn to the Book of Common Prayer, the church calendar, or contemplative practices. This is the Notre-Dame approach: wrestling that leads back to older traditions.
- **The gas fireplace test:** As Marty and Brent describe, you can only tell a well-made gas fireplace from a real one by watching closely to see whether the wood burns. Spiritual attentiveness takes the same patience, like Moses noticing the bush.
- **Puzzle-shaped faith:** Evangelistic tracts often state a problem (sin) and offer a neat solution. That frame can be true and still incomplete. Practicing mystery might mean letting a hard question stay open in a small group without rushing to an answer.
- **Modern "Cornelius moments":** Believers meet people outside their tradition who clearly love God, such as someone from another denomination or culture. These encounters may push us to tell scripture's actual commands apart from traditions built around them.
- **Tracing a belief:** Take one belief you hold firmly. Ask where it came from (scripture, tradition, or experience). Critique it, retrieve what is good in it, and construct a more mature understanding.
- **Group discernment:** Like the Jerusalem Council, churches facing hard questions can wrestle together in prayer, listening for what "seems good to the Holy Spirit and to us."

### Summary

This episode argues that wrestling is not optional. It keeps us following God instead of our ideas about God. Marty's central image is Dunkeld Cathedral, where the iron that once held the walls together is now pushing them apart. Faith can work the same way: some structures that served us well can, left unexamined, start to break it apart. Wrestling puts up scaffolding. It critiques, retrieves what is good, and builds again so the cathedral of faithfulness keeps standing. Brent's Notre-Dame example adds that this work sometimes means going back to old traditions rather than replacing them, and that the tension never fully resolves. Moses at the burning bush shows the posture wrestling needs: curiosity, attention, and wonder. Peter on the rooftop in Joppa shows the process. A biblically grounded tradition met a new experience of the Spirit, and Peter chose to follow the God behind his belief system rather than the system itself. Running through all four pillars are the Holy Spirit and prayer. The episode's takeaway for your own faith is that questions are not a threat. Held in prayer and in community, they are how faith keeps growing toward a God bigger than any theology can contain.
