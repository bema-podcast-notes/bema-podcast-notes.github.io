### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** 501: The Tome and the Center of the Spiral  
**Hosts:** Brent Billings with Elle Grover Fricks  
**Focus:** The Bible as sacred text, read through spiral dynamics, with reference to Genesis 1:14, Exodus 3, Exodus 31, Numbers 22, Isaiah 49:10, Romans 8, Ephesians 4 and 6, Hebrews 1:3, Hebrews 4:12, 2 Timothy 3:16, and the Didache

This episode is part of Session 10's slower-paced "pillar" series about the text. Elle Grover Fricks openly calls it her "weirdest episode ever." Two earlier episodes in the series already cover the more conventional ways to approach the Bible. Elle uses this one to offer a provocation: that Scripture may be "bigger, different, or wilder" than modern readers usually allow. She uses a framework called spiral dynamics, which sorts worldviews and value systems by color, and argues that the Bible is "a purple book about purple things calling us to live purple lives." By that she means a book full of the mystical and the supernatural. She says every other lens can be valid and life-giving. Still, she challenges listeners, especially those from rational, Reformed, or post-Enlightenment backgrounds, to stop editing out the supernatural. She asks them to treat Scripture as a sacred "tome" that is worth more of their attention than the news feed or the algorithm. The conversation also covers faith versus certainty, Michael Heiser's *The Unseen Realm*, Brent's own experience of guidance through dreams, cessationism, and how charismatic communities test prophetic "words."

### Key Takeaways

- **Spiral dynamics is a lens, not a ladder.** The framework sorts worldviews by color (beige, purple, red, blue, orange, green, yellow, turquoise). People and communities can move between stages in either direction. Moving from one to another often feels like a personal "apocalypse," meaning a revelation.
- **Elle's central claim is that the Bible is a purple book.** From Genesis to Revelation, Scripture is full of blessings, curses, dreams, angels, demons, holy places, sacred seasons, visions, and pillars of fire and cloud.
- **Basic Christian belief is already supernatural.** Christians believe in one sovereign God who made the stars and knows each person's thoughts, a Triune God, a resurrected Messiah, and the Spirit of resurrection living in believers (Romans 8:11). None of that fits a strictly rational, post-Enlightenment worldview.
- **Christians often trim the supernatural to look respectable.** Explaining a miracle as a volcano or an eclipse can reflect a wish to be "taken seriously by the world." That world will not accept the central claim of the resurrection anyway.
- **Every lens can be life-giving.** Blue, orange, green, yellow, and turquoise readings of Scripture are all valid. Elle is not condemning any church or tradition.
- **If your Bible reading feels stale, try a different lens.** The goal is faithfulness over a lifetime. If devotions have felt fruitless for months, a new angle may help.
- **We are called both to know (*yada*) and to trust (*batach*).** Believing only what we can be certain of misses part of what God asks of us.
- **"Stick your head out the window."** Take one text that seems too strange, such as a talking donkey or prophetic dreams. Ask God about it, wrestle with it, and see whether your worldview shifts.
- **Scripture deserves more attention than the algorithm.** Social media sells the idea that you can build Eden yourself, with a project, a discount code, or enough news. Scripture offers the river of life and resilience even in suffering (Isaiah 49:10).
- **Even cessationism admits some ongoing supernatural work.** The Spirit's conviction of sin is supernatural. That raises a question: why would conviction be the *only* thing the Spirit says?

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. Spiral Dynamics: An Overview

Spiral dynamics comes from Don Beck and Christopher Cowan, building on the earlier work of psychologist Clare Graves. It describes how people and cultures organize their values. Beck applied it to work in post-apartheid South Africa with Nelson Mandela and to Israeli-Palestinian peace efforts. Elle notes that it is also used in business settings, which she does not necessarily endorse. Her own familiarity comes from graduate work on practices in early Judaism and early Christianity.

The stages alternate between an individual focus and a communal focus:

1. **Beige: survival.** The world is dangerous. Religion is about basic needs ("please let it rain"). Life is hunting, gathering, and hoping for the next meal.
2. **Purple: magic and mystery.** The world is full of spirits, the supernatural, and deities who meet humans. Mythological literature belongs here.
3. **Red: domination.** Might makes right. Power, conquest, and control matter most. The powerful deserve their power and the downtrodden deserve their lot. The focus is the individual.
4. **Blue: order and authority.** The community comes first. There is a higher power and a "capital-T Truth," and the self should be sacrificed for the group and its structure.
5. **Orange: Enlightenment rationality.** The focus returns to the individual: productivity, logic, intellect, and material reality. Religion is valued for ethical guidance and efficiency.
6. **Green: the wider circle.** The focus returns to community, now all communities. Productivity can become an addiction, and attention widens to global needs. Elle's image is the classroom poster of diverse hands joined around the earth.
7. **Yellow: personal freedom.** Elle describes this stage as the individual's pursuit of their own truth and knowledge, sometimes with suspicion of community. (Some original formulations describe yellow as "integrative" or systemic thinking. Elle's description stresses its independent, self-directed character.)
8. **Turquoise: the unity of all things.** There is no individual, only the whole: "I am part of you, and you are part of me." Her example is the Buddhist monk who sets himself on fire in protest, identifying with the forest being burned.

**Key point:** The stages need not be chronological, and people can move back and forth between them. The shifts between them are major moments in both societies and individual lives.

#### 2. Worldview Transitions in the Current Moment

Elle uses the framework to describe current trends:

- **Red to blue:** Reports describe some Gen Z men moving away from "alpha" dominance culture and toward structure, leadership, and authority. Elle connects this to the reported growth of Catholicism and Orthodox Christianity.
- **Blue to orange:** Many people who describe leaving a "cult" came from rigid blue communities. They then discovered that their own thoughts and perceptions matter, especially after seeing corrupt leadership.
- **Orange to green:** People realize, "God says I am enough. I don't have to earn my salvation through productivity."
- **Green to yellow:** A nagging question nobody in the community can answer pushes someone to set off and find answers alone. This is a common theme in deconstruction stories.

#### 3. "The Bible Is a Purple Book"

Elle's thesis rests on two observations.

**First, basic Christian belief is already radically supernatural.** At minimum, Christians believe:

- One God who is all-knowing, present everywhere, and sovereign, who made every star and every neuron
- That this God *chooses* to be good, to know each person, to love them, and to hear every prayer
- The Trinity: three and one, with the Son as "the exact radiance" of God's being (Hebrews 1:3)
- Jesus (Yeshua) worked miracles, was executed by the state, rose from the dead, and ascended
- The Spirit was poured out like fire, and "the Spirit of him who raised Jesus from the dead" lives in believers (Romans 8:11)
- Believers receive specific callings and gifts to help put the world back together (Ephesians 4)
- There is an afterlife

As Elle puts it: "That's not a rational, post-Enlightenment idea… and that's only the beginning."

**Second, the text itself is full of purple material from beginning to end.** Examples include:

- Blessings and curses, starting on the first pages
- Dreams from God throughout Scripture
- An angel blocking the road and a talking donkey (Numbers 22)
- Satan, the devil, and demons, which by Elle's count appear about 116 times. Paul says our struggle is "not against flesh and blood" (Ephesians 6:12)
- Temples, mountaintops, and sacred groves. God cares about avoiding some of these places, which would be odd if none of it mattered
- Holy and unholy places, clean and unclean things
- Sacred festivals and rites of passage: baptism, circumcision, the betrothal at Sinai
- Seasonal timekeeping. The center of the Genesis creation pattern is the celestial lights for "signs and seasons" (Genesis 1:14), and one of the first commands is to keep the new moon (Exodus 12:2)
- Mystical experiences such as visions, hearing God, seeing God, and Jacob's encounters with angels
- The burning bush that does not burn up, where it is hard to say whether God or an angel is speaking (Exodus 3)
- God striking armies, and the pillars of fire and cloud

#### 4. Lenses for Reading Scripture

Elle insists that the Bible can be read through every color of the spiral:

| Lens | How it reads Scripture |
|------|------------------------|
| **Blue** | Capital-T Truth that holds steady while the world keeps shifting |
| **Orange** | Spiritual practice makes me better at everything I do: parenting, creative work, marriage |
| **Green** | It's not all about me. I'm called to love God, love people, and mend the world |
| **Yellow** | I have free will. Belief is a choice, and I choose Scripture as my compass instead of "hurt people on TikTok" |
| **Turquoise** | I'm part of the body of Christ, a thumbprint of God and a vessel of the Most High |
| **Purple** | A sacred, living book that calls us to "live by the Spirit" (Romans 8) and not by the material alone |

Her aim is not to replace the other lenses. She wants to be "the one voice" in the pillar series arguing for the purple lens, which she believes is under-used and essential.

#### 5. The "Fantasy Archetype" Argument

Elle's message for the nerds: set the Sunday-school brain aside and look at Christian teaching with a fiction reader's eyes. Christians have:

- A *patron deity* who gives gifts
- A calling to walk differently from the rest of the world
- Gifts to be used toward specific ends
- A divine voice heard through a sacred book (a "tome") and through the world

In *Dungeons & Dragons* terms, "We are warlocks. We're clerics. We're paladins." Her "hot take" is that fantasy stories resonate because they reflect a deeper truth. Archetypes like the mystic sage in the cave (think Obi-Wan) ring true because some people really are called to hand on God's wisdom.

#### 6. Knowing (*Yada*) vs. Trusting (*Batach*)

Brent says he struggles with *The Unseen Realm* because it seems to rest on assumptions he "can't actually know." Elle answers with two Hebrew ideas:

- ***Yada*** means to know, especially relational and experiential knowing of God, not just facts. Much of the BEMA approach, such as tracing a puzzling teaching through other texts until it "all comes together," feeds this kind of knowing.
- ***Batach*** means to trust, to put confidence in someone beyond what can be proven.

Elle points out that even the central claims, that God is God and Jesus rose from the dead, are not things anyone can "know" in a strict sense. Believers *choose* to trust them. Living only on certainties misses part of what God asks for.

#### 7. Reed's "House" Metaphor

Elle paraphrases a sermon illustration from Reed (with caveats). Christians often live inside a house while possibilities pass by outside the windows. Venturing out in faith would be like entering a whole new world. Everyone, "even the wildest of charismatics," has something they consider beyond belief. The invitation is to "stick your head out the window" and wrestle with God over that thing.

#### 8. Cessationism and the Spirit's Voice

In a post-credits conversation, Elle offers a critique:

- Many Christians accept God's supernatural intervention only in extreme moments, like a hospital crisis or a car crash. They put those experiences in a "little box."
- Even strict cessationism (the view that miraculous gifts ended with the apostles) accepts the Spirit's **conviction of sin** as a normal, ongoing supernatural act.
- **Elle's question:** If the Spirit speaks throughout the Christian life to convict of sin, why would that be the *only* thing the Spirit says? Brent agrees: "It would be very strange to have the Spirit of the living God inside of us and… only this one thing."

#### 9. Guardrails for Prophetic "Words"

Brent raises a fair concern: if revelation continues, how do we stop it from going off the rails? Elle explains that charismatic communities have a "big box of rules" outsiders often don't know about:

- A "word of knowledge" must have a "yes and amen" in Scripture. A word claiming God is evil, or that God is really an angel, gets rejected.
- If something does not ring true in your own spirit, you are free to reject it. Faith should not be fragile.
- The **Didache**, a very early Christian manual on church practice, gives tests for traveling prophets. Its sharpest warning is about prophets who ask for money (Didache 11). Elle jokes that you should "stone them right off." The text itself simply calls such a person a false prophet.

### Examples & Applications

- **The morning choice.** Many people wake up and reach for the news app, social media, or sports scores before Scripture. Remembering how sacred and powerful the text is, "living and active, sharper than any two-edged sword" (Hebrews 4:12), can make spiritual disciplines more appealing.
- **The algorithm's false Eden.** Brent's feed is full of woodworking and home-improvement videos. Elle notes the hidden message: "Eden can be built by your own hands. You just need a discount code." The news promises that if you know enough, you will be prepared and certain. Brent adds another lie: that mastery happens in ten seconds when it really takes years.
- **Bezalel, the original craftsman.** Bezalel was filled with the Spirit of God to build the tabernacle (Exodus 31:1–5). Even craftsmanship ties back to Spirit-given gifting.
- **"Think how much better your marriage would be with built-ins."** Elle's joke points to a real contrast. A couple in a cave, persecuted by Rome but living in the Spirit, could have a richer marriage than the wealthiest emperor.
- **Resilience in suffering.** Isaiah 49:10 pictures God's people neither hungry nor thirsty, unharmed by the heat even in barren places. The river of life flows out of those who live in it.
- **Brent's dreams.** Brent describes two dreams on consecutive nights. In the first, God said "not this." A day later an unexpected opportunity appeared, and he turned it down. The next night a second dream showed him where to go. He is fully convinced God spoke, though dreams are rare for him.
- **Hospital and car-crash stories.** A parent hears "It's going to be okay" while a newborn struggles to breathe. A driver feels a hand on their shoulder mid-crash. Few Christians would call these experiences heretical, which shows that most people already allow for divine breakthrough in some form.
- **A practical step.** Ask God about your dreams, write them down, take them seriously, and seek wise help interpreting them.
- **Diagnosing your own lens.** Ask: "Which color did I grow up in? Which community am I in now? What was that transition like?"

### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

1. **Spiral dynamics in depth:** Read Beck and Cowan's *Spiral Dynamics* and Clare Graves's original research. Compare it with other developmental models, such as Fowler's stages of faith.
2. **Michael Heiser's *The Unseen Realm*:** Study the divine council worldview, Deuteronomy 32, Psalm 82, and the "Deuteronomy 32 worldview." Weigh the assumptions Brent worries about.
3. **Supernatural material in Tanakh and Acts:** Elle notes that sermons often skip narrative books and Acts. Read them with attention to miracles, visions, and spiritual beings.
4. ***Yada* and *batach*:** Do word studies on how Hebrew Scripture links knowing and trusting.
5. **Cessationism vs. continuationism:** Compare the main arguments on both sides and how each understands the Spirit's ongoing work.
6. **The Didache:** Read this early Christian manual, especially chapters 11–13 on testing apostles and prophets.
7. **Sacred time and space:** Explore new moons, festivals, holy places, and the "signs and seasons" of Genesis 1:14 as Elle describes the creation account's structure.
8. **Dreams in Scripture:** Trace Joseph, Daniel, Joseph of Nazareth, Pilate's wife, and Peter's vision in Acts 10.
9. **Imagination and faith:** Read C.S. Lewis and J.R.R. Tolkien on myth, "true myth," and why fantasy resonates with deeper realities.
10. **Upcoming "wrestling" pillar:** Elle hints at a later episode on wrestling with God.

### Personal Summary

This episode is an invitation to see the Bible as strange again. Elle Grover Fricks uses spiral dynamics to show that all of us read Scripture through a lens shaped by our upbringing and communities, whether that is authority, productivity, global compassion, personal freedom, or mystical unity. She affirms each of those lenses, then argues that the Bible itself is unmistakably *purple*. It is full of angels, dreams, sacred seasons, holy places, and a resurrected Messiah whose Spirit now lives in us. Christians who already believe the most outlandish claim of all, that God came back from the dead, have little reason to trim away the rest just to seem reasonable. The challenge is personal and practical. Hold *yada* and *batach*, knowing and trusting, together. "Stick your head out the window" at whatever part of Scripture feels hardest to believe. Each morning, choose the sacred tome over the algorithm. If the Spirit of the living God really lives in us, the life we are called to may be far richer, wilder, and more full of encounter with God than we have dared to expect. For the nerds, Elle adds that we really are clerics and paladins with a patron who gives us gifts.
