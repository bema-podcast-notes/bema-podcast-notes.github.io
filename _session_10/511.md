### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** BEMA 511: Trauma, Lament, and Healing (interview with Dieula Previlon)  
**Hosts:** Brent Billings and Elle Grover Fricks, with guest Dieula Previlon (founder and executive director of Elevate Her International Ministries, counselor, and ordained minister)  
**Focus:** Trauma-informed ministry, biblical lament, embodied spiritual practice, and Sankofa (remembering the past honestly); the biblical touchpoints are the Psalms, Miriam's song at the Sea of Reeds (Exodus 15), and "holy trickery" in Ruth and Esther

In this interview episode, Brent and Elle talk with trauma therapist and minister Dieula Previlon about her book on healing from trauma. Each chapter of the book combines personal story, teaching about trauma, biblical exegesis, prayer, journaling or discussion prompts, and an embodied practice. The conversation covers why the church often struggles to receive hurting people, how to recognize trauma ("too much, too fast, too soon"), and why emotional safety must come before anyone shares their story. It also explores lament as an act of faith and trust, the role of the body in spiritual and healing work, and the West African practice of Sankofa as a model for the church to reckon honestly with its history. Dieula describes how she guards herself against secondary trauma, how she trains Haitian leaders, and how ordinary pastors and church members can become trauma-informed. A closing segment addresses the revocation of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) for Haitian communities and the church's call to protect vulnerable people.

### Key Takeaways

- **The church often feels like a place for people who already "have it together."** That is why hurting people frequently see it as the last place they can be honest. Dieula wishes the church understood that traumatized people are looking for places where they can be known and not treated as "other."
- **Not every elder has wisdom.** In her seminary professor's words, "the older you get sometimes just means the older you get." Healing often starts with *naming*: saying out loud what was wrong, unloving, or harmful.
- **Being trauma-informed starts with slowing down.** When her own sons told her they had experienced generational trauma, Dieula resisted the urge to defend herself. She chose to listen, be curious, and make room for them.
- **Trauma can be described as "too much, too fast, too soon."** These are experiences that overwhelm a person's capacity to cope.
- **Safety comes before storytelling.** Pressuring people to share trauma in shallow small groups can reopen wounds with no support afterward. People must always be free to opt out.
- **Lament is an act of faith.** It trusts both God's goodness and the strength of the relationship. God can handle our anger, questions, and grief.
- **"Connect first, lesson later."** God, like a good parent, comforts before teaching.
- **Relationships are where both healing and accountability happen.** America's loneliness epidemic, and the rise of "yes people" and "yes AI," undermine the honest relationships we need to grow.
- **Don't swing from one extreme to the other.** Unhealthy accountability spaces don't mean we should give up confession and lament altogether.
- **The body matters in spiritual work.** Disconnection from the body is often a trauma response, and reconnecting with it helps heal our relationships with God and with others.
- **Sankofa means "go back and retrieve what is at risk of being lost."** The church needs honest remembering, both of its complicity in injustice and of its courageous abolitionists, so it can heal and move forward.
- **Caregivers must protect their own wellbeing.** Dieula guards her private time with beauty, nature, play, and joy.
- **Healing work isn't only for therapists.** Pastors and ordinary church members can do it if they are willing to slow down, listen, respond reflectively, and "not be a jerk" to people who are hurting.

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. Why the Church Struggles with Trauma

Dieula describes a lifelong struggle with the church. Many congregations quietly signal that healed, put-together people are the norm, so people whose lives are messy feel unwelcome. Common church responses make this worse:

- **Pat answers.** Phrases like "all things work together for good" are used too early, and they shut down honest grief.
- **Dismissive aphorisms from elders.** Advice such as "just give it to God" or "I got over it, so should you" can come from leaders who have never done their own healing work.
- **A dysregulated church.** Dieula argues the church often can't receive traumatized people because it is carrying a great deal of unprocessed trauma itself. Leaders have to learn to care for themselves before they can care well for others.

The ideal she points to is the church as a *hospital*: a place equipped to receive people "with all their messy stories."

#### 2. Naming and the Discipline of Slowing Down

Much of trauma work is **naming**: giving people permission to say what happened, that it was wrong, and that they needed more than they received. Many families never allow this.

Dieula's story about her twin sons shows what it looks like in practice:

1. Her sons told her they had experienced **generational trauma** connected to being children and grandchildren of immigrants, including shame and being silenced.
2. Dieula had experienced that same silencing as a second-generation immigrant and had unknowingly passed it on.
3. Her first instinct was defensiveness: "I'm a therapist. Have you read my book?"
4. Instead, she held the tension, stayed quiet, listened, asked questions, and created space.

She now teaches families to slow down rather than rushing to gaslight or defend themselves.

#### 3. Recognizing Trauma: "Too Much, Too Fast, Too Soon"

Brent highlights this framework from the book. Trauma happens when an experience overwhelms someone's ability to cope:

- **Too much:** the intensity or volume exceeds a person's capacity.
- **Too fast:** there is no time to process or prepare.
- **Too soon:** it happens before a person is developmentally ready, as in childhood trauma.

Many people go through traumatic events that nobody around them recognizes. Simply being able to talk about it openly is a powerful first step.

#### 4. Safety Before Storytelling

Dieula warns against church cultures that push everyone to "tell their story":

- **Wounds need support.** If a client opens a wound in a one-hour session and then goes home for six days without tools or support, they may face emotional dysregulation and triggers alone.
- **Hidden parts of a story are protective.** Painful memories often stay buried for a reason, and uncovering them takes preparation.
- **Grounding comes first.** Therapy begins by building emotional and physical safety and grounding skills.
- **Pretend connection is dangerous.** Dieula left the only evangelical small group she attended because its "make-believe connectedness" let people open wounds with no one checking on them the next day.
- **Healing requires three things:** safety, time, and a loving presence that says, "I see you, I hear you, and I'm not going anywhere."
- **Opting out is safety.** People should never be forced to share.

Elle adds that "mandatory share-and-cry circles" assume a level of community trust that hasn't been earned. Ending with a quick prayer and sending everyone home can leave people with PTSD facing more nightmares and flashbacks.

#### 5. Lament as Theological Tension and Faithful Trust

Dieula defines lament as **the ability to sit in theological tension** without dismissing the trauma, defending God, or jumping to easy answers.

- **It requires confidence that God can handle it.** After her brother's death, a friend told her it was okay to be angry "but don't take it too far." Dieula was "just getting started."
- **Lament made her faith stronger.** Her confidence in God grew *through* lament, as she brought the gap between doctrines of God's omnipresence, omnipotence, and omniscience and her actual experience into the relationship.
- **No questions are off limits.** "Why are some questions off limits? What kind of relationship is this?"
- **No apology is needed.** If you can't say the hard thing to God, you aren't truly connected to God.
- **Faith means trust and faithfulness, not just belief.** Elle frames lament as trust in God's goodness *and* in a relationship strong enough to survive our anger.

**The good-parent analogy:** Elle would never tell her grieving five-year-old to sort out his feelings alone and come back once he could say thank you. She would scoop him up right away. Dieula sums it up as **"connect first, lesson later,"** a pattern she sees in how God treats her.

Lament in a family looks different for each person. After several losses in a row, some of Dieula's relatives wanted to "be done with God," while others over-spiritualized the loss. Dieula modeled a third way: "I'm not done with you yet, but you're not getting off the hook."

#### 6. Relationships, Accountability, and the Loneliness Epidemic

Elle reads a quote from the book:

> Your engagement and relationships can help you become aware of sin in your life. It takes great humility to submit to accountability, allowing others to see you and speak into your life for your benefit. Awareness is key when you need to confront sin and relationships are vital in supporting that process.

Dieula's response covers four points:

- **Loneliness is a real epidemic.** Americans are lonely and disconnected, with no one to speak life or correction into their lives.
- **Echo chambers.** We surround ourselves with "yes people" or "yes AI," and some people take advice from AI more than from their friends.
- **People who know us.** We need people we have walked with and given permission to "call us in."
- **The middle ground.** Because some accountability spaces were unhealthy, people tend to throw away confession entirely. Some relationships must end, but not all. The skill is discerning which hard relationships should continue.

#### 7. Embodied Spirituality

Elle notes that many church communities treat people as "brains in bottles" with no bodies to stretch, dance, or get sunlight.

- **Disconnection from the body is often a trauma response.** People learn to leave their bodies to survive.
- **The church undervalues the body.** It often treats the body as less important than the mind in spiritual work.
- **Dieula's practice is noticing.** What happens in your stomach, chest, shoulders, or knees when you hear a song or read a Psalm?
- **The goal is relational.** Disconnection from the body rarely stays limited to one area. It affects connection with God, children, and friends.

**A biblical example:** After the Sea of Reeds, Miriam and the Israelites *sing and dance* (Exodus 15:20–21). Elle notes that singing stimulates the vagus nerve, which helps regulate the nervous system after trauma.

#### 8. Sankofa: Remembering Rightly

**Sankofa** comes from the Akan people of Ghana and is often pictured as a bird looking backward. It means "go back and retrieve what is at risk of being lost." Dieula has taken Sankofa journeys to the American South, to Africa, and to the Atlantic.

From the book: "Remembering helps us confront the truth of evil. Remembering cautions our souls to never get comfortable with injustice toward anyone anywhere."

Applied to the church:

- **The church's complicity.** Dieula cites a womanist scholar's Substack article (the name is garbled in the transcript as "Tracy Blackman"). It argues that the theology of apartheid "did not begin in parliament; it began in the pulpit." Likewise, the theology of American slavery began in churches before it reached Congress, and Christians called segregation God's will before governments enforced it.
- **Erasure.** The church tends to hide, excuse, or pretend away its past.
- **Moving forward needs memory.** True reckoning and healing require remembering. The truth makes us uncomfortable, but that is where real Sankofa happens.
- **Reclaim all of it.** Elle adds that remembering also recovers treasures, such as the brave abolitionists whose activism came out of their faith.

#### 9. Guarding Against Secondary Trauma

Secondary (vicarious) trauma is the emotional toll of repeatedly hearing other people's trauma. Dieula's practices:

- **Protect private time.** She doesn't keep the news on 24/7 and avoids triggering media.
- **Take daily walks in nature,** noticing beauty such as children doing three-legged races at summer camp or a young woman braiding a friend's hair.
- **Keep a slow pace,** because her sessions bring unpredictable, heavy content.
- **Make room for joy and play:** daily FaceTime with her granddaughter, dancing, and creative projects.

#### 10. Working with Haitian Leaders

- **Representation.** Haitian leaders are proud of Dieula and can "see themselves doing it through me."
- **Building on cultural strengths.** Haitians already practice **konbit** (written "combit" in the transcript), a Creole tradition of pooling resources and labor for the common good. Dieula reminds leaders of what they already have rather than importing outside models.
- **Respecting limits on resources.** She is careful not to push leaders toward projects that suit her "Haitian American mindset" of relatively plentiful resources when local leaders don't have the money, food, or partners to carry them out.
- **Joy alongside pain.** Haitian gatherings are loud, expressive, and joyful, "a love fest." The people's capacity for joy matches the generations of pain they have endured.

#### 11. Equipping Leaders: Everyone Can Do Healing Work

- **Elevate Her's six-week cohort** for pastors, leaders, and community organizers runs twice a year and covers listening, gentle communication, conflict resolution, and trauma-informed basics.
- **Dieula's core questions:** Are you willing to slow down? To truly listen? To respond reflectively? To not be a jerk to people who are hurting?
- **Trauma-informed care means** listening without defending, without gaslighting, and without rushing someone's healing, and simply being present.

#### 12. Postscript: TPS Revocation and "Holy Trickery"

After the main interview, Elle asks how to respond to the revocation of Temporary Protected Status for Haitian (and Syrian) communities. Dieula names these needs:

- **Legal help:** free legal representation and advice.
- **Material help:** food, clothing (with winter coming), and money, since employers were already telling TPS holders not to come back to work before the change took effect.

**Holy trickery:** Dieula points to biblical figures who acted shrewdly against empire to protect God's people, such as Ruth and Esther. She connects this to Dr. Martin Luther King Jr.'s distinction between just and unjust laws, and argues that evangelicals' instinct to always cooperate with authorities may need rethinking when unjust laws threaten vulnerable people.

### Examples & Applications

1. **Responding to a family member's hurt.** When someone says, "This is how I felt growing up," follow Dieula's example. Notice the urge to defend yourself, pause, and ask, "Can you tell me more about what that was like?"
2. **Redesigning small groups.** Replace mandatory "go around the circle" sharing with invitations to share or pass. Set up follow-up care, such as a call or walk the next day, for anyone who shares something painful.
3. **Practicing lament with the Psalms.** Read Psalms 13, 22, or 88 aloud, or write your own lament using the biblical pattern: complaint, request, and (eventually) trust. Don't force a resolution.
4. **Noticing the body during Scripture reading.** While reading a Psalm, pause and ask where you feel tension or ease. Write down what your body tells you alongside what your mind thinks.
5. **Celebrating after hardship.** Like Miriam, or the mentor who brought a mariachi band, mark the end of something hard by singing or dancing in your kitchen ("get some Stevie Wonder going").
6. **A congregational Sankofa.** A church could research its own history. What did it preach during slavery, Jim Crow, or the civil rights era? Who resisted injustice? Then it could hold a service of honest confession and remembrance.
7. **Curating your inputs.** Pastors, counselors, nurses, and first responders can follow Dieula's example by limiting news intake, walking outside, and scheduling joy on purpose.
8. **Supporting immigrant neighbors.** Ask local immigrant-led churches and organizations what they need: legal clinics, food drives, winter clothing, or employment help.
9. **Choosing human counsel.** Be wary of relying only on AI or "yes people" for life advice. Build a few relationships with people who have permission to tell you hard truths.

### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

- **The theology of lament:** Lamentations, the lament psalms (about a third of the Psalter), and Job. How does Israel's practice of protest prayer compare with modern Christian worship?
- **Trauma and the body:** Study polyvagal theory, the vagus nerve, and Bessel van der Kolk's *The Body Keeps the Score*, and compare them with biblical portrayals of embodied worship (dance, lifting hands, fasting, feasting).
- **Generational and immigrant trauma:** How do silencing and shame pass between generations, and what does Scripture say about generational patterns (Exodus 20:5–6; Ezekiel 18)?
- **Sankofa and biblical remembrance:** Compare Sankofa with Israel's commands to "remember" (*zakhor*), such as Deuteronomy's repeated "remember you were slaves in Egypt," and with the Passover Seder as a ritual of memory.
- **The church's role in slavery and apartheid:** Look at the history of pro-slavery theology, the Dutch Reformed Church's role in apartheid, and Christian abolitionist movements.
- **"Holy trickery" in Scripture:** Study Shiphrah and Puah (Exodus 1), Rahab (Joshua 2), Tamar (Genesis 38), Ruth at the threshing floor (Ruth 3), and Esther. When does Scripture affirm deception for the sake of protecting life?
- **King's "Letter from Birmingham Jail":** Read his argument about just and unjust laws and its theological roots in Augustine and Aquinas.
- **Konbit and communal economics:** Compare the Haitian konbit with the early church's sharing (Acts 2:44–45; 4:32–35) and with Torah provisions such as gleaning and Jubilee.
- **Secondary trauma in ministry:** Research on pastoral burnout and sabbath as a practice of resistance and restoration.

### Personalized Summary

This episode is an invitation to make the church the hospital it was meant to be. Dieula Previlon reminds us that hurting people aren't looking for quick answers. They are looking for safety, time, and someone who will say, "I see you, I hear you, and I'm not going anywhere." Her story of listening to her sons instead of defending herself shows the heart of trauma-informed care: slow down, stay curious, and don't make your own ego the priority. Lament turns out to be one of the deepest forms of trust. It assumes God is good enough, and the relationship strong enough, to hold our anger and questions. Like Miriam dancing after the sea, we're called to bring our bodies, our memories, and our communities into healing. Sankofa calls us to remember both the church's failures and its faithful witnesses so we can move toward shalom. Above all, Dieula's message is encouraging: you don't need to be a therapist to do healing work. If you are willing to listen, be present, and "not be a jerk," you can help create spaces where wounded people finally belong.
