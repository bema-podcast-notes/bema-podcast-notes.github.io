### Title & Source Summary

**Episode:** BEMA Episode 516: Aksa (Achsah)  
**Hosts:** Brent Billings with guest co-host Elle Grover Fricks (Marty Solomon absent)  
**Focus:** Joshua 15:15–19 and Judges 1:11–15 (parallel passages); the story of Aksa, daughter of Kalev (Caleb)

In this episode of the series on women in the biblical narrative, Elle Grover Fricks guides Brent Billings through the short story of Aksa. Her father Kalev offers her in marriage as a prize to whoever captures Kiryat Sefer. She is given to Otniel, then rides to her father and boldly asks for a blessing: springs of water for her land in the Negev. Elle avoids the two easy readings, "this is pure oppression" and "#girlboss." Instead she reads Aksa as a woman with some agency inside a flawed system. The episode looks at the meaning of the names, the Hebrew word for Aksa's "urging," and the contrast with Rivka's consent and blessing. Most of it is given to "Elle's Biblical Womanhood Corner," which draws six observations from Aksa's story and offers a reading of Kalev as a model of generous, non-defensive leadership.

### Key Takeaways

- **Joshua 15 and Judges 1 hold the same story.** Recording it twice may itself be a "double portion" that signals its importance.
- **The names carry meaning.** Kalev means "dog," Kiryat Sefer means "city of scrolls," Aksa means "ankle bangle," Otniel means roughly "my God is violence," and Kenaz means "snare." Aksa's name points to ornament: a woman valued for how she looks rather than as an image bearer.
- **Aksa's family are converts.** Kalev's family are Kenizzites, outsiders (possibly of Edomite descent) grafted into Israel. That may explain why Kalev set up an unusual way of finding her a husband.
- **There is no consent or blessing scene.** Rivka is asked whether she will go and is then blessed (Genesis 24). Aksa is given away without either, and her demand "Give me a blessing" calls attention to that missing step.
- **Aksa "pricks" or "incites" Otniel.** The verb suggests something sharp, like a thorn. The text records no rebuke of her for this.
- **Otniel never acts.** Aksa goes to Kalev herself and speaks in commands: "Give me a blessing… you will give me a spring of water."
- **Kalev answers generously and right away.** He gives her both the upper and the lower springs, a double portion, with no lecture about her tone and no questions about where her husband is.
- **Biblical womanhood, as this text shows it:** she fights to be heard, gives her wisdom to her husband, approaches power with confidence, skips personal attacks, says what she needs, and does not manage the feelings of the powerful.
- **The lessons apply to everyone.** Men also struggle to name their needs, and leaders of any gender can learn from Kalev.

### Main Concepts & Theories

#### 1. Reading Women's Stories Without the "Two Buttons"

Elle describes two reflexive ways people read stories about women in Scripture:

1. **The oppression reading:** "This is bad, and maybe the Bible is bad."
2. **The #girlboss reading:** "Don't worry, she's actually super empowered," even when she clearly is not (for example, Esther being taken into the king's harem).

She proposes a third approach. Read with curiosity and accept the "messy middle," where women have limited but real agency inside flawed systems. Many readers today live in that same middle space, and Aksa's story offers them company and comfort.

#### 2. The Family Tree and Possible Motives

- Kalev and Kenaz are brothers (sons of Jephunneh). Otniel is Kenaz's son, so Aksa marries her **first cousin**, and Kalev is Otniel's **uncle**.
- The Kenizzites are outsiders. Kalev may have joined Israel first, as Joshua's right-hand man, with his brother's family following later.
- **A sympathetic reading of Kalev:**
  - Because of the family's convert status, Aksa may have struggled to find a husband ("She's part of God's people, but not in my family line").
  - The rabbis say Kalev was irresponsible because a slave could have won the city. Elle thinks the real candidates were a few commanders Kalev would gladly accept.
  - The contest filters out men who don't want to marry her, since no one risks the front lines for a bride he doesn't want.
  - Aksa and Otniel may already have wanted to marry, with this as the way around some barrier.
- **The discomfort remains.** Even on this reading, Aksa is treated as a trophy. No one wants their marriage settled by "the luck of the draw."

#### 3. The Meaning of "Aksa" (Ankle Bangle)

- A bangle is a chunky bracelet or anklet. You can almost hear it jingle.
- Names about ornament first appear with **Adah and Zillah**, the wives of **Lamech** (Genesis 4). Lamech is the first to use the language of "taking" a wife, and his story comes just before the Flood, where the text mentions violence against women.
- Aksa's name places her in a culture that saw women as decoration that exists for someone else's benefit, not as image bearers with their own calling and gifts.

#### 4. Rivka as a Contrast (Genesis 24)

- Rivka's marriage also involves an uncle figure (Lavan, or Laban). Even so, she is **asked for her consent** ("Will you go with this man?").
- After she agrees, the household **blesses her** with words that echo the blessing given to Avraham. That ties her into the family's destiny.
- Aksa's "Give me a blessing!" can be read as: "We skipped steps. You never asked what I wanted, and you sent me off without a blessing."

#### 5. The Hebrew Word for "Urging"

- Translations soften it as "persuaded" or "urged." Elle links the word to a Hebrew term for a **thorn**, so it suggests pricking, provoking, or inciting. Lexicons also list "allure" or "entice."
- Church cultures often hold such strategies against women ("nagging," "complaining," "using their wiles").
- Elle's point is that these strategies often grow out of systems where women's direct words go unheard. If plain requests were answered the way Kalev answers, indirect tactics would be less needed.
- **The text does not condemn Aksa.** Scripture often adds a note of judgment ("the Lord's nose burned"), but there is none here.

#### 6. Otniel's Silence

Possible reasons he doesn't act:

- He doesn't care, or he ignores her.
- He lacks her knowledge of land management.
- He is intimidated by Kalev, the family patriarch, or feels like a newcomer without standing.
- **Brent's suggestion:** Otniel may not know the family stories (Rivka, Jacob, blessings) the way Aksa does. She knows how this people looks after one another.

Whatever the reason, Otniel "chooses not to make himself a protagonist" and drops out of the story.

#### 7. Aksa's Request and Kalev's Response

- **"What do you want?"** could be grumpy. Elle prefers a warm reading: "My daughter! I haven't seen you since the wedding. What do you need?" Aksa had to travel by donkey, so she has come some distance.
- **Imperative speech:** Aksa gives no softening preamble. She says "Give me a blessing" and "You will give me a spring of water."
- **A material request:** She doesn't ask for a "spiritually chic" blessing such as holiness or patience. She asks for water, which she needs to survive on land in the Negev desert.
- **Double portion:** She asks for one spring and receives the upper and lower springs.

#### 8. Translation and Place Names

- The NET notes say some translations treat "springs of water" and "upper/lower springs" as **place names**. Brent's generous reading is that she receives whole towns that grew up around the springs.
- Elle regrets that translators often transliterate place names (a habit possibly shaped by 19th-century scholars like Gesenius surveying actual sites). Transliteration hides meaning, like writing "Kingville" without noticing that someone was crowned king there.
- **Her example is the Red Sea (Sea of Reeds) crossing.** The Hebrew place names point to an Egyptian deity's idol and to caves in the rock walls. Israel had a feasible-looking escape into the caves, where empires historically smoked out and killed refugees. That makes the choice to walk into the sea far more dramatic.

#### 9. Restitution Law

Brent asks whether Kalev's double gift echoes Torah's restitution laws (Exodus 22; Leviticus 6):

- Stolen livestock that was slaughtered or sold: **five oxen** for an ox, **four sheep** for a sheep.
- Stolen animal found **still alive**: pay **double**.
- Fraud, cheating, or lying: return the item **plus 20%** (a fifth).
- Brent jokes that if the spring is "still flowing," you give back two.

#### 10. A Christological Note

Like Aksa, Jesus rides a donkey rather than a warhorse (into Jerusalem). As he approaches the cross he, in effect, asks for a blessing, and his double portion is salvation for both **Jews and Gentiles**. Brent adds: "If anyone is thirsty, let him come to me" (John 7:37).

#### 11. Elle's Biblical Womanhood Corner: Six Observations

1. **She fights to be heard in a system that objectifies women.**
   - Objectification is the opposite of listening. It strips away personhood and image bearing and turns a person into an object, like an idol.
   - Telling women to "smile more," or teaching that a wife's main job is to be attractive and pleasing, imports pagan "fertility goddess" theology into Christian marriage.
   - The first love of every person, male or female, is Jesus (Revelation 2:4). "Am I now seeking the approval of man, or of God?" (Galatians 1:10).
2. **She offers her wisdom, direction, and vision to her husband.**
   - She does not follow the "say it once, gently, then let it go" model.
   - Real examples: a husband volunteering to redeploy while his wife is pregnant, and a husband buying $40,000 of equipment while his wife felt it was her duty to accept it.
   - God personifies Wisdom as a woman in Proverbs. Dismissing a spouse's wisdom labels selfishness as godliness and makes the husband a "little pagan deity" in his own household.
3. **She walks up to systems of power with full confidence.**
   - Today she might be sent from friend to small-group leader to an older female mentor.
   - Instead she goes straight to the patriarch. Kalev does not ask "Where's your husband?" or "Does he know you're here?"
4. **She doesn't waste time accusing Kalev.**
   - She doesn't say "You failed to provide for me" or "You call yourself a leader of Israel?" She also doesn't flatter him.
   - Prophetic confrontation is biblical in its place, as with Natan and David (2 Samuel 12), but it isn't her task here. She simply asks for the blessing.
5. **She says what she needs.**
   - Women are often trained out of naming desires, whether by family, church, Stoic influence, or hyper-Calvinist teaching ("Be grateful you're not in hell").
   - Elle critiques the song lyric "I'm not here for blessings / You don't owe me anything." Jacob wrestled all night for a blessing.
   - Her analogy: a child who says "I love you, so I don't want the snack you made me" makes no sense. Giving is one of God's love languages.
   - Applying this to marriage: tell your spouse "I need two days of rest" instead of silently hoping God provides an "oasis."
6. **She isn't codependent or worried about how Kalev feels.**
   - She speaks in commands about the future and is never told to fix her tone.
   - Organizations often stall: "We hear you… there's a board meeting next month… maybe a quarter of a spring… be grateful." Kalev acts at once, even though taking the springs from others surely cost him.
   - How Kalev feels and how he handles the fallout are his business. Her business is to state her family's need.

#### 12. Kalev as a Model Leader

Elle closes with a blessing that includes being like Kalev:

- Find creative ways to bless people inside complex systems.
- Repent of defensiveness. He doesn't say "I would have, if it weren't for that guy."
- Have the humility to accept directives from unexpected people. A daughter speaking this way was culturally unusual.
- Serve people simply. He carries the hard conversations with others himself and doesn't ask her for affirmation.
- Be generous enough to give double portions, and set aside the fear of man when others resent that generosity.

### Examples & Applications

- **Workplace and church leadership:** When someone brings a clear, urgent need, ask whether "process" is truly required or just more comfortable. Are you waiting for gratitude or a softer tone before you act?
- **Marriage decisions:** Before a large purchase or a life-changing commitment (a career move, a deployment), seek and weigh your spouse's view as wisdom from God, not as an obstacle. Brent admits he would own a $15,000 camera lens without Maggie's "wise opposition."
- **Naming needs:** Instead of "I'm fine," try "I know this is inconvenient, but I really need a weekend to rest." Holding needs in until they explode helps no one.
- **Everyday objectification:** Notice habits such as commenting on women's looks or telling them to smile. Brent notes how culture trains these reflexes and how unlearning them takes intentionality, community, humility, and repeated tries.
- **Reading Scripture:** When you meet a hard story about a woman, set aside both the outrage and the empowerment reflex. Look at names, word choices, and parallel stories (such as Rivka) to see what the text is actually doing.
- **Men too:** The "Clint Eastwood" ideal of needing nothing hurts men as well. Stating needs plainly is wisdom for everyone.

### Potential Areas for Further Exploration

1. **The Kenizzites:** Their origins (Edomite or otherwise), how they were incorporated into Judah, and Otniel's later role as Israel's first judge (Judges 3:7–11).
2. **Name meanings across Genesis–Judges:** Trace names tied to ornament and objectification (Adah, Zillah, Aksa) and what they say about each culture.
3. **Consent and blessing in biblical marriages:** Compare Rivka (Genesis 24), Rachel and Leah (Genesis 29), Aksa, and Michal (1 Samuel 18; 2 Samuel 3, 6).
4. **The daughters of Zelophehad (Numbers 27, 36):** Another story of women stating their needs about land, discussed earlier in this series.
5. **Hebrew word study:** Study the verb for Aksa's "urging" (root *sut*) and its use elsewhere, for example in Job 2:3 and 1 Kings 21:25.
6. **Place names in translation:** How transliteration hides meaning, including the Exodus 14 locations Pi-hahiroth, Migdol, and Baal-zephon.
7. **Restitution laws:** Exodus 22:1–4, Leviticus 6:1–7, Numbers 5:5–7, and Zacchaeus' fourfold repayment (Luke 19:8).
8. **The "fear of man" in Scripture:** Proverbs 29:25, Galatians 1:10, and how it shapes both those who ask and those who answer.
9. **Wisdom as a woman in Proverbs:** Proverbs 8–9 and 31, and what it means for women's voices in the home and the church.

### Personal Summary

Aksa's story takes only a few verses, but it is surprisingly rich. She is named for a piece of jewelry, offered as a war prize, and married off without being asked or blessed. Still, she doesn't stay silent. She pushes her husband, and when he does nothing she rides to her father and tells him plainly what she needs: water for her desert land. She doesn't attack him or flatter him, and she doesn't worry about how her request will look. Kalev, for his part, doesn't get defensive or demand gratitude, and he doesn't hide behind process. He gives her twice what she asked for and handles any fallout himself.

For me, the challenge goes both ways. When I am the one with a need, I can name it honestly and trust that asking is not a lack of faith. When I am the one with power, I can listen to people I might overlook, take directives humbly, and give generously and quickly, even when it costs me. Elle's line sums it up: "May we be more like Aksa", and may we also be more like Kalev.
